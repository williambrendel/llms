# How do non-oxidizing biocides control bacteria?

## Executive Summary

Non-oxidizing biocides control bacteria through targeted chemical mechanisms like membrane disruption, enzyme inactivation, and protein crosslinking rather than oxidation. They require adequate concentration and contact time at the point of control, with dosing strategies ranging from intermittent slug feeding for rapid knockdown to continuous feeding for sustained suppression.

## Overview

A cooling tower operator notices bacterial counts climbing despite regular chlorine dosing, but adding a glutaraldehyde-based product brings the system back under control within hours. Non-oxidizing biocides succeed where oxidizers sometimes fail because they attack bacteria through completely different pathways that don't depend on oxidation potential. These chemicals can penetrate biofilms more effectively and maintain activity even when organic loads consume available oxidizers.

## Primary Control Mechanisms

Non-oxidizing biocides kill bacteria through four main chemical pathways, each targeting different cellular components.

**Cell membrane disruption** represents the most common mechanism. Cationic biocides like quaternary ammonium compounds carry positive charges that bind to negatively charged bacterial cell surfaces. This binding destabilizes the membrane structure, creating holes that allow cellular contents to leak out and ultimately kill the cell.

**Enzyme inactivation** stops bacterial metabolism at the source. These biocides react with critical enzyme functional groups, particularly sulfur-containing components, effectively poisoning the cell's ability to generate energy or reproduce. Isothiazolinones work primarily through this mechanism.

**Protein crosslinking** creates permanent bonds between cellular proteins, disabling essential functions. Dialdehydes like glutaraldehyde excel at this mechanism, forming bridges between protein molecules that render them non-functional. This broad-spectrum approach proves particularly effective against biofilm-associated organisms when sufficient contact time allows penetration.

**Energy transfer interference** disrupts the bacterial cell's power generation system. Thiocyanate and benzothiazole compounds target electron transport pathways, starving cells of the energy needed for growth and survival.

## Performance Requirements

Real-world bacterial control demands two critical conditions beyond just chemical selection.

**Adequate concentration at the point of control** accounts for the fact that water systems consume biocide through demand. Organic matter, deposits, and existing microbial mass all react with and neutralize biocide before it reaches target bacteria. Effective dosing must satisfy this background demand while maintaining sufficient residual concentration for kill.

**Adequate contact time** distinguishes non-oxidizing biocides from their oxidizing counterparts. Many non-oxidizers require hours rather than minutes to achieve intended kill rates. This extended contact requirement influences both dosing strategy and system design considerations.

## Slug Feed Versus Constant Feed Strategies

The choice between intermittent and continuous dosing creates different control profiles with distinct advantages and limitations.

**Slug feeding** applies higher concentrations for defined periods, then allows decay until the next dosing event. This approach delivers rapid knockdown of planktonic bacteria and can recover systems quickly after high bacterial counts develop. Slug feeding proves particularly effective for disrupting early biofilm formation when paired with cleaning agents or dispersants.

However, the "peaks and valleys" concentration profile allows bacterial regrowth during low-concentration periods between doses. Higher peak concentrations may also increase material compatibility risks depending on system metallurgy and corrosion inhibitor programs.

**Constant feeding** maintains steadier concentrations through continuous dosing during system operation. This approach reduces bacterial rebound by providing continuous suppression and simplifies monitoring since concentration becomes less dependent on sampling timing relative to dosing events.

The steady-state approach can increase total chemical consumption in high-demand systems where continuous dosing must satisfy ongoing biocide demand. Continuous low-to-moderate concentrations may also prove less effective against mature biofilm unless combined with dispersants and periodic higher-dose events.

## Common Non-Oxidizing Biocides and Dosing

Ten widely used non-oxidizing biocides represent the major chemical classes and mechanisms available for bacterial control.

**Glutaraldehyde** crosslinks cellular proteins through its dialdehyde structure, providing broad-spectrum kill including biofilm-associated organisms. Example label directions show weekly maintenance dosing of 10 to 25 mg/L active glutaraldehyde, often as part of glutaraldehyde plus quaternary ammonium blends.

**Isothiazolinones** inactivate enzymes by reacting with sulfur-containing cellular components. Batch treatment typically uses 50 to 120 mg/L of 1.5% product for several hours, while continuous applications dose around 10 to 20 mg/L of 1.5% product.

**2,2-dibromo-3-nitrilopropionamide** acts as a fast-acting cellular poison, reacting with cellular nucleophiles to rapidly stop cell function. Technical manuals describe slug dosing of 10 to 30 mg/L active ingredient for 30 minutes to 3 hours every several days.

**Tetrakis(hydroxymethyl)phosphonium sulfate** disrupts metabolic processes and controls biofilm-associated growth. Regulatory labels show initial treatment at 93 to 350 mg/L, maintenance at 14 to 98 mg/L, and continuous treatment at 14 to 67 mg/L depending on application.

**Bronopol** disrupts cellular metabolism through its 2-bromo-2-nitro-1,3-propanediol structure. Product directions typically specify 25 to 100 mg/L active ingredient for recirculating cooling towers.

**Methylene bis(thiocyanate)** interferes with essential cellular processes through metabolic inhibition. Example labels recommend initial dosing of 50 to 100 mg/L, followed by maintenance dosing of 25 to 50 mg/L weekly.

**Potassium dimethyldithiocarbamate** reacts with sulfur-containing enzyme sites to disrupt microbial metabolism. Cooling tower directions specify initial slug dosing of 160 to 240 mL per 10,000 L, with subsequent slugs of 80 to 240 mL per 10,000 L every 2 to 5 days.

**Quaternary ammonium compounds** function as cationic surfactants that adsorb to cell surfaces and disrupt membranes. Labels for glutaraldehyde plus quaternary ammonium blends recommend 40 to 100 mg/L product weekly, equivalent to about 15 to 37 mg/L total active.

**Polymeric quaternary ammonium compounds** bind to cell surfaces as cationic polymers, often noted for slime control effectiveness. Example labels specify initial slug dosing to achieve 16 to 20 mg/L product, with subsequent slugs of 4 to 10 mg/L every 2 to 5 days.

**2-(thiocyanomethylthio)benzothiazole** interferes with microbial metabolism and growth, commonly used as an industrial slimicide. Technical documents report typical concentrations around 40 to 75 mg/L for initial slug dosing and 20 to 50 mg/L for maintenance.

## Optimizing Non-Oxidizing Biocide Programs

Successful bacterial control requires matching biocide mechanism to system conditions and monitoring response through both chemical residuals and biological indicators. Test contact time requirements under actual system conditions rather than relying solely on laboratory data, since real systems present different organic loads and pH conditions that affect performance.

Monitor bacterial counts weekly during program optimization, then monthly once stable control is achieved. Maintain biocide residuals above minimum effective concentrations specified on product labels, typically measured 2-4 hours after dosing for slug feed programs or continuously for constant feed systems. Consider combining non-oxidizing biocides with dispersants quarterly to address biofilm accumulation that may reduce biocide penetration over time.

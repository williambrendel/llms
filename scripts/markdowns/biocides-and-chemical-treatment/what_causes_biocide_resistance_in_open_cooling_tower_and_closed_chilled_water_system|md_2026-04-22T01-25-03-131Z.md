# What causes biocide resistance in open cooling tower and closed loop chilled systems?

## Executive Summary

Most "biocide resistance" complaints in HVAC and process water systems stem from biofilm protection, chemical demand, and application errors rather than true microbial adaptation. Open cooling towers face higher resistance likelihood due to stronger selection pressure from air exposure and nutrient loads.

## Overview

A cooling tower running the same biocide program for months suddenly shows bacterial counts climbing despite proper dosing. The real culprit usually isn't microbes developing superhuman powers—it's biofilm acting like a fortress, protecting organisms from chemical contact while they multiply and repopulate after each treatment cycle.

## Understanding Biocide Resistance Mechanisms

True biocide resistance involves four primary factors that often work together to reduce treatment effectiveness. Biofilm protection ranks as the most common cause, where microorganisms survive inside extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) that shield them from biocide exposure. The CDC emphasizes sediment and biofilm as key factors enabling microbial growth while reducing control effectiveness.

Chemical demand consumes biocides before they reach target organisms, particularly problematic in systems with high organic loads. Application errors include sublethal dosing and inadequate contact time, especially during slug feeds that fail to reach or maintain target levels long enough for effective kill. Some non-oxidizing biocides also face true microbial adaptation, where repeated exposure selects for tolerant populations.

## Open Cooling Tower Systems

Cooling towers create strong selection pressure because they remain open to air, accumulate higher nutrients and solids, and develop biofilm readily. This combination makes them more susceptible to resistance issues than closed systems.

Biofilm shielding and penetration limits top the list of causes. Organisms survive inside EPS matrices and biofilm structures, then repopulate the system after biocide dosing ends. These protective layers physically block chemical contact and create microenvironments where standard concentrations prove ineffective.

Sublethal dosing and inadequate contact time frequently occur during slug feeds that don't reach target levels or maintain them long enough. High oxidant demand from organic loads consumes oxidizers before they reach microbes, forcing some towers to shift toward non-oxidizing biocides.

Wrong pH levels for halogen biocides reduce effectiveness by decreasing the more potent species. HOCl concentration decreases as pH rises, while bromine tends to perform better at higher pH than chlorine. Poor deposition control allows scale, corrosion, and sediment to increase surface area and create sheltering spots that make any biocide less effective.

Repetitive use of a single non-oxidizer selects for tolerant populations over time. Industry practice commonly recommends alternating non-oxidizing biocides to prevent this selection pressure.

## Closed-Loop Chilled Water Systems

True closed loops usually experience lower nutrient input and less biological pressure, making resistance more often program-related or condition-related rather than genetic. The overall likelihood remains lower than open systems, but specific conditions can still create problems.

Stagnant zones in dead legs, idle coils, and low-circulation areas prevent biocide from reaching all system areas. These zones become breeding grounds where biofilm develops without chemical interference. Biofilm formation in low-flow areas like strainers, coils, and dead ends uses the same protective mechanism as towers but typically stays more localized.

Makeup water events from leaks or drain-and-refill operations introduce nutrients and organisms while consuming oxidant if used. Compatibility issues arise when certain non-oxidizers decay in specific loop chemistry and temperature conditions, with performance varying by vendor formulations and application methods.

Misapplication occurs when operators treat closed loops like open systems or vice versa, failing to match contact time, retention requirements, and target organism profiles to the actual system type.

## Oxidizing Biocide Performance

Five oxidizing biocides dominate cooling water applications, each with distinct resistance patterns:

| **Oxidizing biocide (category)** | **Typical use note** | **Likelihood of practical tolerance/resistance** |
|---|---|---|
| **Chlorine (gas / hypo / chlorine-release agents)** | Widely used; effectiveness decreases at higher pH | Medium (mostly biofilm + demand + pH-driven) |
| **Bromine (generated from bromide + oxidant / bromine-release)** | Often preferred at higher pH vs chlorine | Medium (biofilm + demand-driven; "true resistance" uncommon) |
| **Chlorine dioxide** | Not strongly pH-sensitive; generated on-site | Low–Medium (more often demand/coverage issues than resistance) |
| **Ozone** | Strong oxidant; onsite generation; operational/material constraints | Low (failures usually delivery/contact, not resistance) |
| **Hydrogen peroxide ("peroxide")** | Degrades to water; performance depends on program | Medium (biofilm protection and demand issues are common) |

Chlorine and bromine face medium resistance likelihood, primarily from biofilm protection, chemical demand, and pH-driven effectiveness changes rather than true microbial resistance. Chlorine dioxide shows better pH stability but still encounters demand and coverage challenges. Ozone failures typically result from delivery or contact issues rather than resistance development. Hydrogen peroxide commonly faces biofilm protection and demand problems despite its clean breakdown products.

## Non-Oxidizing Biocide Categories

Ten non-oxidizing biocide categories see common use in cooling water systems, with varying resistance potential:

| **Non-oxidizing biocide (category)** | **Typical use note** | **Likelihood of practical tolerance/resistance** |
|---|---|---|
| **Isothiazolinone (e.g., isothiazolone/CMIT/MIT family)** | Broad-spectrum; common tower secondary | Medium–High (selection possible with repeated use) |
| **Glutaraldehyde** | Strong against many slimes; needs contact time | Medium (often defeated by heavy biofilm/poor penetration) |
| **DBNPA (dibromonitrilopropionamide)** | Fast-acting; can degrade quickly; pH-sensitive | Medium (more "loss by chemistry/contact" than true resistance) |
| **Quaternary ammonium compounds (quats)** | Surface-active; can foam; can be deactivated by anionics | High (tolerance/selection and deactivation issues are common) |
| **Organotin + quats** | Historically used; restrictions may apply | Medium (use limited; efficacy issues often program-driven) |
| **MBT (methylene bis thiocyanate)** | Effective in certain conditions; pH-sensitive | Medium (loss often tied to water chemistry constraints) |
| **Dodecylguanidine HCl (DGH/DGH-based)** | Surface-active; targets biofilm as well as planktonic | Medium (selection possible; performance program-dependent) |
| **Carbamates (carbamate biocides)** | More limited by metallurgy/constraints | Medium (practical limitations often drive "failure" reports) |
| **Terbutylazine (triazine algaecide)** | Primarily algae control; "blocks photosynthesis" | Medium (algae can rebound if conditions persist) |
| **THPS (tetrakis(hydroxymethyl)phosphonium sulfate)** | Commonly used in industrial water systems; often positioned for SRB/biofilm/MIC programs | Medium (selection possible; outcomes highly program-dependent) |

Quaternary ammonium compounds show the highest resistance likelihood due to common tolerance development and deactivation by anionic materials. Isothiazolinones rank medium-high because repeated use can select for tolerant populations. Most other non-oxidizers face medium resistance likelihood, with failures often tied to program design, water chemistry constraints, or biofilm penetration issues rather than true genetic resistance.

## Key Takeaways

Address biofilm formation through mechanical cleaning and biofilm-penetrating treatments before resistance becomes established. Monitor biocide residuals weekly in cooling towers and monthly in closed loops, maintaining oxidizer residuals above 0.5 ppm free chlorine equivalent and non-oxidizer concentrations per manufacturer specifications. Rotate between different non-oxidizing biocide families every 3-6 months to prevent selection pressure, and consider combination programs using both oxidizing and non-oxidizing biocides for enhanced effectiveness against established biofilm communities.

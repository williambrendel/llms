# What is the difference between Oxidizing and Non Oxidizing Biocides used in Cooling tower and closed loop treatments?

## Executive Summary

Oxidizing biocides kill microorganisms through rapid electron transfer that damages cell structures, while non-oxidizing biocides use specific biochemical mechanisms like membrane disruption or enzyme inhibition. Oxidizers typically maintain continuous residuals in open systems, whereas non-oxidizers deliver periodic shock doses to penetrate biofilms and target resistant organisms.

## Overview

A cooling tower running chlorine at 0.2 ppm might still develop biofilm in dead zones where non-oxidizing biocides excel. These two biocide categories don't just kill differently—they occupy completely different roles in water treatment strategy, especially when you consider that oxidizers can actually undermine closed-loop corrosion control programs.

## How They Kill

**Oxidizing biocides** kill through oxidation—electron transfer reactions that rapidly damage cell walls, enzymes, and membranes. Common oxidizing actives include chlorine (hypochlorite), bromine, chlorine dioxide, ozone, and peroxide/peracetic acid blends.

**Non-oxidizing biocides** kill via specific biochemical mechanisms that don't primarily involve oxidation. These include disrupting cell membranes, inhibiting cellular respiration, or reacting with essential proteins. Typical non-oxidizing actives include isothiazolin, glutaraldehyde, DBNPA, quaternary ammonium compounds (quats), bronopol, and THPS.

## Speed, Residual, and Application Methods

**Oxidizers** act fast and commonly maintain a measurable disinfectant residual through continuous feed or frequent pulses. Their performance depends strongly on pH, system demand, and contact time. Chlorine and bromine effectiveness, for example, varies dramatically with pH and requires careful residual control.

**Non-oxidizers** often work as periodic "slug" or "shock" doses that supplement oxidizers. They penetrate established biofilm and target organisms resistant to oxidation. Some systems feed them continuously, but most use them in combination with oxidizers—the oxidizer provides background control while the non-oxidizer delivers periodic intensive treatment.

## Material and Chemical Compatibility

**Oxidizers** can increase corrosion risk and consume other treatment chemicals, particularly oxygen scavengers and reducing agents. They can degrade certain polymers and elastomers depending on dose and materials. A classic incompatibility occurs when oxidizers react with sulfite-type oxygen scavengers, reducing both disinfectant effectiveness and oxygen removal.

**Non-oxidizers** generally pose less immediate corrosion risk than strong oxidizers, though they still require compatibility checks with metals, seals, and coatings. Some non-oxidizers are reactive and short-lived, while others carry specific occupational exposure constraints.

## Cooling Tower Applications

In **open recirculating cooling towers**, oxidizing biocides serve as the primary "workhorse" for ongoing microbial control because they act fast and allow residual monitoring for process control.

Non-oxidizing biocides supplement oxidizers to broaden the kill spectrum, disrupt biofilm, manage high contamination periods, and control organisms less sensitive to oxidation. This combination approach addresses both planktonic bacteria in the bulk water and sessile organisms in biofilms.

## Closed Loop Considerations

Closed loops operate under fundamentally different conditions—they target very low oxygen levels, stable chemistry, and minimal makeup water. Microbial issues typically involve microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) and biofilm formation in low-flow areas.

**Oxidizers often conflict with closed-loop strategy** because they oppose "reducing" corrosion-control approaches, react with oxygen scavengers and corrosion inhibitors, and can elevate corrosion risk when misapplied.

**Non-oxidizing biocides work better in closed loops** when biology must be addressed. They can be applied as controlled slugs without maintaining an oxidant residual that interferes with the system's corrosion control chemistry.

## Selection Guidelines

For continuous, measurable disinfectant control in open systems, start with an oxidizer. When you need to penetrate biofilm, broaden the kill spectrum, or rotate modes of action, add periodic non-oxidizer treatment.

In closed loops with corrosion-sensitive metallurgy and reducing chemistry, choose non-oxidizers when biocides are needed. Use oxidizers only with a very deliberate plan that accounts for their chemical interactions.

## Dosing Examples

**Oxidizing Biocides:**

- **Chlorine (sodium hypochlorite):** Continuous dosing maintains 0.1–0.3 ppm free halogen in return water. Intermittent treatment raises levels to 0.5–1.0 ppm for 2–4 hours, typically three times per week.

- **Bromine programs:** Intermittent treatment establishes about 1 ppm active residual bromine for roughly 4 hours, following label specifications.

- **Chlorine dioxide:** Continuous programs maintain 0.1–0.2 ppm residual. Intermittent treatment achieves 0.5–1.0 ppm during treatment periods.

**Non-Oxidizing Biocides:**

- **Glutaraldehyde:** Typical slug dose delivers 100 ppm active ingredient with 6 hours contact time.

- **DBNPA:** Shot feeding provides 5–30 ppm doses 2–3 days per week, depending on fouling level and pH conditions.

- **Isothiazolin blends:** Slug dosing targets 100 ppm with 4 hours contact time. Some vendor guidance specifies 60–100 ppm feed rates in recirculating water.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor cooling tower oxidizer residuals daily and supplement with non-oxidizer shock treatments weekly to monthly based on biological monitoring results. In closed loops, use ATP testing or dipslides quarterly to determine if infrequent non-oxidizer slug doses (similar ppm targets but much lower frequency) are needed, avoiding oxidizers that interfere with corrosion control chemistry.

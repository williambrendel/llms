# Which biocide works better for Legionella control in rooftop units: DBNPA or glutaraldehyde?

## Executive Summary

DBNPA outperforms glutaraldehyde for Legionella control in rooftop units because it acts fast and breaks down quickly, making it ideal for systems that cycle between wet and dry conditions. While glutaraldehyde provides longer-lasting biofilm control, DBNPA's rapid action and lower setup costs make it the superior choice for these intermittent-use systems.

## Overview

Rooftop units present a unique challenge for Legionella control—they operate with small water volumes in unpredictable wet-to-dry cycles of random lengths. DBNPA costs 10 times less to set up than glutaraldehyde feed systems, despite being more expensive per pound. This dramatic cost difference stems from DBNPA's simple hockey puck feeder design versus glutaraldehyde's complex liquid dosing requirements for makeup water.

## DBNPA: The Primary Recommendation

DBNPA (2,2-Dibromo-3-Nitrilopropionamide) emerges as the clear winner for rooftop unit applications. This fast-acting, non-oxidizing biocide quickly penetrates bacterial cell walls and disrupts metabolism through a sophisticated mechanism that goes beyond simple bromine release.

The biocide works through rapid cell penetration due to its small molecular size, then disrupts protein and enzyme function by reacting with thiol (-SH) groups in bacterial enzymes. While DBNPA contains bromine atoms, it doesn't function like traditional free bromine disinfectants. Instead, it slowly releases brominated compounds as it hydrolyzes in water, providing secondary antimicrobial effects before rapidly decomposing into less active byproducts.

**Dosing strategy:** Apply 2–10 ppm active DBNPA in the water basin using perforated feeders that activate on contact with water. Allow 30–60 minutes contact time before draining or cycling the unit. The rapid decomposition makes it safe for intermittent wet/dry cycles without residual buildup concerns.

## Glutaraldehyde: The Secondary Option

Glutaraldehyde offers a different approach as a slow-acting, non-oxidizing biocide that works by cross-linking bacterial proteins and disrupting cell function. Its strength lies in biofilm penetration, where Legionella thrives, and its stability provides longer-lasting control in water systems.

However, glutaraldehyde requires liquid application to all makeup water, raising concerns about biocide entering unintended building water systems. This limitation, combined with slower action and higher setup costs, relegates it to second choice for rooftop applications.

## Comprehensive Operating Strategy

Prevention and routine cleaning form the foundation. Flush and clean basins quarterly, removing sediment and organic matter that supports bacterial growth. 

Chemical treatment requires a dual approach: use DBNPA for quick knockdowns when restarting systems after dry periods, and apply low-dose glutaraldehyde only when testing indicates biofilm formation through BART SLYM and lab pseudomonas analysis. Consider alternating biocides to prevent bacterial resistance.

Water management best practices demand maintaining pH between 6.5–8.5 for optimal biocide effectiveness and conductivity below 2000 µS/cm to avoid excess hydraulic holding time. Monitor temperature carefully, as Legionella thrives in the 77–113°F (25–45°C) range common during summer operation. Use non-corrosive dispersants when required cleanings or disinfections demand enhanced biocide penetration through biofilms.

## Materials Compatibility Considerations

Both biocides show similar compatibility limitations with common rooftop unit materials. Neither works with aluminum fins (though minimal mist contact reduces concern), brass, copper piping, polycarbonate, or acrylic components. 

Key differences emerge with specific materials: DBNPA tolerates zinc and polystyrene while glutaraldehyde damages them. Conversely, glutaraldehyde works with PVC and neoprene while DBNPA corrodes these materials. Both biocides require stainless steel basins and corrosion monitoring through coupons placed in the basin.

## Key Takeaway

Install DBNPA hockey puck feeders in each rooftop unit for primary Legionella control, conducting quarterly basin cleanings and monthly water quality testing for pH, conductivity, and bacterial indicators. When testing reveals biofilm formation, supplement with low-dose glutaraldehyde treatment while monitoring for material compatibility issues through basin-mounted corrosion coupons.

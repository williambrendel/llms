# What is Isothiazolone and How Does it Control Microbial Growth in Cooling Towers?

## Executive Summary

Isothiazolone is a broad-spectrum biocide that kills bacteria, algae, and fungi in cooling tower systems by disrupting cellular enzymes and damaging cell membranes. It effectively controls biological fouling at concentrations of 1-15 ppm active ingredient.

## Overview

A single biofilm layer just 0.002 inches thick can reduce heat transfer efficiency by 20% in cooling towers. Isothiazolone compounds attack this problem by targeting the cellular machinery that keeps microorganisms alive. These biocides work so effectively that they can eliminate biofilm-forming bacteria like *Pseudomonas* and dangerous pathogens like *Legionella* within hours of proper dosing.

## What Isothiazolone Actually Is

"Isothiazolone" refers to a family of related compounds, primarily CMIT (5-chloro-2-methyl-4-isothiazolin-3-one) and MIT (2-methyl-4-isothiazolin-3-one). Manufacturers typically blend these compounds in specific ratios—commonly CMIT/MIT at 3:1—and market them under trade names like Kathon, Acticide, or Proxel.

These compounds function as non-oxidizing biocides, meaning they kill microorganisms through chemical reactions rather than oxidation processes. This characteristic makes them particularly valuable in cooling tower applications where oxidizing biocides might interfere with other system chemistry.

## How Isothiazolones Kill Microorganisms

Isothiazolones employ a dual-action mechanism that attacks microbial cells on multiple fronts.

**Enzyme Inhibition Through Thiol Reactivity**

The primary killing mechanism targets thiol (-SH) groups in proteins and enzymes inside microbial cells. These thiol groups are essential for enzyme function—without them, cellular metabolism stops completely. When isothiazolones react with or oxidize these groups, they inactivate critical enzymes. The cell loses its ability to produce energy or maintain its structure, leading to rapid death.

**Membrane Disruption**

Simultaneously, isothiazolones damage the cell membrane itself, making it permeable and leaky. This causes the loss of vital ions, nutrients, and cellular contents. The cell can no longer regulate its internal environment, which proves lethal even if some enzymes remain functional.

This dual action creates broad-spectrum effectiveness against gram-negative bacteria, gram-positive bacteria, fungi, and algae. The mechanism proves particularly effective against biofilm-forming bacteria like *Pseudomonas*, which typically resist single-mode biocides.

## Practical Application Parameters

| Property | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Effective Range | 1–15 ppm active / 66 to 1000 ppm as product |
| Spectrum | Broad (bacteria, fungi, algae) |
| Synergy | Often paired with oxidizing biocides (e.g., bleach, bromine) |
| Non-oxidizing | Yes |
| Degrades | Biodegradable under normal conditions |
| pH Tolerance | Stable up to ~pH 8.5; degrades faster at high pH |
| Residual Control | Short-lived—requires frequent or continuous feed |

Isothiazolones can be fed as slug doses for shock treatment or continuously for ongoing control, depending on system bioload. The choice depends on factors like water temperature, pH, and the severity of existing biological fouling.

## Critical Dosing Considerations

Isothiazolones require careful attention to system chemistry. At pH levels above 8.5, these compounds degrade rapidly and lose effectiveness. Systems operating at pH greater than 9 should consider alternative biocides like glutaraldehyde, DBNPA, or TTPC.

High concentrations of strong oxidizers can also cause premature degradation. When pairing isothiazolones with oxidizing biocides, operators must sequence the additions properly to avoid chemical conflicts.

Material compatibility becomes important at higher concentrations. Soft elastomers and certain plastics may experience degradation with prolonged exposure to concentrated isothiazolone solutions.

## Why Biological Control Matters

Uncontrolled microbial growth in cooling towers creates multiple operational problems. Biofilms act as insulation, reducing heat transfer efficiency and forcing systems to work harder. Microbiologically Influenced Corrosion (MIC) can damage expensive equipment. Biological growth clogs nozzles, strainers, and fill material, reducing system capacity. Most critically, cooling towers can harbor *Legionella* bacteria, creating serious health risks.

## Monitoring and Best Practices

Effective isothiazolone programs require regular monitoring using test kits, dip slides, or ATP meters to track biological activity. Many operators alternate isothiazolones with other biocide types—both oxidizers and different non-oxidizers—to prevent microbial resistance development.

The short-lived nature of isothiazolones means they provide limited residual protection. Systems with heavy bioloads may require continuous feed, while cleaner systems can often maintain control with periodic slug doses.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor biological activity weekly using dip slides or ATP meters, maintain system pH below 8.5 for optimal isothiazolone stability, and dose at 1-15 ppm active ingredient based on system bioload. Consider alternating with glutaraldehyde or DBNPA every 30-60 days to prevent microbial resistance.

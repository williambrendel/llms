# How Do I Know If I'm Overfeeding or Underfeeding Biocide in My Water System?

## Executive Summary

Both underfeeding and overfeeding biocide create distinct warning signs. Underfeeding shows up as rising bacteria counts, visible slime, odors, and declining system performance. Overfeeding appears as persistently high residuals, material damage, strong odors, and unnecessary costs without added microbial control benefits.

## Overview

A cooling tower that suddenly develops a musty smell and slippery surfaces might need more biocide — or the problem could be poor distribution of an already adequate dose. Meanwhile, a system with perfect bacteria results but corroding heat exchanger tubes might be getting too much chemical. The most reliable diagnosis comes from tracking multiple indicators together rather than relying on any single measurement.

## Recognizing Underfeeding Signs

Underfeeding reveals itself through loss of biological control across multiple system indicators.

**Microbiological indicators** show the clearest evidence. Bacteria test results climb over successive weeks or months. Biofilm appears on wetted surfaces as a slippery coating. Slime accumulates in basins, piping, strainers, and low-flow areas where microorganisms can establish themselves.

**Water quality changes** become noticeable to operators. The system develops a musty or septic odor. Water appears cloudy or dirty compared to normal conditions. Residual biocide disappears faster after each dose, and microorganisms return more quickly between treatments.

**System performance degrades** in measurable ways. Heat exchangers foul more rapidly, reducing heat transfer efficiency. Filters load up faster, creating higher pressure drops. Deposit-related corrosion increases, particularly in areas where biofilm provides protective environments for aggressive bacteria.

These problems typically indicate insufficient dose, inadequate feed frequency, poor chemical distribution, or overwhelming organic loads that consume the biocide before it can control target microorganisms.

## Recognizing Overfeeding Signs

Overfeeding creates chemical exposure beyond what the system needs for microbial control, often producing unwanted side effects.

**Chemical indicators** show excess activity. Residual concentrations remain well above target levels for extended periods. Strong chemical odors persist after treatment, and operators report eye, nose, or skin irritation near the system.

**System damage** becomes apparent over time. Seals, gaskets, coatings, and monitoring probes deteriorate faster than expected. Soft metals show increased corrosion rates. Water may foam or behave unusually after biocide addition.

**Economic and operational impacts** accumulate. Chemical consumption exceeds what effective control requires. Bacteria results drop to very low levels without corresponding improvements in system cleanliness or performance. Downstream wastewater treatment or discharge compliance becomes more difficult.

These symptoms suggest doses higher than necessary, excessive feed duration, overly frequent treatment cycles, or biocide chemistry too aggressive for the specific system conditions.

## Distinguishing Real Problems from Look-Alikes

Several conditions can mimic true underfeeding or overfeeding, making accurate diagnosis more challenging.

**False underfeeding signals** can result from poor chemical mixing that leaves some areas untreated while others receive adequate doses. Dead legs and low-flow zones may harbor growth regardless of overall system treatment levels. Heavy organic contamination or high suspended solids can consume biocide before it reaches target microorganisms. Inaccurate pump calibration, poor sampling locations, or delayed sample testing can create misleading results.

**False overfeeding signals** may come from wrong residual test methods or interference in field tests. Samples taken too close to injection points show artificially high concentrations. Corrosion caused by low pH or other water chemistry problems can be mistakenly attributed to excessive biocide feed. Odors from chemical breakdown products or interactions with other treatment chemicals can suggest overfeeding when the actual biocide dose remains appropriate.

## Diagnostic Approach

Reliable diagnosis requires examining multiple indicators simultaneously rather than reacting to individual measurements.

**Operational data** provides the foundation. Track actual dosage rates, measured residual concentrations, feed frequency, contact time, and pump calibration accuracy. Document system flow patterns and mixing effectiveness.

**Microbiological monitoring** shows biological response. Follow bacteria count trends over time rather than single results. Observe surface growth patterns, biofilm development, and how quickly microorganisms return after each treatment cycle.

**System performance measurements** reveal practical impacts. Monitor heat exchanger efficiency, filter loading rates, visible deposit formation, corrosion rate data, water clarity, and any materials damage.

**What it measures:** The relationship between biocide dose and microbial control effectiveness

Formula:
```
Control Effectiveness = (Baseline Count - Treated Count) / Baseline Count × 100%
```

**Example:** If bacteria counts drop from 10,000 CFU/mL to 100 CFU/mL after treatment, control effectiveness equals (10,000 - 100) / 10,000 × 100% = 99%. However, increasing the dose to achieve 99.9% control (10 CFU/mL) might provide no practical benefit while increasing costs and side effects.

## Making Adjustments

**For likely underfeeding situations** with persistent slime, rising bacteria trends, rapid residual loss, and declining performance, consider increasing dose rates, extending contact time, improving chemical distribution, or addressing competing organic loads before assuming inadequate chemistry.

**For likely overfeeding situations** with consistently high residuals, material damage, operator complaints, and rising costs without better control, reduce dose rates, shorten feed duration, lower target residual levels, or evaluate alternative chemistry better suited to system conditions.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor biocide residuals weekly alongside bacteria counts and visual system inspections. Target residual levels between 0.5-1.0 mg/L for most oxidizing biocides, adjusting based on system response rather than maintaining arbitrary high levels. When problems persist despite apparent adequate dosing, investigate distribution, contact time, and competing chemical demands before increasing feed rates.

# How do oxidizing biocides control bacteria, and what is the difference between slug feed and constant feed?

## Executive Summary

Oxidizing biocides control bacteria by chemically damaging cell membranes, proteins, and genetic material through electron-transfer reactions that stop metabolism and cause cell death. Slug feed applies high concentrations intermittently for rapid knockdown but allows regrowth between doses, while constant feed maintains steady residuals for continuous suppression with more predictable control.

## Overview

A single chlorine molecule can destroy a bacterial cell in seconds, yet cooling towers still experience microbial fouling despite regular biocide treatment. The difference often comes down to how and when the oxidant reaches the bacteria. Most facilities choose between two fundamentally different approaches: hitting microbes hard and fast with periodic shock doses, or maintaining a continuous chemical presence that never gives bacteria a chance to establish.

## Common Oxidizing Biocides

Water treatment relies on several families of oxidizing chemicals, each with distinct handling and performance characteristics.

**Chlorine-based oxidizers** dominate most applications. Chlorine gas delivers the most active ingredient per pound but requires specialized safety equipment. Sodium hypochlorite (liquid bleach) offers easier handling but degrades over time, especially in heat and sunlight. Calcium hypochlorite provides stable solid storage but can leave calcium residue in soft water systems. Chlorinated isocyanurates like sodium dichloroisocyanurate and trichloroisocyanuric acid release chlorine gradually, making them popular for smaller systems.

**Bromine-based oxidizers** work better than chlorine at higher pH levels. Most "bromine programs" actually use sodium bromide activated by an oxidizer to generate active bromine on-site. Solid bromine compounds like 1-bromo-3-chloro-5,5-dimethylhydantoin and 2,2-dibromo-3-nitrilopropionamide provide convenient tablet or granular forms.

**Chlorine dioxide** penetrates biofilms more effectively than chlorine or bromine but requires on-site generation from sodium chlorite and an acid or oxidizer. The equipment complexity limits its use to larger facilities.

**Ozone** provides the strongest oxidation potential but decomposes rapidly, requiring on-site generation and immediate use. Its short half-life means no harmful residuals but also no lasting protection.

**Peroxygen oxidizers** including hydrogen peroxide and peracetic acid work across a wide pH range and break down into harmless byproducts. Peracetic acid combinations offer both immediate kill and some residual protection.

**Monochloramine** provides longer-lasting residuals than free chlorine with less reaction with organics, making it valuable for distribution systems and large cooling circuits.

## How Oxidizing Biocides Control Bacteria

Oxidizing biocides kill bacteria through multiple simultaneous attack mechanisms that overwhelm cellular repair systems.

**Oxidation of the cell envelope** represents the first line of attack. Oxidants react with membrane lipids and proteins, disrupting the barrier that keeps the cell intact. Once membrane integrity fails, essential cellular contents leak out while harmful substances flow in, leading to rapid cell death.

**Inactivation of proteins and enzymes** shuts down bacterial metabolism. Oxidation changes protein structure and function, particularly targeting sulfur-containing amino acids and enzyme active sites. When key metabolic enzymes stop working, the cell cannot maintain basic life processes.

**Damage to genetic material** prevents bacteria from reproducing and repairing oxidative damage. Oxidants attack both DNA and RNA, breaking chemical bonds and creating structural changes that block replication and protein synthesis.

**Key performance factors** determine whether oxidation actually reaches target organisms. The delivered residual at the point of control matters more than the dose added to the system, since organic matter, reduced metals, and deposits consume oxidant before it reaches bacteria. Contact time cannot be shortchanged—higher concentration does not fully compensate for inadequate mixing or insufficient exposure time. System demand and deposition create the biggest challenge, as biofilm and accumulated solids both consume oxidant and physically shield organisms from contact.

## Slug Feed (Shock Dose) Strategy

Slug feed applies high oxidant concentrations intermittently, creating short-duration treatment events separated by periods of low or zero residual.

**Advantages** make slug feed attractive for specific situations. Rapid knockdown of planktonic bacteria works well after system upsets or during startup when high bacterial counts need immediate reduction. High concentrations can overwhelm short-term oxidant demand, achieving better penetration into early deposits than steady low-level feeding. Simple implementation appeals to facilities with limited continuous feed controls or where manual dosing fits operational patterns better.

**Disadvantages** create significant control risks. Regrowth between doses allows bacterial populations to rebound when residuals fall, potentially reaching higher levels than before treatment. Concentration peaks can increase corrosion and material stress, particularly in systems with marginal metallurgy or insufficient corrosion inhibitor programs. Uneven treatment becomes likely when poor mixing or dead zones receive inadequate oxidant contact. Less stable results make trending difficult since bacterial counts depend heavily on sampling timing relative to the last dose.

## Constant Feed (Continuous Feed) Strategy

Constant feed maintains near-continuous oxidant residuals during system operation through controlled pacing and monitoring.

**Advantages** provide more predictable microbial control. Continuous suppression of microbial growth reduces rebound potential by never allowing bacteria extended time to multiply unchecked. More consistent control and trending becomes possible when managing to a target residual range rather than trying to time intermittent doses. Most continuously operating open recirculating systems prefer this approach since it reduces time-at-risk when conditions favor rapid bacterial growth.

**Disadvantages** increase complexity and cost in demanding systems. Higher total chemical use results when system demand must be met continuously rather than intermittently. Established biofilm often resists constant low-level oxidant exposure, requiring supplemental dispersants and periodic higher-dose events for effective removal. Reliable equipment and controls become essential since feed system failures can cause prolonged underdosing with rapid loss of microbial control. Chronic exposure can increase corrosion risk if residuals run too high, requiring careful balance with corrosion inhibitor programs and regular monitoring.

## Practical Use Pattern

Most successful programs combine both approaches strategically. Constant feed provides baseline microbial suppression during normal operation, maintaining steady residuals that prevent bacterial establishment. Periodic slug events target deposits and biofilm, especially after high bacterial counts or seasonal load changes when system demand increases.

Coordination with biodispersant improves both removal and oxidant penetration. Dispersants loosen biofilm structure, allowing oxidants to reach protected bacteria while helping remove dead biomass that would otherwise consume additional biocide.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor free residual levels at least twice daily in critical systems, targeting 0.5-1.0 mg/L for continuous programs and 5-10 mg/L during slug events. Consider switching from slug-only programs if bacterial counts exceed 10,000 CFU/mL more than once monthly. Combine constant feed with weekly dispersant treatment and monthly shock doses for optimal biofilm control in high-fouling systems.

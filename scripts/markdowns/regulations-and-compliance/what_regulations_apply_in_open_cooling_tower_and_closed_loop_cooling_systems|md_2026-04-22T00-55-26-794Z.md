# What regulations apply to cooling tower operations in open tower and closed loop cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Cooling tower operations face multiple regulatory layers including environmental discharge permits, pesticide registration requirements, OSHA safety standards, public health water management programs, and waste disposal rules that vary significantly between open towers with routine discharge and closed loops with intermittent discharge.

## Overview

A single cooling tower system can trigger compliance requirements across six different regulatory frameworks simultaneously. Open towers face continuous discharge oversight while closed loops encounter the same rules during maintenance events, and both systems must navigate overlapping federal, state, and local authority structures that can create compliance gaps.

## Environmental Water Discharge Regulation

### Open Cooling Towers (Routine Discharge)

Cooling tower blowdown is a wastewater discharge that triggers different permit requirements depending on destination. NPDES permits under the Clean Water Act govern discharges to surface waters through the federal framework that states often administer. 40 CFR Part 122 establishes the NPDES permitting program structure.

When discharging to sanitary sewers, facilities must comply with pretreatment program requirements and local POTW limits covering pH, metals, residual oxidants, and other parameters. The EPA pretreatment program operates within the NPDES framework but gets implemented through local sewer authorities with site-specific limits.

Permits typically regulate parameters directly relevant to towers: pH, total residual oxidant, metals, TSS, and temperature. The specific requirements depend on the individual permit and receiving system characteristics.

### Closed Loops (Intermittent Discharge)

Closed loops normally have no routine discharge, but the same discharge rules apply during maintenance activities. This includes draining the loop for maintenance, flushing after cleaning, and disposing of glycol or cleaning wastes.

## Pesticide and Biocide Use Regulation

Most cooling tower biocides are regulated as pesticides in the US under FIFRA, which governs registration, distribution, sale, and use of pesticides. Pesticide use must follow the label requirements since the label is the law.

States can add requirements beyond federal standards. NYSDEC has specific conditions for cooling tower biocide applications and related discharge constraints under SPDES context.

Closed loops use biocides less commonly, but when facilities do dose a biocide, FIFRA label compliance still applies.

## Worker Safety Regulations (OSHA)

Both system types must comply with commonly applicable OSHA standards during operation and maintenance:

**Lockout/Tagout (hazardous energy control)** under 29 CFR 1910.147 applies to all energy isolation procedures.

**Permit-required confined spaces** under 29 CFR 1910.146 often applies to tower basins, sumps, and some pits or enclosures depending on configuration.

**Fall protection** requirements apply when working on tower decks, roofs, ladders, and platforms. The applicable standard depends on whether the work qualifies as general industry or construction activity.

**Hazard Communication** requirements under 29 CFR 1910.1200 cover SDS, labeling, and training for acids, alkalis, biocides, and inhibitors.

## Public Health and Building Water Risk Management Requirements

Many jurisdictions require water management or risk management programs for building water systems that include cooling towers, especially in healthcare and high-risk buildings. These requirements extend beyond Legionella-specific regulations.

ASHRAE Standard 188 is a consensus standard widely used as the basis for enforceable programs when jurisdictions adopt it or reference it in local rules.

Some states and cities have explicit cooling tower program requirements, with New York providing a clear example of comprehensive local tower requirements.

Closed loops are generally not regulated as aerosol devices, but they may be included in facility water management plans depending on building type and local adoption.

## Waste and Chemical Disposal Regulations

RCRA and local rules apply to both systems when facilities generate wastes such as spent cleaning solutions, sludge and solids from basins and filters, glycol, or concentrated chemical containers and residues.

Whether these materials are regulated as hazardous waste depends on their characteristics and local or state rules, requiring testing or knowledge-based determinations.

## Mechanical, Building Code, and Cross-Connection Requirements

State and local jurisdictions typically enforce these requirements through adoption of model codes. Common requirements include:

**Backflow prevention and cross-connection** controls on makeup water connections protect potable water systems.

**Pressure safety and relief provisions**, expansion tanks, and related equipment are especially relevant to closed loops.

**Local plumbing and mechanical code** provisions govern installation and modification activities.

These requirements are enforced locally and vary by jurisdiction.

## Special Case: Plate-and-Frame Economizers

When tower water passes through plate heat exchangers (PHE), poor tower water quality makes PHE channels more prone to fouling and plugging. This drives more frequent chemical cleaning and waste generation, which increases exposure to OSHA and waste discharge handling requirements.

Industry guidance notes that PHE fouling and plugging increases pressure drop and can be mitigated through filtration, straining, and good solids control.

## Key Takeaways

Identify your specific discharge destination first, then obtain the corresponding NPDES or pretreatment permits before operation. Implement ASHRAE 188-based water management programs with monthly monitoring for high-risk buildings, and maintain pH between 6.0-9.0 for most discharge permits. Consider upgrading to automated chemical feed systems with data logging to demonstrate consistent compliance across multiple regulatory frameworks.

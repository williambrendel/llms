# How is water lost in open tower and closed loop cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Water leaves open cooling towers through four pathways—evaporation, blowdown, drift, and leaks—while closed loop systems should have near-zero routine water loss after initial fill.

## Overview

A single large cooling tower can lose thousands of gallons per hour by design, yet a closed loop system losing even a few gallons daily signals trouble. The difference lies in whether the system intentionally contacts outside air or seals water away from it entirely.

## Open Cooling Tower Systems

Open recirculating cooling towers lose water through four primary pathways that the EPA has clearly defined.

**Evaporation** represents the intentional heat rejection mechanism. Hot water from the process flows over fill material while air passes through the tower, causing water to evaporate and carry away heat. This accounts for the largest portion of water loss in normal operation.

**Blowdown or bleed-off** involves intentional discharge to control dissolved solids concentration. As water evaporates, it leaves behind minerals and other dissolved substances. Without blowdown, these would concentrate to levels that cause scaling, corrosion, and biological growth.

**Drift** consists of entrained water droplets carried out with the exhaust air. Modern towers minimize this loss through drift eliminators—devices that force air to change direction and drop out water droplets before exiting the tower.

**Leaks and overflows** represent unintended losses from basin overflow, piping leaks, and valve issues. While not part of the designed operation, these losses occur frequently enough to warrant inclusion in water balance calculations.

Engineers commonly use this mass balance equation to track tower water consumption:

```
Makeup Water = Evaporation + Blowdown + Drift + Leaks/Overflows
```

## Closed Loop Cooling Systems

Closed loop systems seal water away from atmospheric contact and are designed to have near-zero routine water loss after initial fill and air purge. Any ongoing makeup water requirements typically indicate a problem requiring attention.

**Leaks** from piping, valves, joints, coils, and heat exchangers represent the most common loss pathway. These can range from minor weeping at gaskets to significant ruptures in heat exchanger tubes.

**Mechanical seal leakage** occurs at pump connections where rotating shafts penetrate the closed system. Even properly functioning mechanical seals allow small amounts of controlled leakage.

**Relief valve discharge and overflow from expansion tanks** happen when system pressure exceeds design limits or thermal expansion exceeds tank capacity. While these safety features protect equipment, they result in water loss.

**Venting and air removal events** cause some water loss during system bleeding to remove air pockets. This loss remains small unless chronic air ingress creates repeated venting needs.

**Intentional draining** for maintenance, freeze protection, or repairs removes water from the system but falls outside normal operating losses.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor makeup water consumption weekly in both system types to identify problems early. Open towers typically require makeup water equal to 1.2-2.0 times the evaporation rate, while closed loops needing more than 1-2% system volume monthly warrant immediate leak investigation. Consider installing automated makeup water meters and conductivity controllers to optimize blowdown in open systems.

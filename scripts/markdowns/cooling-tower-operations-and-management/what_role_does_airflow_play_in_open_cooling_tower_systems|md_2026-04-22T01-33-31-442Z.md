# What role does air flow play in the chemistry of Open Tower cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Airflow drives mass transfer processes that directly alter cooling tower water chemistry by controlling evaporation rates, gas exchange (CO₂ and O₂), and aerosol generation. These processes concentrate dissolved solids, shift pH upward through CO₂ stripping, maintain oxidizing conditions, and influence biological risk management.

## Overview

A single cooling tower fan can move over 100,000 cubic feet of air per minute, creating chemistry changes that operators often underestimate. Higher airflow accelerates evaporation and concentrates every dissolved solid in the circulating water, while simultaneously stripping CO₂ that raises pH and drives calcium carbonate scaling. The same airflow that keeps your system cool also creates the oxidizing environment that makes corrosion control essential.

## Mass Transfer Processes and Water Chemistry Changes

Airflow serves as the primary driver of mass transfer processes in open cooling towers. These processes directly modify water chemistry through three key mechanisms: evaporation control, gas transfer involving CO₂ and O₂, and aerosol generation.

The relationship between airflow and evaporation creates immediate chemistry consequences. More airflow produces more evaporation for any given heat rejection requirement and operating condition. Since evaporation removes essentially pure water while leaving dissolved solids behind, the circulating water becomes increasingly concentrated unless operators increase blowdown rates proportionally.

This concentration effect raises total dissolved solids and conductivity, creating a higher "cycles" tendency unless bleed rates increase. The concentrated ions also increase scaling tendency for calcium carbonate, calcium sulfate, and silica. Higher chloride and sulfate concentrations from this process can increase pitting risk and under-deposit corrosion susceptibility.

## CO₂ Stripping and pH Control Challenges

Airflow strips dissolved carbon dioxide from tower water, shifting carbonate equilibrium and typically raising pH across the tower. This pH increase can dramatically increase calcium carbonate scaling potential, especially on fill surfaces where air-water contact is most intensive.

Towers with high effective air-water contact or high airflow rates often require tighter pH and alkalinity control along with enhanced scale inhibitor performance. Operators frequently discover that chemistry programs that work well in low-airflow conditions fail when fan operation increases or fill efficiency improves.

## Oxygen Management and Corrosion Control

Air-water contact maintains dissolved oxygen levels near equilibrium with atmospheric air, creating a continually replenished oxidizing environment. This oxygen saturation supports corrosion reactions on susceptible metals unless corrosion inhibitors and filming agents provide effective control.

In open towers, operators generally cannot control oxygen levels through chemistry alone. Instead, corrosion control relies on protective films, inhibitors, deposit prevention, and appropriate metallurgy selection. The consistent oxygen presence makes these protective measures essential rather than optional.

## Aerosol Generation and Biological Risk

Airflow from fans contributes directly to aerosol generation and transport, creating drift that carries water droplets beyond the tower structure. This mechanism explains why drift eliminators serve as key controls for public and occupational exposure risk, and why cooling tower operation and maintenance remain central to Legionella control programs.

Chemistry and operations connect through drift losses, which slightly increase makeup water demand and can transport treatment chemicals out of the system. Poor drift eliminator condition increases offsite aerosol risk even when water chemistry meets all other acceptability criteria.

## Airborne Contamination Effects

Higher airflow and increased air-path exposure can increase contamination loading from external sources. Dust, pollen, and industrial particulates enter the system and raise suspended solids levels, create under-deposit risks, and increase dispersant chemical demand.

Organic contamination from airborne sources raises biocide demand and increases biofilm formation risk. These effects vary significantly by site location, with towers near roads, other cooling tower plumes, or industrial exhausts experiencing higher contamination rates.

## Airflow Extremes and System Response

Insufficient airflow from fan problems, blocked air intake, or fouled fill creates higher cold-water basin temperatures. Operators often respond by reducing blowdown to conserve water or by adjusting chemistry programs, but both responses can destabilize overall system control.

Warmer basin water from poor airflow can increase biological growth potential when disinfectant control operates at marginal levels. The temperature increase provides better conditions for microbial reproduction even when chemical residuals remain detectable.

High airflow conditions or high evaporation demand from elevated heat loads and dry ambient air create faster concentration rise in the circulating water. Blowdown rates must increase proportionally or scaling and corrosion safety margins shrink rapidly.

Enhanced CO₂ stripping under high airflow conditions creates higher pH tendencies, and calcium carbonate scaling risk increases substantially when alkalinity and hardness are present in the makeup water.

## Operational Control Strategy

Stable, well-controlled airflow makes chemical control significantly easier because evaporation rates, pH shifts, and concentration effects become predictable. Systems with fans operating correctly, uniform air distribution, clean fill, and intact drift eliminators respond consistently to blowdown adjustments and chemical feed changes.

Operators should monitor airflow performance through regular fan amperage checks, visual inspection of air intake areas, and fill condition assessments. When airflow changes due to equipment issues or seasonal conditions, chemistry control parameters need corresponding adjustments to maintain water quality targets and system protection.

# What is the impact of over blowdown in cooling tower and closed loop water systems?

## Executive Summary

Over-blowdown in cooling towers increases water costs, destabilizes chemical treatment, and creates operational instability, while in closed-loop systems it defeats the purpose of water conservation and accelerates corrosion by repeatedly introducing oxygen.

## Overview

A cooling tower bleeding just 20% more water than necessary can add thousands to annual operating costs while making the system harder to control, not easier. In closed-loop systems, excessive dumping creates a destructive feedback loop where each refill introduces fresh oxygen and dissolved solids that generate more fouling, leading operators to dump even more water in a costly cycle that masks underlying problems.

## Open Cooling Tower Over-Blowdown Impact

Over-blowdown means bleeding more water than necessary to control cycles and total dissolved solids (TDS). This seemingly minor operational error creates cascading problems across multiple system aspects.

### Water and Sewer Cost Increases

More blowdown directly increases makeup demand because, at steady operation, makeup water replaces both evaporative losses and blowdown. Since blowdown represents the primary controllable water loss in cooling systems, optimizing cycles becomes critical for reducing overall water consumption and associated sewer discharge costs.

### Chemical Treatment Disruption

Over-blowdown flushes inhibitors, biocides, and dispersants out of the system faster than designed. Treatment programs require higher chemical feed rates to maintain target residual levels, increasing chemical costs while reducing treatment efficiency. Poorly tuned controllers compound this problem by creating valve cycling that destabilizes residual control.

### Reduced System Stability

While lower cycles may reduce scaling tendency, excessive blowdown creates new operational challenges. The practice reduces alkalinity buffering capacity and changes pH stability throughout the system. When inhibitor residuals drop due to frequent dilution, corrosivity can actually increase in some treatment programs as protective films become disrupted.

### Thermal and Operational Instability

Frequent large bleed events create swings in conductivity and pH that translate to inconsistent inhibitor residuals. These oscillating conditions produce unpredictable scale, corrosion, and biological growth patterns. The net result contradicts the intended goal: costs rise while control becomes less stable rather than more predictable.

## Closed-Loop System Over-Blowdown Impact

Closed-loop systems should not require routine blowdown under normal operation. Over-blowdown in these systems typically manifests as excessive dumping and refilling that defeats the fundamental purpose of closed-loop design.

### Water and Chemical Waste

Each dump-and-fill cycle wastes both water and the expensive inhibitor chemistry designed to protect the system long-term. The frequent loss of passivation chemistry creates higher re-treatment demand and associated costs.

### Accelerated Corrosion Risk

Repeated refilling reintroduces dissolved oxygen into the system, restarting corrosion cycles that generate magnetite and iron sludge. This fouling accumulation creates more operational problems, leading operators to dump more frequently in a destructive feedback loop.

### Root Cause Masking

Dumping may temporarily improve water appearance and test results, but it fails to address underlying system drivers. The practice often masks critical issues like leaks, air ingress through open expansion tanks, inadequate startup cleaning, or contamination from glycol breakdown or process ingress.

## Identifying Over-Blowdown Conditions

### Cooling Tower Warning Signs

Cycles consistently run well below target values without any known limiting constituent driving the restriction. Blowdown valves show signs of leakage or operate too frequently with excessive duration. Conductivity monitoring reveals a sawtooth pattern with deep drops below the established setpoint, indicating poor control stability.

### Closed-Loop Warning Signs

Regular partial dumps become standard "maintenance" practice rather than emergency response to specific contamination events. System chemistry never stabilizes, with inhibitor residuals and pH values drifting significantly after each refill cycle. Iron levels and turbidity rebound quickly following refills, indicating ongoing corrosion and solids generation.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor cooling tower cycles weekly and maintain blowdown controllers to stay within 5% of target cycles, while closed-loop systems showing rising iron levels above 5 ppm should be evaluated for leaks and air ingress rather than dumped and refilled.

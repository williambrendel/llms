# What Causes Powdery Deposits When Cooling Tower Systems Shut Down?

## Executive Summary

Powdery calcium carbonate deposits in cooling tower basins after shutdown indicate controlled precipitation during water evaporation, not antiscalant failure. The absence of hard scale on heat transfer surfaces confirms the treatment program is working as designed.

## Overview

A cooling tower that develops soft, white powder after draining reveals something counterintuitive: your antiscalant may be doing exactly what it should. When 10,000 gallons of recirculating water concentrates down to puddles and films during shutdown, the remaining supersaturated brine precipitates calcium carbonate faster than any chemical can prevent it. The real question isn't whether powder forms—it's where that powder appears and what texture it takes.

## What Happens During System Drydown

When an open-loop cooling system goes offline and drains, water doesn't disappear instantly. Residual water collects in low points, clings to surfaces, and slowly evaporates. As evaporation proceeds, the remaining water becomes increasingly concentrated with dissolved minerals. This supersaturation forces calcium carbonate out of solution, creating the characteristic white residue.

The process accelerates as CO₂ degasses from the warming, stagnant water. This shifts the bicarbonate equilibrium toward carbonate ions, which readily combine with calcium to form calcium carbonate crystals. What you see in the basin represents the final stage of this concentration process.

## Interpreting the Deposits

The texture and location of calcium carbonate deposits reveal whether your antiscalant program is effective:

| Observation | Interpretation |
|-------------|----------------|
| Soft, powdery CaCO₃ in sump only | Controlled precipitation—antiscalant working |
| Hard scale on tubes, fill, or spray nozzles | Treatment program failing or improperly dosed |
| Powder forms only after system dries | Normal behavior for supersaturated water |

Antiscalants perform three distinct functions: they prevent crystal nucleation at supersaturation levels, distort crystal shapes to reduce surface adhesion, and keep formed particles suspended in flowing water. If you find soft powder in the basin rather than hard scale on heat transfer surfaces, all three mechanisms are functioning properly during operation.

## Why Antiscalants Can't Prevent Drydown Precipitation

Antiscalants prevent scaling, not all precipitation. They work by interfering with crystal nucleation and growth, allowing systems to operate above saturation levels that would normally cause immediate scaling. However, they cannot indefinitely hold minerals in solution when water concentrates beyond their design limits.

During shutdown, several factors overwhelm antiscalant effectiveness. Low flow conditions create localized supersaturation and pH drift that antiscalants cannot control without turbulent mixing. The chemicals themselves may degrade under high temperature, high pH, or oxidative conditions, especially if chlorine levels remain elevated. Most critically, antiscalants become ineffective below critical concentrations—exactly what happens as water volume decreases during evaporation.

**What it measures:** The relationship between water conductivity and total dissolved solids concentration

Formula:
```
TDS (mg/L) ≈ 0.65 × Conductivity (µS/cm)
```

**Example:** Water with 3,000 µS/cm conductivity contains approximately 1,950 mg/L total dissolved solids. As this water evaporates to half its volume, conductivity doubles to 6,000 µS/cm, representing 3,900 mg/L TDS—well beyond most antiscalants' effective range.

## Reducing Drydown Deposits

Test your system's Langelier Saturation Index and calcium carbonate saturation index to quantify precipitation potential. Values above +0.5 indicate aggressive scaling tendency that requires immediate attention. Verify antiscalant dosage, particularly when operating at high concentration cycles or elevated pH levels above 8.5.

Install sidestream filtration to continuously remove suspended calcium carbonate particles before they accumulate in low-flow areas. Consider adding biodispersants or sludge conditioners if deposits interfere with sump drainage or piping access. During planned shutdowns lasting more than 48 hours, flush the system with low-TDS makeup water to minimize residual mineral concentration.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor your system weekly for hard scale formation on heat transfer surfaces rather than focusing on soft deposits in the basin after shutdown. Maintain antiscalant residuals between 10-15 ppm during operation, and consider pre-shutdown flushing with makeup water when extended downtime exceeds 72 hours.

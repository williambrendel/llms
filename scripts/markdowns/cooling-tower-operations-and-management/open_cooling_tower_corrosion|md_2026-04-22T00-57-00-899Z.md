# How Do I Control Corrosion in My Cooling Tower System?

## Executive Summary

Controlling corrosion in cooling tower systems requires a balanced approach that maintains stable water chemistry, prevents deposits, controls microbial growth, and uses the right inhibitor package for your specific metallurgy and makeup water conditions.

## Overview

A single cooling tower system might contain carbon steel, stainless steel, copper alloys, and galvanized surfaces—each requiring different protection strategies. The three most common failures that destroy corrosion protection are loss of chemical control, deposit formation that creates localized attack cells, and biofilm growth that accelerates metal loss even when bulk water chemistry appears normal.

## Establishing Your Baseline

Before selecting any treatment program, document what you're protecting. Identify all metals present in your system: carbon steel, stainless steel, copper/brass components, and any galvanized surfaces. Each material responds differently to inhibitors and pH conditions.

Test your makeup water for alkalinity, hardness, chlorides, sulfate, silica, iron, manganese, pH, and conductivity. These parameters determine your scaling tendency and corrosion potential. Document your operating conditions including heat load, typical cycles of concentration, drift losses, blowdown control methods, and any periods of stagnation.

The right inhibitor chemistry and pH window depends entirely on your specific metallurgy and makeup water quality. No universal setpoints exist that work for every system.

## Managing Cycles of Concentration

Use conductivity-based automated blowdown to prevent excessive concentration of corrosive ions and dissolved solids. Track your cycles using a conservative tracer—typically conductivity, sometimes chloride concentration.

Cooling tower chemistry control focuses on pH, alkalinity, conductivity, hardness, microbial growth, biocide levels, and corrosion inhibitor concentrations. Controlling these parameters supports stable operation and enables higher cycles of concentration, reducing operating costs.

## Maintaining pH and Alkalinity Stability

Keep pH and alkalinity stable to avoid rapid swings that stress protective films. Low pH increases general corrosion rates across all metals. High pH drives scale formation, which then promotes severe under-deposit corrosion.

Use alkalinity management through acid feed or alkalinity addition only with tight control systems and safety interlocks. Set exact pH and alkalinity targets based on your site's specific combination of metals, water quality, and scaling tendency.

## Selecting Corrosion Inhibitor Programs

Match your inhibitor package to your system metallurgy. For carbon steel protection, phosphonate/phosphate-based programs combined with polymer/dispersant blends are common and effective.

Systems containing copper alloys require azole addition (such as TTA or BTA) for copper and brass protection. Maintain a measurable azole residual to sustain the protective film, especially when using oxidizing biocides that can degrade these inhibitors.

Galvanized surfaces require inhibitor strategies compatible with zinc coatings and proper startup/passivation procedures. If you discharge cooling tower blowdown to a regulated sewer, choose chemistries that comply with your discharge permit limits for phosphorus and metals.

## Preventing Deposit Formation

Deposits create localized corrosion cells and oxygen concentration gradients that drive pitting and under-deposit corrosion. This localized attack often proves more damaging than general corrosion rates suggest.

Maintain a scale control program using inhibitors and dispersants appropriate for your water's LSI/RSI scaling tendency. Add side-stream filtration processing 5-10% of your recirculation flow if you experience high suspended solids or significant airborne dirt loading.

Keep water distribution uniform across cooling tower fill to prevent dry spots. Clean basins regularly and verify proper water distribution during routine inspections.

## Controlling Microbiological Growth

Biofilm accelerates corrosion directly through microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) and indirectly by creating under-deposit conditions that disrupt inhibitor films.

Maintain an oxidizing and/or non-oxidizing biocide program appropriate for your system and local regulations. Implement regular cleaning and disinfection procedures with verified disinfectant residual control and monitoring.

The CDC emphasizes that scale control, corrosion control, sediment management, and system cleaning are critical for cooling tower operation and Legionella prevention. Monitor and manage disinfectant residuals consistently.

## Monitoring Corrosion Performance

Use at least one direct corrosion measurement method rather than guessing based on water chemistry alone. Install corrosion coupons for both steel and copper alloys, with stainless steel coupons if your system contains these materials.

Consider online corrosion probes using linear polarization resistance (LPR) or electrical resistance (ER) methods for faster feedback on changing conditions. Remember that pitting can be severe even when average corrosion rates appear acceptable.

Federal guidance for cooling towers explicitly requires coupons for monitoring both steel and copper corrosion. Good and bad corrosion rates are relative to your specific operating conditions.

## Operational Monitoring Schedule

Check conductivity (cycles), pH, alkalinity, hardness, inhibitor residuals, oxidant residual/ORP, and visual basin condition daily or weekly. Monthly or quarterly tasks include coupon exposure and reading, microbiological testing, fill/basin/nozzle inspection, and heat exchanger approach temperature trending.

Annual activities should include planned cleaning and disinfection, review of corrosion data versus chemistry history, and program adjustments based on performance data.

## Common Failure Modes

Loss of chemical control represents the most frequent cause of corrosion protection failure. This appears as inhibitor residual not maintained due to feed pump failure, empty chemical drums, poor calibration, or inadequate mixing. pH and alkalinity swings, uncontrolled cycles, and high chloride concentrations from over-cycling compound the problem.

Most inhibitor programs rely on protective film formation. No chemical residual combined with unstable chemistry equals film loss, followed by rapid metal attack. Many systems lack adequate corrosion monitoring or fail to act on monitoring data.

Deposits, scale, and sediment create the second major failure mode through under-deposit corrosion. This manifests as scaling on heat exchangers, fouled fill, sediment accumulation in basins, and high turbidity. Pitting beneath deposits often exceeds what average coupon corrosion rates suggest.

Deposits create differential aeration cells, concentrate corrosive species, and block inhibitor contact with metal surfaces. The CDC highlights that scale control, corrosion control, sediment management, and system cleaning are critical for cooling tower operation.

Biofilm and microbiological growth, including MIC, represent the third common failure mode. Systems show slime formation, elevated sessile bacteria counts, recurring fouling, and pitting with tubercle formation. Corrosion persists even when bulk water chemistry appears acceptable.

Biofilm accelerates corrosion directly through MIC mechanisms and indirectly by creating under-deposit conditions that disrupt inhibitor films. MIC in cooling systems is widely recognized, with bacteria as primary drivers requiring sessile monitoring programs.

## Key Takeaways

Test water chemistry weekly and maintain corrosion coupons with quarterly readings to verify protection effectiveness. Keep conductivity below system-specific limits (typically 1000-3000 µS/cm depending on makeup water) through automated blowdown control. Install side-stream filtration processing 5-10% of recirculation flow if suspended solids exceed 25-50 mg/L consistently.

# How does heat load affect treatment requirements in open tower and closed loop cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Heat load changes treatment requirements primarily because it changes surface temperature (heat flux) and therefore supersaturation at the metal surface. Higher heat loads generally increase scaling potential for most common cooling water scales, while lower loads can create silica precipitation risks.

## Overview

A cooling tower running at 50% design load might seem easier to treat, but the reality depends entirely on which scale-forming compounds dominate your system. Most operators discover that calcium carbonate becomes their worst enemy during peak summer loads, while silica can surprise them with stubborn deposits during cooler, low-load periods when solubility drops.

## Heat Load Effects in Open Cooling Towers

### Higher Heat Load Increases Scaling Potential

Most problem scales in cooling water are retrograde solubility salts where solubility decreases as temperature increases. Common scale-forming salts like calcium carbonate, calcium phosphate, and magnesium silicate can remain soluble in cooler bulk water but supersaturate and precipitate in the higher-temperature boundary layer next to heat-transfer surfaces.

Three mechanisms make scaling harder under higher heat loads:

**Hotter surface film temperature** creates lower local solubility and faster precipitation kinetics for retrograde salts.

**Higher evaporation needed to reject more heat** leads to faster concentration of dissolved solids unless blowdown increases proportionally.

**CO₂ stripping in the tower raises pH**, which further increases CaCO₃ scaling tendency at a given calcium/alkalinity level. This tower chemistry effect amplifies under high load conditions with increased air-water contact.

Treatment implications under higher heat load require tighter control of cycles, conductivity, and blowdown rates. Systems demand higher concentrations of scale inhibitor and dispersant, plus more frequent monitoring of scaling indices and exchanger performance through approach temperature and head pressure measurements.

### Lower Heat Load Creates Specific Silica Risks

For amorphous silica, solubility generally increases with temperature, meaning cooling can reduce silica solubility and trigger deposition under certain conditions. When temperature drops, silica solubility drops correspondingly.

Metal silicates such as magnesium silicate behave differently and exhibit inverse solubility, placing magnesium silicate among retrograde scale formers on hot surfaces.

**What it measures:** The relationship between temperature and silica solubility determines where precipitation occurs in the system.

Formula:
```
Amorphous silica solubility increases ~15-25 mg/L per 10°C temperature rise
```

**Example:** At 30°C, amorphous silica solubility might be 120 mg/L, but drops to 105 mg/L at 20°C. A system operating at 150 mg/L silica could see precipitation in cooler zones during low-load operation.

### Operational Implications for Tower Systems

If your silica issue involves amorphous silica or polymerized silica, risk increases in cooler zones like cold-water basins, low-load operation in cool weather, and sections with large temperature drops. This occurs especially at higher pH and high ionic strength conditions.

If your silica issue involves metal silicate compounds like magnesium silicate, higher temperatures near hot surfaces remain a strong scaling driver.

Treatment implications at low load for silica-sensitive towers require silica control through lower cycles even when scaling indices for CaCO₃ appear safe. Systems need tighter pH control since silica polymerization and metal silicate risks rise at higher pH levels. Maintaining dispersion and solids control becomes critical because silica often deposits as glassy films that resist removal.

## Heat Load Effects in Closed Loop Systems

### Scaling Behavior Without Evaporation

Closed loops do not concentrate by evaporation, but surface temperature still controls precipitation on heat exchangers and coils. Higher heat load generally increases scaling potential at heat-transfer surfaces.

Even with stable bulk chemistry, higher heat flux raises the boundary-layer temperature and can drive supersaturation and precipitation of retrograde salts on hot surfaces using the same boundary-layer concept that affects open towers.

In closed loops, high load and high outlet temperatures can increase scaling risk when fill water quality is poor or pH and alkalinity levels are high.

### Corrosion Considerations

Heat load affects corrosion indirectly in closed systems. Higher temperatures accelerate corrosion kinetics, but in closed loops the dominant control variable remains oxygen ingress and inhibitor/pH stability.

### Control Difficulty Comparison

For most scales including CaCO₃, CaPO₄, and many deposits listed in cooling water guidance, lower heat load makes scale control easier because the hottest-surface driving force reduces and evaporation/concentration rate typically slows.

For amorphous/polymerizing silica, higher temperature is generally more forgiving from a pure solubility standpoint since silica solubility increases with temperature. Temperature drops can trigger deposition, making low-load/cooler operation harder for silica-specific control.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor your system's limiting scale-forming species to determine optimal treatment strategy. If your limiting species is carbonate/phosphate/metal-silicate scale, higher heat load creates higher scaling drive requiring tighter cycles, increased inhibitors, and enhanced monitoring frequency.

If your limiting species is amorphous/polymerized silica, manage cool-side precipitation risks during low-load/cool conditions. Test silica levels weekly during seasonal transitions, maintain pH below 8.5 where possible, and remember that silica control is not always worst at the hottest surface.

For both open and closed systems, measure surface temperatures at heat exchangers monthly during peak load periods and adjust inhibitor dosing based on actual heat flux rather than design conditions.

# How do cycles of concentration affect water chemistry?

## Executive Summary

Cycles of concentration multiply the levels of dissolved ions in recirculating water systems, typically increasing scaling and corrosion risks while reducing water waste through less blowdown.

## Overview

A cooling tower running at 5 cycles of concentration transforms makeup water with 20 mg/L chloride into tower water with roughly 100 mg/L chloride. This multiplication effect touches every dissolved constituent in the water, creating a balancing act between water efficiency and equipment protection that defines how industrial water systems operate.

## Understanding Cycles of Concentration

Cycles of concentration (CoC) describe how much dissolved material builds up in recirculating water because evaporation removes mostly pure water and leaves dissolved ions behind. The calculation reveals this concentration effect through two equivalent approaches:

- **CoC ≈ (tower/blowdown conductivity) ÷ (makeup conductivity)**
- **CoC ≈ makeup flow ÷ blowdown flow**

These formulas work because evaporation concentrates everything that stays dissolved, while blowdown removes concentrated water to prevent unlimited buildup.

## How CoC Transforms Water Chemistry

### Ion Concentration Effects

CoC concentrates most dissolved ions roughly in proportion to the cycle number. As CoC increases, TDS and conductivity rise along with many dissolved constituents including calcium, magnesium, alkalinity, chloride, sulfate, and silica—provided they remain soluble.

The multiplication follows predictable patterns for conservative ions. When makeup chloride measures 20 mg/L and the system runs at 5 CoC, tower chloride approaches 100 mg/L because chloride typically stays fully dissolved. This rule-of-thumb proportionality holds for actual values, though side-stream losses, drift, leaks, and chemical reactions introduce variations.

### Scaling Tendency Increases

Higher CoC drives higher calcium hardness and alkalinity, often accompanied by rising pH. This combination increases the likelihood of exceeding solubility limits and forming scale, particularly calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), calcium sulfate (CaSO₄), and silicates.

CoC becomes limited by whichever constituent hits its constraint first. Common limiting factors include CaCO₃ scaling potential, silica solubility, chloride levels, and sulfate concentrations. The system cannot operate beyond the point where the most restrictive constituent causes problems.

### Corrosion Risk Changes

Higher dissolved solids and specific ions, notably chloride, increase corrosion tendency and stress corrosion risk for certain alloys. CoC increases both scaling and corrosion potential unless operators implement appropriate controls. The concentration effect amplifies aggressive ions alongside protective ones.

### Treatment Strategy Implications

Higher CoC typically reduces blowdown requirements, improving water efficiency. However, this efficiency comes with tighter control requirements for inhibitors and biocides, plus closer monitoring of limiting ions. The concentrated environment demands more precise chemical management.

Operators face a fundamental trade-off: too high CoC forces scale and corrosion events, while too low CoC wastes water and chemicals through excessive blowdown.

## Measurement Complications

Conductivity-based CoC calculations can mislead when significant precipitation occurs. Scale formation removes ions from solution, potentially decreasing conductivity and distorting CoC calculations. This measurement artifact can mask the true concentration state of the system.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor conductivity ratios weekly to track actual CoC performance, target CoC levels that keep the most limiting constituent below 80% of its threshold, and consider automated blowdown control systems when operating above 4 CoC to maintain precise chemical balance.

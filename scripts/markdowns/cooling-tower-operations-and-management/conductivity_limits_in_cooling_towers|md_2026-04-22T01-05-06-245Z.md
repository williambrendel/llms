# Why is high conductivity bad in a cooling tower?

## Executive Summary

High conductivity in cooling towers signals excessive dissolved salts that cause scaling, accelerate corrosion, reduce heat transfer efficiency by 10-15% with just 1/16 inch of scale buildup, and force operators to waste more water and chemicals to maintain system performance.

## Overview

A single millimeter of scale formation can cripple a cooling tower's efficiency more than most operators realize. When conductivity climbs in these systems, it reveals that dissolved salts have concentrated beyond safe operating limits, setting off a cascade of expensive problems that compound quickly.

## Understanding Conductivity in Cooling Systems

Conductivity measures water's ability to carry electrical current, and this measurement directly correlates with dissolved ion concentrations. In cooling towers, these ions include calcium, magnesium, chloride, sulfate, and sodium. Operators use conductivity as a control parameter because it provides real-time insight into cycles of concentration.

**What it measures:** The relationship between conductivity and total dissolved solids concentration.

Formula:
```
TDS (mg/L) ≈ 0.65 × Conductivity (µS/cm)
```

**Example:** Water with 3,000 µS/cm conductivity contains approximately 1,950 mg/L of dissolved solids, indicating the system has concentrated salts nearly three times beyond typical makeup water levels.

## How Evaporation Concentrates Problems

Cooling towers remove heat through evaporation, but this process creates an inevitable concentration effect. Pure water evaporates while dissolved solids remain behind in the circulating water. Each cycle of concentration doubles the salt content unless operators control it through blowdown. This concentration mechanism transforms harmless makeup water into a potentially destructive solution.

## Scaling Destroys Heat Transfer

High conductivity typically indicates elevated calcium hardness and alkalinity levels. When these minerals exceed their solubility limits, they precipitate as scale, primarily calcium carbonate. This scale formation devastates system performance with shocking efficiency.

Just 1/16 inch of calcium carbonate scale reduces heat transfer efficiency by 10-15%. The scale acts as an insulating layer on heat exchange surfaces, forcing equipment to work harder while delivering less cooling capacity. Energy costs climb while system reliability plummets.

## Corrosion Accelerates Under High Conductivity

Elevated chloride and sulfate levels contribute significantly to conductivity readings, and these ions accelerate corrosion across multiple materials. Carbon steel experiences general corrosion, stainless steel develops pitting, and copper alloys deteriorate rapidly under high ionic strength conditions.

The mechanism involves increased electrochemical corrosion rates. Higher dissolved salt concentrations create more aggressive electrolytes that attack metal surfaces more vigorously than dilute solutions.

## Chemical Treatment Becomes Ineffective

Many corrosion inhibitors and biocides have upper conductivity limits beyond which they lose effectiveness. High dissolved solids interfere with chemical performance, forcing operators to increase feed rates, switch to more aggressive inhibitors, and monitor systems more frequently. Treatment costs escalate while protection diminishes.

## Water Waste Multiplies Costs

Controlling high conductivity requires increased blowdown to purge concentrated salts from the system. This creates multiple cost penalties: higher makeup water consumption, increased sewer disposal costs, and loss of chemically treated water that operators must replace and re-treat.

The water waste becomes particularly expensive in drought conditions or areas with high water costs, where cooling tower operation can represent a facility's largest water use.

## Operating Limits and System Design

| System type | Typical upper limit (µS/cm) |
|-------------|----------------------------|
| Open cooling tower (carbon steel) | 2,000–3,500 |
| With good chemistry & materials | 4,000–6,000 |
| Above this | High risk unless specially designed |

Actual limits depend on makeup water chemistry, metallurgy, and treatment program effectiveness. Systems operating above these ranges require specialized design considerations and intensive management.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor conductivity daily and maintain blowdown to keep readings below 3,000 µS/cm for standard carbon steel systems. When conductivity exceeds safe limits, increase blowdown immediately rather than attempting to compensate with additional chemicals. Consider water treatment technologies like reverse osmosis or ion exchange for makeup water when local water sources consistently produce high-conductivity conditions that force excessive blowdown rates.

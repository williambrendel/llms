# Why Does Scale Form in Open Cooling Tower Systems?

## Executive Summary

Scale forms in cooling towers when evaporation concentrates dissolved minerals beyond their solubility limits, causing precipitation onto heat-transfer surfaces. The most common culprit is calcium carbonate, driven by rising pH from CO₂ stripping and increasing calcium and alkalinity concentrations as water cycles through the system.

## Overview

A single cooling tower can lose thousands of gallons per day to evaporation, yet every dissolved mineral stays behind in the shrinking water volume. This concentration effect, combined with chemical changes from air contact, creates conditions where minerals that were perfectly stable in your makeup water suddenly crash out as hard, heat-blocking deposits on the surfaces you need to keep clean.

## Main Drivers

### Evaporation Concentrates Minerals

Cooling towers reject heat by evaporating a portion of the recirculating water. Evaporation leaves dissolved ions behind, raising TDS/conductivity and the concentration of scale-forming species like Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, HCO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, and SiO₂.

### Chemistry Shifts During Concentration

As the tower "cycles up," alkalinity and calcium often reach conditions where calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) becomes supersaturated. Higher pH strongly increases CaCO₃ scaling tendency via the carbonate/bicarbonate equilibrium.

### Temperature Effects on Solubility and Kinetics

On hot surfaces like heat exchangers, local temperature exceeds bulk tower water temperature. For common tower scales like CaCO₃, effective solubility decreases with increasing temperature while precipitation rates increase—so scale often forms first on heat-transfer surfaces.

### Loss of CO₂ Raises pH

Towers aerate water, stripping CO₂ to air. CO₂ loss shifts bicarbonate → carbonate, increasing pH and promoting CaCO₃ precipitation.

### High Cycles from Inadequate Blowdown

If blowdown is insufficient, concentration continues to rise until solubility limits are exceeded.

### Seeding and Deposition Conditions

Scale forms preferentially where conditions favor nucleation and growth:

- High heat flux (hot tubes/plates)
- Turbulence changes, dead legs, low flow zones
- Rough or fouled surfaces that act as nucleation sites

## Common Cooling Tower Scales

**Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃)** represents the most common scale type, favored by high pH, high calcium, high alkalinity, CO₂ stripping, and hot surfaces.

**Calcium sulfate (CaSO₄)** forms when high calcium combines with sulfate. This scale shows less pH-dependency and can form at high concentration factors.

**Silica/silicates** develop from high silica and magnesium/calcium concentrations. These deposits resist control efforts and form tenacious deposits.

**Calcium phosphate** precipitates when phosphate is present and conditions favor mineral formation.

## Practical Indicators

You can spot developing scale conditions through rising cycles/conductivity without sufficient blowdown, increasing pH (especially with high alkalinity makeup), and scaling tendency flags tracked through indices such as LSI/RSI for CaCO₃ risk.

## Top Root Causes

**Unfavorable carbonate chemistry** tops the list. High makeup alkalinity and calcium hardness, combined with pH elevation from CO₂ stripping in the tower or caustic feed, drives calcium carbonate scale formation—the most common scaling pathway in open towers.

**Over-concentration from evaporation** occurs when evaporation concentrates calcium, alkalinity, sulfate, silica, and overall TDS. If blowdown control proves inadequate or conductivity controllers mis-set or fail, water reaches supersaturation and minerals precipitate.

**High local temperature and poor hydraulics** at heat transfer surfaces create problems even when bulk water chemistry looks acceptable. Hot tube or plate surfaces create local supersaturation and accelerate precipitation.

**Low flow, stagnation, dead legs, or fouled/rough surfaces** promote nucleation and deposition, turning borderline chemistry conditions into actual scale formation.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor conductivity daily and maintain cycles below system-specific limits based on your makeup water chemistry. Keep pH below 8.5 for most systems, and ensure heat exchanger approach temperatures stay within design specifications. When scaling occurs despite proper cycles control, evaluate your makeup water's calcium hardness and alkalinity—you may need acid feed or alternative treatment chemistry to maintain clean heat transfer surfaces.

# Why doesn't bromine kill algae in my cooling tower after 2 weeks?

## Executive Summary

Bromine at 3-5 ppm may not reach the algae location due to poor distribution, or the "algae" may actually be bacterial biofilm that requires dispersants in addition to oxidizers for complete removal.

## Overview

After two weeks of aggressive bromine treatment, that stubborn green growth coating your cooling tower's interior surfaces should show some signs of retreat. Instead, it clings there like nothing happened, despite free bromine levels that should kill most microorganisms within hours. The most likely culprit isn't organism resistance—it's that your biocide never actually reaches the problem areas at effective concentrations.

## Distribution and Measurement Issues

Where you measure biocide residual determines whether your treatment program succeeds or fails. Short-circuiting, poor distribution, or rapid chemical demand can leave interior fill and wetted zones under-dosed during the 2-hour contact time. This represents the most common field reason for no visible change after weeks of treatment.

Testing reveals the truth quickly. During your next bromine slug, measure free bromine directly at the point where algae appears—not just at the sample port near injection. Take readings at time zero, 30 minutes, 60 minutes, and 90 minutes. If residual bromine doesn't reach the biological population at target levels for the required contact time, you're dealing with a distribution problem, not organism resistance.

## Dead Biofilm Persistence

Killing microorganisms differs fundamentally from removing them. Oxidizers like bromine can eliminate living cells within minutes, but dead biofilm matrix stays attached and looks unchanged until something disperses or physically removes it. Most successful programs combine oxidizers with non-oxidizing biocides and biodispersants to strip established films rather than relying on oxidation alone.

Without dispersants and mechanical cleaning, total removal becomes delayed or never fully visual. You often end up with persistent staining and thin films even when organisms are completely dead.

## Misidentified Growth Type

True algae requires light for photosynthesis. Growth on fully wetted, non-sunlit interior surfaces more likely represents bacterial or fungal biofilm that appears green or brown from trapped solids and microbial pigments. Bacterial biofilms respond differently to treatment than algae and typically require more aggressive dispersant action.

## Biofilm Maturity Challenges

Intermittent oxidizing biocides work best when fed frequently enough and while biofilm remains minimal enough for chemical penetration. Thick, established biofilm resists intermittent oxidation alone. The matrix protects interior organisms from biocide contact, requiring sustained chemical attack or physical disruption.

## Expected Treatment Timeline

Visual progress follows predictable patterns when oxidants reach deposits and some dispersive action occurs:

| Week | Expected Visual Change |
|------|----------------------|
| 1 | Little change; possible slight bleaching or dulling of green; film still adherent |
| 2 | Patchiness begins with small clean islands; edges look thinner; more debris appears in basin after slugs |
| 3 | Obvious lifting at edges; stringy slough; localized clean zones; transient cloudiness after slugs |
| 4 | Noticeable peeling in larger sections, especially higher-shear areas; remaining material looks like stain rather than living slime |
| 5 | Mostly clean surfaces; residual tan/green staining; thin films in protected pockets |
| 6 | As clean as chemistry alone typically gets; mainly staining remains with persistent spots in low-flow areas |

## Key Takeaway

Test bromine levels directly at algae locations during treatment, not just at injection points. If levels drop below 3 ppm within 30 minutes at the problem area, increase dosing frequency to twice weekly and add a biodispersant to your program. Expect 4-6 weeks for complete visual improvement of established biofilms.

# How Does Wet Layup Change Water pH When Systems Go Offline?

## Executive Summary

Wet layup causes pH to swing in both directions depending on water temperature, alkalinity, and exposure conditions. Cold water warming up loses CO₂ and increases pH by 0.4-1.0 units, while stagnant open systems absorb atmospheric CO₂ and develop biological activity that drops pH significantly.

## Overview

A cooling tower that ran perfectly stable for months suddenly demands twice the acid when it restarts after a long weekend shutdown. Meanwhile, another system comes back online with pH so low that operators shut off acid feed entirely. These opposite pH swings during wet layup follow predictable patterns that most water treatment programs overlook.

## Temperature-Driven pH Increases During Spring Startups

Cold water entering a laid-up system carries excess dissolved CO₂ that becomes unstable as temperatures rise. This creates the most dramatic pH swings in spring conditions when building water enters cold and warms during the layup period.

**What it measures:** The relationship between CO₂ solubility and carbonic acid formation that controls natural pH buffering.

Formula:
```
CO₂ + H₂O ↔ H₂CO₃ ↔ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
```

**Example:** Water entering at 50°F holds 1.45 g/L of dissolved CO₂, but warming to 77°F drops capacity to 0.6 g/L. The excess CO₂ degasses, removing protons from solution and raising pH by 0.4-0.8 units in low-alkalinity water.

The magnitude depends on both temperature change and water alkalinity buffering capacity:

| Temp Shift | Alkalinity Range | pH Increase (Typical) |
|------------|------------------|----------------------|
| 50°F → 77°F | 0 – 10 ppm M | +0.4 to +0.8 pH units |
| 50°F → 95°F | 80 – 200 ppm M | +0.2 to +0.6 pH units |
| 41°F → 86°F | 20 – 60 ppm M | Up to +1.0 (if well-aerated) |

These graphs show CO₂ solubility dropping from 1.45 g/L at 0°C to below 0.6 g/L by 40°C, with the resulting pH increase reaching 0.6-0.8 units in poorly buffered water. This effect disappears in summer when tower water enters buildings already warm.

Overdosing inhibitors compounds the problem. Excess sodium nitrite, borate, or phosphate pushes pH higher if dosing pumps malfunction or operators miscalculate chemical additions during startup.

## pH Drops from Atmospheric Exposure and Biological Activity

Stagnant open systems experience the opposite effect through multiple simultaneous mechanisms that drive pH downward over 2-4 day layups.

| **Cause** | **Mechanism** | **Severity** | **Notes** |
|-----------|---------------|--------------|----------|
| Atmospheric CO₂ absorption | CO₂ dissolves into exposed water forming carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), reducing pH | High | Especially rapid if circulation stops and system is vented to air |
| Biological activity | Bacteria/fungi grow in stagnant water, generating organic acids (e.g., acetic, lactic) | High | Common in towers, sumps, or basins without biocide residual or where biofilm exists |
| Oxidation of nitrite | NaNO₂ oxidizes to nitrate or N₂ gas, releasing protons and lowering pH | Moderate | Accelerated in presence of dissolved oxygen, iron, and bacteria |
| Dilution or evaporation effects | Water loss via evaporation concentrates some ions, potentially destabilizing buffering system | Moderate | May not drop pH directly but can shift equilibrium, especially in soft water systems |
| Loss of buffering agents | Buffers (e.g., borates, phosphates) may degrade, precipitate, or be consumed | Moderate | Precipitation more likely if temperature or concentration fluctuates |

Stagnation creates ideal conditions for these pH-dropping mechanisms. Dissolved oxygen remains high without circulation, accelerating both corrosion reactions and bacterial metabolism. Open exposure allows atmospheric CO₂ and airborne bacteria to enter freely. Pre-existing biofilm colonies reactivate quickly without flow or residual biocide to suppress growth.

The nitrite oxidation pathway becomes particularly aggressive when dissolved oxygen, iron surfaces, and bacteria combine in stagnant conditions. Each mole of nitrite oxidized releases protons directly into solution while depleting the corrosion inhibitor that operators expect to protect the system.

## Prevention and Monitoring Strategies

Successful wet layup management requires different approaches for the two pH swing scenarios. Systems prone to warming-induced pH increases need alkalinity buffering or controlled CO₂ addition. Systems experiencing biological pH drops need biocide residuals and atmospheric isolation.

| Strategy | Action |
|----------|--------|
| Apply biocide before shutdown | Maintain a slug of oxidizing (e.g., NaOCl) or non-oxidizing biocide before and during layup |
| Monitor temperature differential | Track incoming water temperature vs. system temperature to predict CO₂ degassing |
| Maintain inhibitor residuals | Verify nitrite, azole, and phosphate levels before shutdown and after restart |
| Control atmospheric exposure | Seal vents and openings where possible to limit CO₂ absorption and contamination |
| Test pH within 24 hours | Check pH daily during layup rather than waiting for restart to identify trends |

## Key Takeaways

Test pH and alkalinity within 24 hours of any wet layup lasting more than 48 hours. Systems with incoming water below 60°F need alkalinity buffering above 50 ppm to prevent CO₂-driven pH swings exceeding 0.5 units. Open systems require biocide residuals of 0.5-1.0 ppm free chlorine or equivalent non-oxidizing biocide throughout the layup period to prevent biological acid production.

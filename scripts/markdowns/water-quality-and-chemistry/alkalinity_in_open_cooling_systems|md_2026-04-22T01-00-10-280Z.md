# How Does Alkalinity Affect Cooling Tower Performance and Treatment?

## Executive Summary

Alkalinity directly controls scaling, corrosion, microbial growth, and biocide effectiveness in cooling towers, with optimal performance typically requiring alkalinity below 120 ppm as CaCO₃. High alkalinity drives pH above 8.5, which accelerates calcium carbonate scaling, reduces biocide kill power, and creates conditions that favor biofilm formation.

## Overview

A single water chemistry parameter can make or break an entire cooling system. When alkalinity climbs above 150 ppm, calcium carbonate scaling becomes aggressive even with moderate calcium levels. Meanwhile, that same high alkalinity pushes pH into ranges where your most common biocides lose 50-80% of their effectiveness, creating the perfect storm for both fouling and biological growth.

## Scaling Potential

Alkalinity supplies the carbonate and hydroxide ions that drive precipitation of the most troublesome cooling tower scales. The chemistry works through predictable pathways that operators can anticipate and control.

Calcium carbonate forms when alkalinity provides carbonate ions that combine with calcium. This reaction accelerates dramatically when alkalinity exceeds 150 ppm as CaCO₃, especially if pH rises above 8.3. The supersaturation drives rapid precipitation on heat transfer surfaces where temperatures are highest.

Magnesium hydroxide precipitation follows a different pathway but still depends on alkalinity's pH-buffering effect. When pH climbs above 9.5, hydroxide ions react directly with magnesium to form this particularly tenacious scale.

| **Scale Type** | **Reaction Pathway** | **Alkalinity Role** | **Risk Increases When** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) | Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃↓ | Alkalinity supplies carbonate ions; supersaturation with Ca drives precipitation | Alkalinity > 150 ppm as CaCO₃ and/or pH > 8.3 |
| Magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)₂) | Mg²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Mg(OH)₂↓ | Hydroxide from high pH drives this scale at high temperatures | pH > 9.5 |
| Calcium phosphate | Ca²⁺ + PO₄³⁻ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂↓ | Carbonate buffering keeps pH high, driving PO₄ precipitation | Alkalinity and orthophosphate both high |
| Silica scale | SiO₂ polymerization | Indirect—higher alkalinity raises pH, reducing solubility of Mg/Si compounds | pH > 8.5 in high-silica makeup |

High alkalinity combined with elevated pH creates aggressive scaling conditions, especially when calcium exceeds 80 ppm and cycles exceed 3-4×. The scaling risk compounds because alkalinity not only provides the reactive ions but also buffers the system at the high pH where these scales become most insoluble.

## Corrosion Risk

Alkalinity's role in corrosion depends entirely on concentration and the specific metallurgy in your system. The same alkalinity that protects carbon steel can aggressively attack copper alloys and aluminum.

Low alkalinity below 50 ppm creates poor pH buffering. CO₂ absorption from air can cause pH to plummet, dramatically increasing corrosion rates of carbon steel and copper. The system becomes vulnerable to rapid pH swings that strip protective oxide films.

Moderate alkalinity between 50-150 ppm provides ideal conditions for most cooling systems. This range maintains pH between 7.5-8.5 while avoiding the aggressive high-pH conditions that attack non-ferrous metals.

| **Condition** | **Effect of Alkalinity** |
|---|---|
| Low alkalinity (< 50 ppm) | Poor pH buffering; CO₂ absorption can cause pH to plummet, increasing corrosion of carbon steel and copper |
| Moderate alkalinity (50–150 ppm) | Ideal range for buffering without aggressive scaling; helps maintain pH 7.5–8.5 |
| High alkalinity (> 200 ppm) | Tends to push pH above 8.8, which can increase aluminum, copper and lead corrosion, and stress corrosion cracking in stainless steel |

Excessive alkalinity above 200 ppm becomes corrosive to yellow metals like brass and bronze, and particularly attacks aluminum components. At elevated temperatures, high alkalinity can also promote stress corrosion cracking in stainless steel grades.

## Microbiological Activity

Alkalinity influences microbial growth through pH buffering and nutrient effects that create stable environments for biofilm development.

pH buffering from alkalinity maintains conditions between 7.5-9.0 that support robust biofilm formation. These films provide protection for bacteria unless aggressive disinfection or biodispersants break up the matrix. The stable pH environment allows microbial communities to establish and persist.

Some alkalinity sources, particularly bicarbonate from CO₂ conversion, can directly support microbial metabolism. Certain bacteria use these compounds as carbon sources, creating additional growth pressure in high-alkalinity systems.

| **Mechanism** | **Influence** |
|---|---|
| pH buffering | Alkalinity keeps pH in a range (7.5–9.0) that supports biofilm formation unless aggressive disinfection or biodispersants are used |
| Nutrient effects | Some alkalinity sources (e.g., bicarbonate from CO₂ conversion) can support microbial metabolism |
| Resistance to disinfection | High alkalinity waters may enable microbial communities to better resist pH swings from oxidizing biocides, reducing effectiveness |

High alkalinity waters enable microbial communities to better resist pH swings from oxidizing biocides. The buffering capacity reduces the effectiveness of pH-dependent kill mechanisms. Systems with poor biocide management and high alkalinity consistently develop stable biofilms that shelter Legionella, pseudomonads, and algae.

## Biocide Effectiveness

Alkalinity directly impacts biocide performance by controlling pH, which determines the active species concentration for most disinfectants.

**Oxidizing Biocides**

Free chlorine demonstrates the most dramatic pH sensitivity. The active species hypochlorous acid (HOCl) predominates below pH 7.5 but converts to the much weaker hypochlorite ion (OCl⁻) as pH rises. Alkalinity above 120 ppm typically drives pH above 8, reducing chlorine's kill power by 50-80%.

Stabilized bromine shows better pH tolerance, remaining effective up to pH 8.5, but still loses potency as alkalinity pushes pH higher. Chlorine dioxide stands alone among oxidizers as completely pH-independent, maintaining full effectiveness regardless of alkalinity levels.

| **Biocide** | **Optimal pH** | **Alkalinity Impact** |
|---|---|
| Free chlorine (HOCl) | 6.5–7.5 | Alkalinity > 120 ppm usually drives pH above 8, reducing HOCl to less effective OCl⁻ |
| Stabilized bromine (HOBr) | 7.5–8.5 | Less pH-sensitive; still effective in alkaline systems but loses some power > pH 8.6 |
| Chlorine dioxide | 5.5–9.0 | pH-independent; unaffected by alkalinity |

**Non-Oxidizing Biocides**

DBNPA hydrolyzes rapidly at high pH, dramatically shortening its contact time and reducing effectiveness. Systems with high alkalinity require much higher doses or more frequent applications to achieve the same microbial control.

Isothiazolone compounds show sharp effectiveness drops above pH 8.6. The combination of reduced stability and altered speciation makes these biocides poor choices for high-alkalinity systems.

| **Biocide** | **Optimal pH** | **Alkalinity Impact** |
|---|---|
| DBNPA | < 8.5 | Hydrolyzes faster at higher pH; shortens contact time |
| Isothiazolone | < 8.5 | Effectiveness drops sharply > pH 8.6; stability affected by metals and pH buffering from alkalinity |
| Glutaraldehyde | 7.5–8.5 | Relatively stable, but alkalinity can raise pH beyond effective zone |
| Polyquats, THPS | 6.5–9.0 | Generally stable; indirect impact via pH shift minimal |

Glutaraldehyde maintains reasonable stability across a wider pH range but still suffers when alkalinity pushes pH beyond 8.5. Polyquats and THPS show the best alkalinity tolerance among non-oxidizers, with minimal impact from pH shifts in the normal operating range.

## Operational Strategy

Effective alkalinity management requires balancing competing demands across all four impact areas. The optimal range depends on your specific system priorities and constraints.

| **Control Objective** | **Alkalinity Target (as CaCO₃)** | **Management Notes** |
|---|---|---|
| Prevent CaCO₃ scaling | < 150 ppm | Especially important if Ca > 80 ppm and pH > 8.2 |
| Minimize corrosion | 80–120 ppm | Balances pH buffering with minimized aggressive high-pH conditions |
| Optimize biocide effectiveness | < 120 ppm | Enables use of pH-sensitive oxidizers/non-oxidizers without overstabilization |
| Reduce biofilm formation | < 100 ppm | Combined with routine biodispersant and oxidizer slug feeds |

Monitor alkalinity weekly in most systems, daily during startup or when changing cycles of concentration. Test both total alkalinity and pH to understand the buffering capacity and current chemical balance. Alkalinity control through blowdown adjustment provides the most reliable long-term management, while acid addition offers short-term pH control when alkalinity levels are acceptable but pH runs high.

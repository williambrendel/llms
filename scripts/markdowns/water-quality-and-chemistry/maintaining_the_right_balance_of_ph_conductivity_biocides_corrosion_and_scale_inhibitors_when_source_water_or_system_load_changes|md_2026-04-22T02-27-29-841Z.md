# How do you maintain the right balance of pH, conductivity, biocides, corrosion and scale inhibitors when source water or system load changes?

## Executive Summary

Maintain balance by running the system to **defined control limits** (not "best guesses"), using **continuous monitoring where possible** (pH, conductivity, disinfectant residual), and **trend-based corrective actions** when values drift.

## Overview

Conductivity control (via blowdown) serves as the main lever controlling concentration of dissolved minerals, while pH control strongly shifts scaling and corrosion tendency. When operators try to fix water chemistry problems without a systematic approach, they often solve one issue while creating two others. The most effective systems use continuous monitoring for fast-changing variables and tie inhibitor dosing to the same trend signals that show stress.

## Establish a Control Framework

**Define control limits and response actions in writing.** For each key parameter, set a target range, alert limit, action limit, and designate who responds. The critical parameters include pH (drives carbonate scaling tendency and metal stability), conductivity (proxy for dissolved solids and concentration cycles), disinfectant or biocide residual (microbial control), corrosion inhibitor residual or surrogate control metric, and scale inhibitor residual or surrogate control metric.

Increase monitoring frequency when values become unstable. Variability itself triggers the need to monitor more often.

**Use the "four critical growth drivers" as a cross-check.** Sediment and biofilm, temperature, water age, and disinfectant residual represent key factors affecting microbial growth. If any remains uncontrolled, chemical dosing alone becomes unreliable.

## Maintain Conductivity and Concentration Cycles

Evaporation concentrates dissolved minerals. Higher concentration increases scaling risk and can increase corrosion risk through higher salt content and deposit formation.

**Use blowdown control to hold conductivity within limits.** The conductivity setpoint should tie to a maximum allowable scaling tendency and a maximum allowable salt level for materials and inhibitors.

**When source water changes, recalculate the allowable maximum cycles immediately.** Hardness, alkalinity, silica, chlorides, and sulfate can shift the safe limit. If silica or sulfate rises, allowable cycles often drop first, though this varies by site.

## Maintain pH

pH shifts carbonate chemistry and scaling tendency. pH also affects corrosion behavior and some inhibitor and biocide performance.

**Control pH to the range required by your scaling limit and your corrosion limit.** If scaling rises (for example calcium carbonate tendency), lower pH or reduce cycles. If corrosion indicators rise at lower pH, adjust pH upward and strengthen corrosion inhibitor control, while also lowering salts if needed through conductivity control.

Do not use pH control as a substitute for blowdown control. pH adjustment can reduce some scaling tendency, but it does not remove concentrated salts.

## Maintain Biocides

**Monitor and adjust disinfectant residual as part of cooling tower control.** Public health guidance explicitly states that disinfectant residual should be monitored and adjusted.

For oxidizing biocides, maintain a measurable residual at the control point and verify delivery to high-risk zones. If demand rises due to organic load or deposits, residual collapses first. Treat demand and deposits, not only dose.

For non-oxidizing biocides, match dose timing to contact time and system turnover. When load changes, shorten interval or increase dose within product directions until indicators stabilize.

**Treat sediment and deposits as a control measure.** Deposits protect organisms and consume disinfectant, making "biocide-only management" ineffective.

## Maintain Corrosion and Scale Inhibitors

Higher cycles and higher temperature increase deposition stress, especially at hot surfaces and low velocity zones. Higher salts and deposits increase corrosion stress and under-deposit corrosion risk through localized cells.

**Keep inhibitor control tied to the same trend signals that show stress.** If scaling tendency rises, reduce cycles, adjust pH, and verify scale inhibitor control level. If corrosion indicators rise, verify corrosion inhibitor control, reduce salts if possible through conductivity management, and remove deposits that create under-deposit cells.

## Managing Changing Source Water or Load

**Add continuous instruments and alarms for fast variables.** Conductivity and pH (plus disinfectant residual where used) should be continuously monitored when feasible to enable immediate adjustment.

**Add periodic laboratory checks for slow variables.** Test hardness, alkalinity, chloride, sulfate, silica, metals, and microbial indicators at frequencies based on their stability.

**Use a three-step response when any parameter drifts.** First, confirm measurement and locate the cause (instrument, dosing, blowdown, makeup change). Second, restore the control value within limits through corrective action. Third, verify stability by increasing monitoring frequency until variability drops again.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor conductivity and pH continuously where possible, test inhibitor residuals weekly during stable periods and daily during upsets, and maintain written control limits with specific response actions rather than relying on operator judgment alone.

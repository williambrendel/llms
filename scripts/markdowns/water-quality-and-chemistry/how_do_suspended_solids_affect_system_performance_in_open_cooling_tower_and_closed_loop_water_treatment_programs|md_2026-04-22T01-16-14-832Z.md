# How do suspended solids affect performance in open cooling tower and closed loop water systems?

## Executive Summary

Suspended solids create a destructive feedback loop in both cooling towers and closed-loop systems, driving deposit formation that reduces heat transfer efficiency, accelerates corrosion, and makes biological control nearly impossible.

## Overview

A single gram of suspended solids can trigger thousands of dollars in energy losses and equipment damage. In cooling towers, these particles become nucleation sites that make scale formation up to 10 times faster, while in closed loops, persistent solids usually signal active corrosion that's eating your system from the inside out. The CDC explicitly connects suspended solids to Legionella growth, making this more than just an efficiency problem.

## Open Cooling Towers: The Cascade Effect

### Heat Transfer Loss and Energy Penalties

Suspended solids—dust, silt, corrosion byproducts, and precipitated minerals—deposit directly on heat-transfer surfaces and tower internals. These deposits act like insulation blankets, reducing heat transfer capacity and restricting flow paths.

The immediate consequences hit your energy bills hard: higher condenser approach temperatures mean lost cooling capacity, while fans and pumps work harder to move the same amount of heat. Chiller efficiency drops as the system struggles against fouled surfaces.

### Scale Formation Acceleration

Suspended solids function as effective nucleation sites for mineral precipitation, making deposits "stickier" and increasing scale formation rates dramatically. These deposits also create oxygen differential cells—localized areas where oxygen concentration varies—that accelerate corrosion throughout the system.

### Biological Control Breakdown

Solids accumulate into sludge and biofilm matrices that dramatically increase disinfectant demand while reducing biocide penetration. The CDC explicitly identifies sediment and biofilm as key factors affecting Legionella growth and recommends considering filtration to reduce suspended solids.

### Equipment and Control Problems

Plugging becomes routine: strainers clog, nozzles block, and fill distribution systems fail. Drift eliminators and tower fill foul, reducing overall tower performance. Sample lines get dirty and probes scale over, leading to poor blowdown, pH, and ORP control stability. This creates cycling chemistry conditions that generate recurring problems.

The bottom line for cooling towers: solids drive a vicious feedback loop where **solids → deposits/biofilm → higher demand and corrosion → more solids**.

## Closed-Loop Systems: Corrosion's Warning Signal

### The Real Culprit

Closed loops don't concentrate minerals like towers, so the dominant solids problem comes from corrosion byproducts—primarily iron oxides and magnetite—plus entrained debris from poor startup or inadequate cleaning.

### Active Corrosion Indicator

NIH research shows that high turbidity or cloudiness indicates high-corrosion situations, since makeup water typically contains minimal dirt or silt. Elevated iron and copper levels relative to makeup water confirm active corrosion processes.

### Fouling and Plugging Damage

Strainers, coils, control valves, and small heat exchanger passages plug progressively. Differential pressure rises, flow imbalances grow, and coils lose heat transfer capacity. Deposits create under-deposit environments that promote microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) and localized attack. NIH emphasizes testing for biofilm and MIC to prevent microbiological fouling and under-deposit corrosion.

### System Control Instability

Solids foul sensors and contribute to erratic chemical control. Conductivity-based control systems fail to track real inhibitor residuals, increasing the risk of recurring corrosion and deposit cycling.

For closed loops, persistent solids typically indicate oxygen ingress, makeup contamination, or failed startup cleaning—not normal operation.

## Troubleshooting Workflow

### Step 1: Characterize the Solids

Never guess the composition. Start with visual inspection plus a magnet test to distinguish magnetite/iron from mineral deposits. Send samples for laboratory analysis: total suspended solids (TSS), particle size distribution, total and dissolved iron. If deposits exist on surfaces, request SEM/EDS or XRD analysis for definitive identification.

### Step 2: Locate the Source

**For cooling towers:** investigate airborne loading, makeup water quality, cycles of concentration, blowdown control effectiveness, and active corrosion or scale precipitation. Check basin sludge accumulation and fill condition—these areas serve as the primary solids reservoir.

**For closed loops:** measure makeup water rate to identify leaks or auto-fill problems, examine open expansion tank behavior, and locate oxygen ingress points. Investigate startup debris and incomplete passivation procedures.

### Step 3: Confirm Impact Metrics

Measure heat exchanger approach temperatures and differential pressure to quantify fouling. Track strainer cleaning frequency as a leading indicator. Monitor corrosion through iron trend analysis and corrosion coupons. For biological indicators, use BART (Biological Activity Reaction Test) and heterotrophic plate counts, with BART serving as a diagnostic tool when MIC is suspected.

## Control Actions That Actually Work

### Open Cooling Tower Solutions

**Solids removal:** Install side-stream filtration and establish routine basin cleaning schedules. The CDC recommends considering filtration specifically to reduce suspended solids.

**Chemistry stabilization:** Implement precise blowdown control to prevent precipitation and maintain stable pH levels.

**Biofilm management:** Deploy dispersant plus biocide strategies that reach all system components. Effective solids control makes reliable biocide control achievable.

### Closed Loop Solutions

**Stop oxygen and makeup ingress:** Quantify actual makeup rates, repair leaks and auto-fill systems, and address open expansion tank mixing or air entrainment issues.

**Remove corrosion products:** Install side-stream filtration systems, often incorporating magnetic separation when magnetite dominates. Implement low-point flushing procedures.

**System reset for poor startups:** Complete system cleaning followed by re-passivation, then control inhibitors based on measured residuals rather than conductivity readings.

## Taking Action Before It's Too Late

**For cooling towers:** Solids and biofilm conditions directly connect to Legionella growth factors and overall system control. Delaying solids control typically increases chemical demand, promotes under-deposit corrosion, and accelerates performance losses.

**For closed loops:** Ongoing solids indicate ongoing corrosion processes. Delaying correction increases deposit inventory, raises under-deposit corrosion and MIC risks, and increases the probability of plugging and system leaks.

The most effective approach combines immediate side-stream filtration (5-10 micron for towers, magnetic separation for closed loops), weekly turbidity monitoring below 5 NTU, and quarterly deposit analysis to track improvement and prevent recurrence.

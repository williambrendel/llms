# How does make-up water quality impact the water treatment of open and closed cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Make-up water quality largely determines what a cooling or closed-loop program must do, because treatment is mostly "managing what comes in" plus what concentrates, reacts, or grows in the system. In open cooling towers, dissolved minerals multiply with cycles of concentration, while closed loops face permanent chemistry impacts from initial fill water.

## Overview

A cooling tower running on reclaimed water can require 3-5 times more biocide than the same tower on potable water. The difference lies in what enters the system as make-up water — dissolved minerals, organics, and microbes that treatment programs must either remove, control, or work around.

## How Make-up Water Changes Treatment Needs

Make-up water impacts cooling systems through five core mechanisms that drive different treatment requirements.

**Dissolved minerals** like TDS, hardness, alkalinity, silica, chlorides, and sulfates drive both scaling risk and corrosion risk. In open cooling systems, these concentrate as cycles increase, so small differences in make-up become large differences in circulating water. Closed loops don't concentrate minerals the same way, but high chlorides and sulfates with poor buffering can still accelerate corrosion, especially with oxygen ingress.

**Suspended solids, turbidity, iron, and manganese** increase fouling and under-deposit corrosion while raising filtration and dispersant needs, mainly in open cooling systems.

**Organics and nutrients** including TOC, COD, ammonia, phosphate, and biodegradable organics drive biofouling and increase oxidant demand. These compounds make it harder to maintain biocide residuals, particularly with reclaimed water sources.

**Microbiology loading** means higher incoming bacteria and algae translate to higher baseline biocide demand in open cooling and higher risk of slime and Legionella amplification if controls are weak.

**Disinfectant residuals** like chlorine and chloramine can help reduce incoming microbes but also react with program chemistries, increasing oxidant demand and impacting some inhibitors while potentially promoting certain corrosion mechanisms.

## Open Cooling Towers: The Multiplier Effect

Open cooling towers amplify make-up water quality impacts through cycles of concentration. Most dissolved constituents rise roughly with cycles:

**What it measures:** The relationship between make-up water chemistry and circulating water chemistry in cooling towers.

Formula:
```
Circulating concentration ≈ Make-up concentration × Cycles (C)
```

**Example:** If make-up chloride is 50 mg/L, at 5 cycles circulating chloride reaches 250 mg/L, while at 10 cycles it hits 500 mg/L — a difference that can flip a system from manageable to high corrosion risk.

The blowdown relationship follows steady-state principles:

**What it measures:** Water discharge needed to maintain target concentration cycles.

Formula:
```
Blowdown (BD) ≈ Evaporation (E) / (C − 1)
```

**Example:** A tower evaporating 100 GPM requires 25 GPM blowdown at 5 cycles but only 11 GPM at 10 cycles, making water quality the key factor in achievable efficiency.

## Treatment Adjustments in Open Cooling

Open cooling systems require four main treatment adjustments based on make-up quality:

**Scale control** involves acid feed control, phosphonates and polymers, softening or RO, and silica control strategies depending on make-up chemistry.

**Corrosion control** requires inhibitor selection and dosing plus metallurgy considerations. Higher chlorides and TDS usually require tighter control and sometimes lower cycles.

**Deposition control** uses side-stream filtration, dispersants, and iron control measures.

**Microbial control** employs oxidizing and non-oxidizing biocides, bio-dispersants, and monitoring programs. Reclaimed water generally increases all these demands.

## Closed Loops: Fill Water Sets the Foundation

Closed loops face make-up impacts mainly at initial fill and during losses from leaks, vents, frequent makeup, or maintenance drains. The initial fill chemistry sets baseline chloride, sulfate, and alkalinity levels that persist throughout system life.

High chlorides and sulfates in fill water can permanently raise corrosion risk because closed loops don't "blow down" like cooling towers. Oxygen ingress often drives corrosion more than water chemistry, but poor make-up quality compounds the problem.

Treatment adjustments in closed loops focus on corrosion inhibitor system selection and dosage, pH and alkalinity control to keep inhibitors effective, and microbial control in warm loops or systems with frequent makeup. Glycol systems require particular attention since make-up water affects inhibitor reserve and can increase corrosion if not properly treated and compatible.

## Water Source Characteristics and Impacts

**Potable water** from municipal drinking water systems offers moderately consistent quality with some disinfectant residual. Hardness and alkalinity can be moderate to high, with generally lower organics than reclaimed sources. Open cooling systems often support conventional tower programs, with primary constraints from hardness, alkalinity, silica for scaling and chlorides and TDS for corrosion and cycle limits. Closed loops usually accept potable water for fill, but chloride and sulfate levels still require checking for sensitive metallurgies.

**Well water** varies highly by aquifer but commonly contains high hardness, alkalinity, iron, manganese, and silica. These sources often have low organics but may contain hydrogen sulfide, CO₂, or other reduced species. Well water typically lacks disinfectant residuals and sometimes carries higher chlorides and TDS from geology or coastal influence.

Open cooling systems using well water frequently need pre-treatment including softening, iron removal, and filtration, sometimes requiring RO. Stronger deposit control addresses common iron fouling. High silica or hardness often becomes the cycle limiter. Closed loops may experience deposit seeding from iron and manganese during initial fill, making filtration and conditioning beneficial.

**Reclaimed water** from treated wastewater reuse presents the most challenging and variable quality. These sources typically carry higher TDS, chlorides, sulfates, nutrients like ammonia and phosphate, and organics. Microbial loads run higher and more variable, with disinfection methods varying between chlorine, chloramine, UV, and other approaches.

Open cooling systems using reclaimed water require more robust microbiological programs with higher oxidant demand, frequent non-oxidizing biocide rotations, and bio-dispersants. Better filtration becomes essential, sometimes including coagulation and clarification. Tighter corrosion control and often lower achievable cycles result from high chlorides and TDS. Ammonia management prevents nitrification and biofouling while managing increased disinfectant demand.

Reclaimed water is generally not preferred for closed-loop fill unless treated to higher quality, since higher chlorides and sulfates raise permanent corrosion risk while organics and nutrients increase microbial risk in warm loops.

## Critical Parameters by System Type

**Open cooling towers** require monitoring hardness, alkalinity, pH, and silica for scale control and cycle limits. Chloride, sulfate, TDS, and conductivity determine corrosion risk and cycle limits. Iron, manganese, and turbidity drive deposition, filtration, and dispersant needs. TOC, COD, ammonia, and phosphate indicate biofouling potential and oxidant demand. Microbial counts and disinfectant residual type guide biocide strategy intensity.

**Closed loops** focus on chloride and sulfate for long-term corrosion risk, alkalinity and pH for buffering and inhibitor effectiveness, and hardness and iron for deposit seeding potential. Dissolved oxygen and air ingress risk from system design drive corrosion, while microbial risk matters in warm systems with frequent makeup or glycol.

## Key Takeaways

Test make-up water monthly for critical parameters — hardness, alkalinity, chlorides, TDS, iron, and TOC as baseline requirements. Potable water typically supports standard programs, well water intensity depends on specific chemistry, and reclaimed water demands the most robust treatment approach. For open cooling, maintain chloride levels below 300 mg/L in circulating water to control corrosion risk, while closed loops should use fill water with chlorides under 100 mg/L for long-term system protection.

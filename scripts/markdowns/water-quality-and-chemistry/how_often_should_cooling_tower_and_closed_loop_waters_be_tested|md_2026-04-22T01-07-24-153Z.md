# How Does Make-Up Water Quality Impact the Water Treatment of Open and Closed Cooling Systems?

## Executive Summary

Make-up water quality largely determines what a cooling or closed-loop program must do, because treatment is mostly "managing what comes in" plus what concentrates, reacts, or grows in the system.

## Overview

In open cooling towers, dissolved minerals multiply with each cycle of concentration — meaning small differences in make-up water become large differences in circulating water. A modest 50 mg/L chloride in make-up water becomes 500 mg/L at 10 cycles, potentially flipping a system from manageable to high corrosion risk. The type of make-up water — potable, well, or reclaimed — fundamentally shapes treatment complexity and cost.

## How Make-Up Water Changes Treatment Needs

### Dissolved Minerals Drive Scaling and Corrosion Risk

Total dissolved solids, hardness, alkalinity, silica, chlorides, and sulfates create two primary challenges. They drive scaling risk through hardness, alkalinity, and silica buildup, while chlorides, sulfates, and high TDS accelerate corrosion.

In open cooling systems, these minerals concentrate as cycles increase. Small differences in make-up become large differences in circulating water. In closed loops, minerals don't concentrate the same way, but high chlorides and sulfates with poor buffering still accelerate corrosion, especially when oxygen enters the system.

### Suspended Solids Increase Fouling

Turbidity, iron, and manganese increase fouling and under-deposit corrosion. They raise filtration and dispersant needs, particularly in open cooling systems where these particles accumulate over time.

### Organics and Nutrients Drive Biofouling

Total organic carbon, chemical oxygen demand, ammonia, phosphate, and biodegradable organics drive biofouling and increase oxidant demand. They make it harder to maintain biocide residuals, especially with reclaimed water sources.

### Microbiology Loading Affects Biocide Demand

Higher incoming bacteria and algae means higher baseline biocide demand in open cooling systems. This creates higher risk of slime formation and Legionella amplification if controls weaken.

### Disinfectant Residuals Create Complex Interactions

Chlorine and chloramine residuals help reduce incoming microbes but also react with program chemistries. They increase oxidant demand, impact some inhibitors, and can promote certain corrosion mechanisms depending on conditions.

## Open Cooling Towers: Why Make-Up Dominates

### The Multiplier Effect of Cycles of Concentration

In an open tower, most dissolved constituents rise roughly with cycles of concentration. The relationship follows a simple pattern:

**What it measures:** How dissolved minerals concentrate as water evaporates and fresh make-up replaces it

Formula:
```
Circulating concentration ≈ Make-up concentration × C
```

**Example:** If make-up chloride is 50 mg/L at 5 cycles, circulating chloride reaches approximately 250 mg/L. At 10 cycles, it hits 500 mg/L — a difference that can flip a system from manageable to high corrosion risk.

The blowdown relationship follows steady-state conditions:

**What it measures:** How much water must be discharged to maintain desired concentration levels

Formula:
```
Blowdown (BD) ≈ Evaporation (E) / (C − 1)
```

**Example:** A system running 1000 gallons per minute evaporation at 5 cycles requires approximately 250 GPM blowdown, while the same system at 10 cycles needs only 111 GPM blowdown.

### Treatment Adjustments in Open Cooling

Scale control requires acid feed control, phosphonates, polymers, softening, reverse osmosis, or silica control strategies depending on make-up quality. Corrosion control demands inhibitor selection and dosing with metallurgy considerations — higher chlorides and TDS usually require tighter control and sometimes lower cycles.

Deposition control needs side-stream filtration, dispersants, and iron control. Microbial control requires oxidizing and non-oxidizing biocides, bio-dispersants, and monitoring. Reclaimed water generally increases all these demands.

## Closed Loops: Make-Up Matters at Fill and During Losses

Closed loops are intended to be stable, so make-up impact centers on initial fill chemistry, which sets baseline chloride, sulfate, and alkalinity levels. Ongoing additions from leaks, vents, frequent makeup, or maintenance drains continue this impact. Oxygen ingress often becomes the true driver of corrosion in closed loops.

### Treatment Adjustments in Closed Loops

Corrosion inhibitor system selection and dosage must account for make-up quality. Systems may use nitrite/azole, molybdate, or phosphate blends depending on metallurgy and temperature. pH and alkalinity control keeps inhibitors effective and corrosion low.

Microbial control becomes necessary in warm loops or systems with frequent makeup — less common than towers but real. In glycol systems, make-up water affects inhibitor reserve and can increase corrosion if not treated properly or compatible.

High chlorides and sulfates in fill water can permanently raise corrosion risk because closed loops don't "blow down" the way towers do.

## Water Source Impacts: Potable vs Well vs Reclaimed

### Potable Water Characteristics

Municipal drinking water typically shows moderate consistency and usually carries some disinfectant residual from chlorine or chloramine treatment. Hardness and alkalinity can be moderate to high, while organics generally stay lower than reclaimed sources. Turbidity usually remains low.

For open cooling, potable water often supports conventional tower programs. Primary constraints come from hardness, alkalinity, and silica for scaling, plus chlorides and TDS for corrosion and cycle limits. Disinfectant residual can reduce incoming microbes but doesn't eliminate tower biological risk.

In closed loops, potable water usually works acceptably for fill, but operators should still check chloride and sulfate levels, especially for stainless steel, aluminum, copper alloys, and mixed metallurgy. Hardness and alkalinity matter less for scale but more for deposit potential if heated surfaces exist.

### Well Water Variables

Groundwater quality varies highly by aquifer but commonly shows high hardness, alkalinity, iron, manganese, and silica. It often stays low in organics but can contain hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, or other reduced species. Well water typically carries no disinfectant residual and sometimes shows higher chloride and TDS depending on geology or coastal influence.

Open cooling systems using well water frequently need stronger pre-treatment including softening, iron removal, filtration, and sometimes reverse osmosis. Deposit control becomes more important due to common iron fouling. Biological load may be lower than surface water, but the absence of residual disinfectant means towers still need robust biocide programs. High silica or hardness often becomes the cycle limiter.

For closed loops, iron and manganese in well water can seed deposits during initial fill — filtration and conditioning help. High chlorides and TDS in well water create long-term problems in closed loops because operators can't "manage it away" without partial drains.

### Reclaimed Water Challenges

Treated wastewater reuse presents the most challenging and variable conditions, changing by treatment plant and season. Reclaimed water typically shows higher TDS, chlorides, and sulfates than potable sources. It carries higher nutrients including ammonia and phosphate, plus higher organics measured as total organic carbon.

Microbial loads run higher and more variable than other sources. Disinfection methods vary and may include chlorine, chloramine, UV, or other approaches. Trace constituents can increase oxidant demand and biofouling propensity.

Open cooling systems using reclaimed water typically require more robust microbiological programs with higher oxidant demand, more frequent non-oxidizing rotations, and biodispersants. Better filtration becomes necessary, sometimes including coagulation and clarification depending on turbidity. Corrosion control tightens and often achieves lower cycles if chlorides and TDS run high.

Ammonia management becomes critical because it drives nitrification, biofouling, and increases disinfectant demand while interacting with chlorine chemistry. Monitoring frequency must increase due to source variability.

Reclaimed water generally isn't preferred for closed-loop fill unless treated to higher quality. Higher chlorides and sulfates raise corrosion risk permanently, while organics and nutrients increase microbial risk if the loop runs warm or has frequent makeup.

When reclaimed water must be used in closed loops, additional treatment before fill becomes common. This may include filtration plus softening or reverse osmosis, or at minimum controlling chlorides and organics with conservative inhibitor and biocide approaches.

## Critical Parameters by System Type

### Open Cooling Tower Priorities

The most important make-up parameters to guide treatment and expected cost include hardness, alkalinity, pH, and silica for scale control and cycle limits. Chloride, sulfate, TDS, and conductivity determine corrosion and cycle limits. Iron, manganese, and turbidity drive deposition, filtration, and dispersant needs.

Total organic carbon, chemical oxygen demand, ammonia, and phosphate affect biofouling and oxidant demand. Microbial counts or ATP measurements guide biocide strategy intensity. Disinfectant residual type — chlorine versus chloramine — shapes oxidant strategy and demand.

### Closed Loop Priorities

The most important make-up and fill parameters include chloride and sulfate for long-term corrosion risk. Alkalinity and pH affect buffering and inhibitor effectiveness. Hardness and iron matter less than in towers but still affect deposit seeding.

Dissolved oxygen and air ingress risk — determined by system design and operation — drive corrosion. Microbial risk becomes relevant in warm systems with frequent makeup or glycol, potentially requiring biocide controls.

## Treatment Intensity by Water Source

Potable water allows baseline programs to work with moderate treatment intensity. Well water can be easy or very difficult — treatment intensity depends on hardness, iron, silica, and TDS levels.

Reclaimed water shows the highest variability and highest biofouling and corrosion management burden in open cooling systems. It's generally the least desirable choice for closed loops unless upgraded or used with conservative controls.

## Key Takeaway

Test make-up water monthly for hardness, alkalinity, chlorides, and TDS as a minimum. Keep chlorides below 200 mg/L in make-up for closed loops, and consider pre-treatment if using reclaimed water. Switch to non-oxidizing biocides when reclaimed water drives oxidant demand above 5 mg/L residual in towers.

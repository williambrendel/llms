# How do plastics and elastomers contribute to nutrients for biological growth in cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Plastics and elastomers release organic chemicals into cooling water that microorganisms use as food, creating localized nutrient sources that promote biofilm formation. Studies show these materials can leach assimilable organic carbon from additives like plasticizers and oils, with elastomers like ethylene propylene diene monomer rubber supporting significantly more microbial growth than metal surfaces.

## Overview

A single cooling tower contains thousands of square feet of polymer surfaces—from fill media to gaskets—that continuously shed microscopic amounts of organic compounds into the circulating water. Research comparing biofilm formation on different materials reveals that oil-rich elastomer surfaces can support microbial communities up to several times denser than copper or stainless steel under identical conditions. This happens because the very additives that make these materials flexible and durable also serve as ready-made food sources for bacteria and fungi.

## How plastics contribute nutrients

Synthetic polymeric materials release dissolved organic molecules that bacteria readily consume. Reviews of plumbing systems demonstrate that plastic pipes and components leach assimilable organic carbon directly into water streams, creating a continuous nutrient supply for microbial growth.

Recent studies of drinking water and wastewater systems confirm that biofilm carbon sources derive partly from upstream water chemistry and partly from the plastic infrastructure itself. The plastic material acts as both a surface for attachment and a slow-release nutrient source.

Common contributing compounds vary by plastic formulation but typically include plasticizers and processing aids, antioxidants and stabilizers, plus low-molecular-weight oligomers and residual monomers left over from manufacturing. Research on polymeric tubing specifically measured migration of microbially available carbon from plasticizer additives and directly linked this leaching to increased biomass formation potential in laboratory tests.

## How elastomers contribute nutrients

Rubber compounds release significantly more organic compounds than most rigid plastics. Materials migration studies document dozens of organic chemicals leaching from typical elastomer formulations, with many compounds showing direct correlation to increased microbiological activity in water systems.

Oil-rich elastomer formulations prove especially problematic for biological control. Controlled studies on ethylene propylene diene monomer rubber surfaces found that microorganisms both adhered more readily and grew more extensively on oil-rich formulations compared to low-oil or oil-free alternatives. The oils embedded in rubber compounds during manufacturing provide concentrated nutrition sources that microbes can access and metabolize over time.

## Why this matters in cooling systems specifically

Cooling towers concentrate the leaching effect through massive surface areas of polymer components. Modern cooling tower fill alone can present 15-20 square feet of plastic surface per cubic foot of fill volume. Even minimal leaching rates become significant when multiplied across these extensive surface areas under continuous wetting conditions.

Elastomer seals, gaskets, and flexible joints create additional risk zones where leached organics concentrate in low-shear environments. These protected microenvironments allow dissolved nutrients to accumulate in the surface boundary layer where biofilms typically initiate and establish. The combination of shelter, nutrients, and reduced water velocity creates ideal conditions for rapid microbial colonization.

## Key takeaways

Monitor dissolved organic carbon levels monthly in systems with extensive polymer components, targeting levels below 1-2 mg/L to limit biological activity. When replacing elastomer components, specify low-oil formulations and avoid plasticizer-heavy compounds where possible. Consider upgrading to materials specifically tested for low biological growth potential, particularly in critical heat transfer zones where biofilm formation directly impacts system performance.

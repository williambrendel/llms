# What causes high biological counts despite biocide feed in cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

High biological counts with normal biocide feed typically result from three core issues: the biocide isn't reaching or staying active where you're sampling, established biofilm and deposits protect organisms from treatment, or continuous re-seeding with nutrients outpaces your biocide program.

## Overview

A properly dosed biocide program should control biological growth, yet many facilities see persistent high counts in their cooling towers and closed loops. The most common culprit isn't insufficient biocide dosing—it's that a 10 ppm chlorine residual at your dosing point might be zero ppm in basin corners where biofilm thrives. Understanding why biocides fail reveals that cooling systems are complex ecosystems where chemistry, hydraulics, and biology intersect in ways that can undermine even well-intentioned treatment programs.

## Open Cooling Towers: When Biocide Programs Fall Short

### Biocide Present But Not Effective

**Low or unstable disinfectant residual** represents the most frequent cause of biological control failures. Poor control systems, intermittent feed, or equipment issues with pumps, injectors, tubing, power, or control logic create gaps where organisms multiply rapidly.

**High oxidant demand** consumes available biocide before it can kill target organisms. High organic loads from process leaks, airborne organics, pollen, and dust create immediate chlorine demand. Ammonia and nitrogen compounds prove particularly problematic because they react with free chlorine to form less effective chloramines. Reducing agents like sulfides and iron also consume oxidizers before they reach biological targets.

**Wrong pH for the oxidizer** dramatically affects biocide efficacy. Higher pH reduces the fraction of hypochlorous acid (HOCl), the most effective chlorine species, so identical ppm measurements deliver vastly different disinfection power.

**Poor distribution and mixing** means biocide never contacts biofilm in basin corners, fill sections, or remote headers. Bad injection locations and insufficient mixing create zones where organisms thrive despite adequate chemistry elsewhere in the system.

**Short-circuiting and dead zones** in basins and low-flow areas allow biological growth to establish and spread back into treated areas.

**Biofilm and deposits** physically shield organisms from biocide contact. The combination of slime, silt, and scale creates protective matrices that routine biocide dosing cannot penetrate. Scale, corrosion, and sediment controls become critical for biological control success.

### Program Design Gaps

**Missing biodispersants or wrong timing** leaves biofilm matrices intact. Applying biocide without first opening protective biofilm structures wastes chemistry and fails to reach target organisms.

**Over-reliance on single chemistry** allows organisms to adapt. Cooling towers often require rotation between oxidizing and non-oxidizing biocides, plus periodic deep cleaning, especially when biofilm becomes established.

**Inadequate blowdown creating high cycles** concentrates suspended solids and nutrients, providing more attachment sites and faster growth conditions.

### Continuous Re-seeding

**Makeup water quality** issues introduce bacteria and nutrients continuously. High-bacteria source water or "dirty" onsite reuse water can overwhelm biocide programs through sheer microbial pressure.

**Environmental loading** from nearby food plants, composting operations, or cooling tower intakes near exhaust sources raises nutrient and organic loads beyond program design limits.

### Measurement Artifacts

**Wrong sampling timing** produces misleading results. Sampling right before a slug dose versus after treatment creates dramatically different readings that don't reflect actual system conditions.

**Missing neutralizer in sample bottles** allows biocide to continue killing organisms after sampling, creating falsely low counts. Poor handling and contamination create the opposite problem.

**Dip slides, HPC, and ATP limitations** mean these tools trend general bacteria rather than specific problematic species. BART tests often provide more actionable information for cooling tower biology.

### Highest-Yield Troubleshooting Steps

Confirm **actual residual at multiple points** including return lines, basin, and the farthest headers at different times throughout the treatment cycle.

Perform **drawdown tests** to quantify actual oxidant demand by measuring how quickly residual decays in your specific system water.

Inspect for **sediment and biofilm** in basins, strainers, fill, and distribution decks, then correct filtration and cleaning procedures accordingly.

## Closed-Loop Systems: Biology in Sealed Environments

Closed loops should maintain low biological activity when properly sealed and clean. Persistent counts usually indicate oxygen ingress, makeup water addition, biofilm establishment, nutrient availability, and flow stagnation working together.

### Oxygen and Makeup Water Ingress

**Leaks, frequent makeup, venting issues, and expansion tank problems** represent the primary root cause of biological issues in closed systems. These conditions introduce dissolved oxygen and microbes while enabling growth and corrosion product formation that shelters biofilm.

### Internal Nutrient Sources

**Nitrite programs** create a paradox where nitrite, a common corrosion inhibitor, serves as a nutrient source for denitrifying bacteria under certain conditions.

**Glycol breakdown products** provide carbon sources for microbial growth, especially in systems with degraded or contaminated glycol.

**Corrosion products and deposits** become both food and habitat, supporting under-deposit biofilm that resists biocide treatment.

### Established Biofilm

Systems not **properly cleaned and flushed at startup** or those experiencing contamination events develop entrenched biofilm that resists routine biocide dosing.

### Hydraulic and Temperature Factors

**Low flow areas, dead legs, and inactive branches** including out-of-service coils and bypasses create stagnant zones where biofilm grows despite main loop treatment.

**Warm closed loops** such as condenser water and heat recovery systems support higher microbial growth rates compared to chilled water systems.

### Biocide Program Issues

**High solids consumption** of biocide, incompatibility with corrosion inhibitors, or dosing at wrong locations prevents biocide from reaching remote system branches.

**Insufficient contact time** results when biocide gets dumped into fast-moving lines without adequate mixing or hold time.

### Highest-Yield Closed-Loop Diagnostics

**Quantify makeup rate** first—if it isn't near zero, solve water ingress before addressing biological treatment.

**Inspect low points and strainers** for black slime indicating microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC), iron sludge, and biofilm accumulation.

**Verify inhibitor levels** and examine conditions that might support denitrification in nitrite-based programs.

## Key Takeaways

Address root causes systematically rather than increasing biocide dosing. Test residuals at multiple system points weekly, maintain proper pH for your chosen biocide, and inspect for biofilm monthly in cooling towers. For closed loops, eliminate makeup water ingress first—target less than 1% system volume monthly—then address any established biofilm through mechanical cleaning and biodispersant treatment before resuming routine biocide programs.

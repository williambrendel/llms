# Why Do I See Low ATP But High BART Results?

## Executive Summary

Low ATP with high BART results typically indicates substantial biofilm growth on system surfaces while the bulk water remains relatively clean. This pattern occurs because ATP measures what's suspended in your water sample right now, while BART tests reveal the growth potential of organisms that may be living primarily as attached biofilms.

## Overview

A cooling tower can show persistently low microbial ATP readings while HAB-BART and SLYM-BART tests scream "very high" week after week. This apparent contradiction catches many water treatment professionals off guard, especially when deciding whether to invest in expensive biocide treatments. The explanation lies in where the microbes actually live versus where you're testing for them.

## Understanding ATP vs BART: Different Questions, Different Answers

ATP testing provides a snapshot of viable microbial biomass present in your water sample at the moment of collection. **Free ATP** measures dissolved ATP outside living cells, often from dead or lysed organisms. **Total ATP** captures both this dissolved ATP plus the ATP inside living cells and particles.

BART tests work entirely differently. These incubation-based methods interpret growth activity and time-to-reaction to estimate the presence and activity of target groups. **HAB-BART** indicates heterotrophic aerobic bacteria activity through dye bleaching and reduction. **SLYM-BART** reveals slime-forming organisms and estimates their population size and types based on growth patterns.

The key difference: ATP asks "what's here now?" while BART asks "what can grow under favorable conditions?"

## The Biofilm Factor

Most biomass in cooling systems lives attached to surfaces as biofilm rather than floating freely in the bulk water. Biofilms colonize pipe walls, heat exchanger heads, strainers, basin surfaces, and dead legs. When you sample bulk water, you're missing this attached population entirely.

**What it measures:** The relationship between conductivity and total dissolved solids in water

Formula:
```
TDS (mg/L) ≈ 0.65 × Conductivity (µS/cm)
```

**Example:** Water with 1,500 µS/cm conductivity contains approximately 975 mg/L total dissolved solids, indicating moderate mineral content that could support biofilm formation.

BART tests need only a small inoculum of cells sloughed from biofilm or trapped in particulates. These few organisms then multiply rapidly in the enriched test environment. The "very high" BART reading reflects this growth velocity and observed activity, not the actual concentration in your original sample.

## Sampling Location Masks the Problem

Where and how you sample dramatically affects results. Well-mixed sampling points, locations after filtration, or clean headers can show low ATP even while slime thrives in low-flow zones, strainers, basins, and heat exchanger heads. Occasional sloughing from biofilms may seed your BART tests but won't reliably raise ATP readings unless you happen to catch an active sloughing event.

This sampling bias explains the most common real-world pattern of repeated "low ATP, high BART" results. Your bulk water genuinely contains low suspended biomass at the sampling location and time, while a persistent slime and heterotroph community maintains strong regrowth potential throughout the system.

## Making Treatment Decisions

Two weeks of very high HAB and SLYM BART results with consistently low ATP indicates low suspended biomass at your sampling point combined with persistent slime and heterotroph presence in the system. This pattern alone doesn't justify expensive system-wide biocide treatment.

Before spending on biocides, test biofilm directly by swabbing strainers, basin walls, heat exchanger heads, dead legs, and low-flow points. Run ATP tests on these swab samples to confirm whether biomass is actually attached to surfaces. Look for operational evidence of slime impacts: rising differential pressure, frequent filter loading, degraded heat transfer, visible slime, or fouling deposits.

## Key Takeaway

Confirm biofilm presence through direct surface sampling and ATP testing of swabs before proceeding with biocide treatment. Test biofilm monthly during high-risk periods, targeting ATP levels below 1,000 RLU on surface swabs, and consider targeted biocide application to specific fouled components rather than system-wide treatment when operational impacts are confirmed.

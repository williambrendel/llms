# How do audits evaluate biological programs? What should an audit checklist include for open-loop and closed-loop systems?

## Executive Summary

Audits evaluate biological control programs by verifying six core elements: program design with current system documentation, technically appropriate controls, disciplined monitoring at required frequencies, quality corrective actions when limits are exceeded, verification that the program was followed, and validation that it actually reduces risk. Open-loop systems like cooling towers require intensive focus on aerosol control and disinfectant residuals, while closed-loop systems emphasize corrosion monitoring and contamination prevention during maintenance events.

## Overview

A cooling tower that passes daily chemistry checks can still fail an audit if nobody responds when those checks go out of range. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports that effective water management requires not just monitoring, but documented corrective action and program verification. Closed systems face different risks than open towers, but both can develop biofilm, microbiologically influenced corrosion, and control failures that auditors specifically target.

## How Audits Evaluate Biological Programs

Audits test six fundamental program elements. **Program design** requires written documentation with current system descriptions, flow diagrams, responsible personnel, and identified risk areas. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends identifying building water systems, hazardous conditions, and control locations as core program elements.

**Technical adequacy** examines whether selected controls match the system type. Cooling towers should address scale, corrosion, sediment, water age, temperature, and disinfectant residuals. **Monitoring discipline** verifies that parameters are measured at required frequencies, using specified methods, with complete documentation. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes routine monitoring of key risk areas as essential.

**Response quality** checks whether the program specifies actions for missed limits and whether records show timely corrective action. Corrective action is a required program element in current guidance. **Verification and validation** serve different functions—verification confirms the program was followed as written, while validation proves it reduced risk in practice. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention distinguishes these two functions in its water management framework.

**Record integrity** examines whether logs are complete, signed, dated, trended, reviewed, and retained long enough to support trend analysis and investigations.

## Example Audit Checklist for Open-Loop Systems

### Document Control
- ☐ Current written biological control or water management program is available
- ☐ Current schematic shows basin, fill, drift eliminators, heat exchanger, side-stream filtration, chemical feed points, bleed-off, make-up water, and sampling points
- ☐ Program names responsible staff and backup staff
- ☐ Review date is current

### Hazard Identification
- ☐ Program addresses sediment, scale, corrosion, stagnation, water age, sunlight, temperature, and disinfectant residual—recognized drivers of Legionella growth and microbial fouling in cooling towers
- ☐ Areas with poor flow, dead zones, splash surfaces, and aerosol release are identified
- ☐ Seasonal start-up and shutdown risks are addressed

### Control Measures and Limits
- ☐ Biocide program defines product, dose basis, feed method, target range, and frequency
- ☐ Secondary controls are defined for scale, corrosion, and solids control
- ☐ Blowdown or bleed control is defined
- ☐ Control limits are written for disinfectant residual, conductivity or cycles of concentration, pH, and visible cleanliness

### Monitoring
- ☐ Daily or defined-operating-day checks are documented for critical chemistry parameters
- ☐ Routine dip slide, adenosine triphosphate, or other microbiological trend method is specified
- ☐ Sampling points are consistent and justified
- ☐ Meters and test kits have current calibration or quality checks
- ☐ Logs include date, time, result, initials, and follow-up

### Physical Condition
- ☐ Basin is free of excessive sludge, scale, and debris
- ☐ Drift eliminators are intact
- ☐ No dead legs, bypasses, or low-flow zones are ignored
- ☐ Filtration equipment operates as intended
- ☐ Tower inspection frequency is defined and followed

### Corrective Action
- ☐ Out-of-limit responses are written, specific, and time-bound
- ☐ Records show actual use of corrective action when limits were missed
- ☐ Repeat deviations trigger escalation, root-cause review, and program adjustment
- ☐ Emergency response steps exist for suspected disease case linkage or gross loss of control

### Verification and Validation
- ☐ Internal review of logs is performed at defined frequency
- ☐ Cleaning and disinfection records are present for start-up, shutdown, and scheduled maintenance
- ☐ Trend review shows whether microbial indicators and chemistry remain stable over time
- ☐ Validation method is defined, such as environmental testing strategy or disease surveillance link

## Example Audit Checklist for Closed-Loop Systems

### Document Control
- ☐ Current written program is available for chemistry and biological control
- ☐ Current loop schematic identifies expansion tank, heat exchangers, dosing points, side-stream filter, dead legs, drain and vent points, and sampling points
- ☐ Program identifies metals present (steel, copper, aluminum, stainless steel, mixed metallurgy) because these affect corrosion risk

### Risk Identification
- ☐ Program recognizes that closed loops have lower external contamination and aerosol exposure than cooling towers, but can suffer oxygen ingress, nutrient introduction, stagnant sections, biofilm growth, and microbiologically influenced corrosion
- ☐ System areas prone to low flow, temporary isolation, poor venting, or repeated make-up water additions are identified
- ☐ Commissioning, refill, repair, and partial drain-down events are treated as contamination risks

### Control Measures and Limits
- ☐ Required inhibitor program is specified and matches system metallurgy
- ☐ Make-up water control is defined, because unexpected make-up can signal leaks and oxygen entry
- ☐ Air elimination and pressurization controls are functioning
- ☐ Control limits are written for pH, conductivity, inhibitor concentration, dissolved oxygen, and suspended solids or turbidity
- ☐ Site has defined trigger for microbiological investigation, such as odor, slime, falling heat-transfer performance, under-deposit corrosion findings, or laboratory counts above action level

### Monitoring
- ☐ Routine chemistry testing is documented at defined intervals
- ☐ Inhibitor residual or concentration is trended
- ☐ Corrosion monitoring is performed using coupon data or approved equivalent method
- ☐ Microbial assessment is included when risk factors exist or when the loop has history of fouling or corrosion
- ☐ Sampling method avoids false results caused by poorly selected sample points or stagnant sample ports

### Physical Condition and Performance
- ☐ No visible slime, black deposits, or foul odor at strainers, vents, low points, or removed sections
- ☐ Strainers and filters are inspected and findings recorded
- ☐ Heat transfer performance and pressure drop are reviewed for signs of fouling
- ☐ Repair history is reviewed for recurring leaks, air ingress, or rapid loss of inhibitor

### Corrective Action
- ☐ Program defines response to low inhibitor, high oxygen entry, high corrosion rate, microbial growth findings, or evidence of fouling
- ☐ Corrective action may include leak repair, flushing, cleaning, inhibitor rebalance, targeted biocide treatment, and increased follow-up sampling
- ☐ Records show the system returned to control after intervention

### Verification and Validation
- ☐ Review confirms monitoring occurred at required frequency
- ☐ Trend review shows stable chemistry and acceptable corrosion performance over time
- ☐ Post-correction data confirm that biofilm, corrosion, or water quality indicators improved
- ☐ Program is reviewed after major repairs, repeated contamination, or seasonal operational changes

## Key Takeaways

A strong biological program answers three audit questions clearly: Did the site choose the right controls for the system? Did the site monitor them often enough and record them correctly? When control was lost, did the site respond quickly and prove recovery? Programs fail audits when they add chemicals without monitoring results, skip corrective action when limits are exceeded, or lack documentation proving the system returned to control. For open-loop systems, focus on daily disinfectant residual monitoring with documented response within 24 hours of excursions. For closed-loop systems, establish quarterly inhibitor testing with annual corrosion assessment and immediate investigation of any make-up water increases above baseline.

# What is the difference between planktonic bacteria and biofilm?

## Executive Summary

Planktonic bacteria float freely as individual cells in water, while biofilm bacteria attach to surfaces and create protective communities embedded in a self-produced matrix. Biofilms resist disinfectants and environmental stress far better than their free-floating counterparts.

## Overview

A single bacterium can exist in two dramatically different states that determine whether your water treatment will succeed or fail. Biofilms can be 1,000 times more resistant to chlorine than planktonic bacteria, turning what should be a simple disinfection process into a persistent contamination problem.

## Physical State and Location

**Planktonic bacteria** exist as free-floating individual cells suspended in water or liquid. These cells remain motile or simply drift with water currents, staying directly exposed to their surrounding environment. You find them distributed throughout the water column as single cells or small clusters with no protective matrix around them.

**Biofilm bacteria** attach themselves to surfaces like pipes, tanks, medical devices, or rocks. Once attached, they become encased in extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and form structured communities called microcolonies. This surface-associated lifestyle creates a completely different biological reality.

## Structural Organization

Planktonic cells maintain simple organization as single cells or small clusters without any protective matrix. Each cell operates independently and remains vulnerable to external conditions.

Biofilm communities develop complex, layered structures. The protective matrix surrounding these communities contains polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and extracellular DNA. This matrix creates distinct microenvironments within the biofilm, allowing different bacterial populations to thrive at various depths and locations.

## Resistance and Survival Capabilities

Planktonic bacteria show greater susceptibility to disinfectants, antibiotics, and environmental stress including temperature changes and drying. Their direct exposure to the environment makes them easier targets for water treatment processes.

Biofilm bacteria demonstrate remarkable resistance due to four key factors. The physical barrier of the matrix blocks disinfectant penetration. Inner cells within the biofilm reduce their metabolic activity, making them less susceptible to treatments that target actively growing bacteria. The community structure enables gene exchange, potentially spreading resistance traits. Limited disinfectant penetration means that even when outer layers die, inner bacteria survive to repopulate the biofilm.

## Growth and Development Patterns

Planktonic bacteria replicate rapidly when conditions favor growth, but they remain vulnerable throughout their lifecycle.

Biofilm development follows distinct stages. Initial attachment occurs when planktonic cells first contact a surface. Irreversible attachment follows as cells produce adhesive substances. Maturation creates the complex community structure with its protective matrix. Dispersion releases planktonic cells back into the water, spreading contamination to new locations.

**What it measures:** The relationship between biofilm resistance and disinfectant effectiveness

Formula:
```
Biofilm resistance factor = 10 to 1,000 × planktonic resistance
```

**Example:** If planktonic *E. coli* dies after 30 seconds of chlorine contact, the same bacteria in biofilm may survive 30,000 seconds (8+ hours) of identical chlorine exposure.

## Water System Implications

Standard water sampling typically detects planktonic cells, which means biofilm contamination often goes unnoticed until problems become severe. This creates a false sense of security about water quality.

Biofilms act as reservoirs for pathogens like *Legionella*, continuously releasing contaminated planktonic cells into the water supply. They cause pipe corrosion, system fouling, and increased disinfectant demand. The matrix protects bacteria from normal chlorine levels, requiring mechanical removal combined with enhanced chemical treatment.

Biofilms also create localized chemistry changes. The matrix can concentrate nutrients, create anaerobic zones in otherwise oxygenated water, and alter pH levels. These microenvironments support bacterial populations that couldn't survive as planktonic cells in the bulk water.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor for biofilm indicators monthly through ATP testing or biofilm sampling, maintain chlorine residuals above 0.5 mg/L throughout distribution systems, and consider UV disinfection or chlorine dioxide for biofilm control when standard chlorination fails to maintain microbial quality.

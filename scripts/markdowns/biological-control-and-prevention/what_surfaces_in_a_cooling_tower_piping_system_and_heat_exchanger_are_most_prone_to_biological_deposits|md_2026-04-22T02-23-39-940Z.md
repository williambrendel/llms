# What surfaces in a cooling tower, piping system, and heat exchanger are most prone to biological deposits?

## Executive Summary

Biological deposits concentrate where water moves slowly, sits stagnant, or encounters rough surfaces that shelter microbes from cleaning forces. The highest-risk zones are cooling tower basins and fill media, dead legs and strainers in piping, and heat exchanger inlet regions.

## Overview

A single cooling tower basin can harbor over 100 times more biofilm in its corners than on its main circulation surfaces. This dramatic difference reveals how surface geometry and flow patterns determine where biological deposits take hold. The same principles that make certain surfaces vulnerable in cooling towers also apply to piping systems and heat exchangers.

## Cooling Tower Surfaces Most Prone to Biological Deposits

### Cold Water Basin and Sump

The basin collects everything that gravity and poor mixing deliver. Solids settle and accumulate, creating a conditioning layer for biofilm. Low shear in corners and near walls promotes attachment, while water age increases in poorly mixed zones.

Basin corners and wall-to-floor transitions present the highest risk. Areas under and around basin strainers and screens trap debris and create perfect biofilm nurseries. Floating debris mats and overflow regions also shelter microbial communities from circulation forces.

### Fill Media

Fill media offers the perfect storm for biological growth: massive surface area, warm oxygen-rich water films, and captured nutrients. Very high surface area provides abundant attachment sites while warm, oxygen-rich thin water films support rapid growth.

The leading edges where water first contacts fill media see the heaviest biological loading. Areas with uneven distribution create particular problems—dry spots adjacent to wet spots experience intermittent wetting that promotes slime and algae formation.

### Spray Distribution System

Hot water basins, spray nozzles, headers, and troughs experience intermittent wetting and wet-dry cycling that promotes slime and algae. Nozzle throats and orifices accumulate biofilm and silt, reducing distribution quality and creating self-reinforcing deposit zones.

Trough edges and under-lip surfaces remain consistently moist but experience low shear, making them prime real estate for biological communities.

### Drift Eliminators and Louvers

These air-water interface components face constant exposure to humid air plus droplets. Their often complex geometry creates low-shear zones that resist cleaning. Downstream faces where droplets impinge and crevices between overlapping sections accumulate the most biological material.

## Piping System Surfaces Most Prone to Biological Deposits

### Low-Velocity and Low-Turbulence Zones

Lower shear allows initial attachment and matrix buildup while solids settle and remain, feeding deposit formation. Oversized piping relative to flow and bypass lines with minimal flow create these problematic conditions.

### Dead Legs and Stagnant Branches

These areas develop long water age and reduced disinfectant delivery. Once seeded, biological communities establish rapidly and persist. Capped tees, unused equipment connections, and infrequently used lines represent the highest-risk locations.

### Strainers, Screens, and Filters

These components capture organics and solids that become biofilm substrate. Crevices and mesh provide shelter from shear forces. Pump suction strainers and side-stream filtration housings accumulate biological deposits most rapidly when poorly maintained.

### High-Deposit Geometry Features

Elbows, reducers, expansion joints, and valve bodies create local turbulence and recirculation zones that trap solids. Heat exchanger inlet piping immediately upstream and downstream experiences similar deposit accumulation.

## Heat Exchanger Surfaces Most Prone to Biological Deposits

### Water-Side Inlet Region

The first-contact zone sees the highest solids loading, with deposits forming early at the entrance. Boundary layer development and surface conditioning start here, making tube sheet inlet sides in shell-and-tube exchangers and first few plate channels in plate-and-frame units particularly vulnerable.

### Low-Velocity Channels and Low-Shear Areas

Reduced shear promotes attachment and retention in large-diameter tubes at low flow and portions of plates with poor distribution or maldistribution. These areas become biological deposit magnets.

### Roughened or Already-Fouled Surfaces

Scale and corrosion products create roughness and protected micro-niches. Under-deposit conditions reduce biocide contact and encourage persistence. Areas with scaling tendency and corrosion tubercles or pitting experience accelerated biological fouling.

### Gaskets and Crevice Regions

In plate heat exchangers, crevices create sheltered zones for biofilm where local chemistry differs from bulk water. These stagnant micro-zones resist treatment and cleaning efforts.

## Practical Field Indicators

Basin walls near corners and waterline show slime formation first, especially under debris or sediment. Strainer baskets and screens displaying slime plus trapped silt provide strong early indicators of biological deposit formation.

Performance declines reveal biological deposit locations through heat exchanger approach temperature increases, often starting at inlet zones, and pressure drop increases indicating channel restriction from deposits.

## Key Takeaways

Inspect basin corners, strainers, and heat exchanger inlet regions weekly for early biological deposit formation. Target areas with water velocity below 3 feet per second and eliminate dead legs longer than 6 pipe diameters. Consider UV disinfection or chlorine dioxide for systems where biological deposits consistently form despite conventional biocide treatment.

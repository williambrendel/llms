# How does the cause and control of excessive total coliforms differ from total viable plate count?

## Executive Summary

Excess total coliforms and excess total viable plate count (TVC/HPC) usually mean different problems, so the investigation and controls differ.

## Overview

A positive total coliform test sends water operators scrambling to find a breach in their distribution system, while an elevated viable plate count might just mean the water has been sitting too long in warm pipes. The same disinfection system can produce water that passes coliform tests but fails plate count standards—or vice versa.

## What Each Test Actually Measures

**Total coliform bacteria** serve as indicator organisms to detect potential problems with treatment adequacy or distribution system integrity—essentially, a pathway for contamination. They don't directly measure overall bacterial biomass in the water.

**Total viable plate count (TVC)**, also called heterotrophic plate count (HPC), provides a broad count of live, culturable heterotrophs including bacteria, yeasts, and molds. This test indicates general microbial conditions and changes in water quality, but it's not specific for fecal contamination.

## Why the Causes Differ

**Excess total coliform** most often indicates a breach or vulnerability rather than general bacterial growth. The typical causes include:

Intrusion pathways create the most common problems: pressure loss and back-siphonage events, cross-connections, backflow prevention failures, and low disinfectant levels in reclaimed water supplies. Contaminated repairs, tie-ins, or main breaks introduce coliforms directly into clean water. Inadequate disinfectant residual fails to reach parts of the system, allowing regrowth and colonization. Storage tanks or distribution issues can also allow coliform survival and regrowth.

Regulatory frameworks treat total coliform positives as triggers for repeat sampling and corrective actions aimed at identifying and fixing distribution system problems.

**Excess TVC/HPC** more often indicates system-wide biological activity rather than a specific breach. The causes include higher nutrient and organic carbon availability, warm temperatures combined with high water age and stagnation, insufficient disinfectant exposure (though not necessarily from an intrusion event), and increased biofilm formation throughout the system.

## Control Strategies: Fixing the Pathway vs. Managing Growth

**Control actions for excessive total coliform** focus on finding and eliminating the contamination pathway. Operators must perform immediate repeat sampling per jurisdictional requirements, including upstream and downstream locations to isolate the problem area. The investigation targets distribution system integrity: recent pressure events, repairs, cross-connections, backflow devices, storage tank defects, and compromised seals, hatches, or vents.

Water systems must restore protective disinfectant residual and flush affected zones as appropriate. Regulatory compliance requires specific corrective actions and documentation.

**Control actions for excessive TVC/HPC** aim to reduce growth conditions and biofilm throughout the system. While operators should verify disinfectant control where used, they must also address water age, temperature, nutrients, and biofilm deposits. Improving flushing and turnover in low-use areas corrects stagnation problems. Regular cleaning and maintenance of strainers, storage tanks, and biofilm-accumulating devices prevents ongoing growth.

## Key Takeaway

Use total coliform results to trigger immediate breach investigations and system integrity repairs, while treating TVC/HPC as a trend metric to monitor and improve general water quality conditions through enhanced flushing, biofilm control, and reduced water age.

# How Do Process Upsets Impact Biological Control?

## Executive Summary

Process upsets reduce the stability and performance of biological control by stressing or damaging the living microbial community that carries out treatment. In water and wastewater systems, this usually causes slower contaminant removal, lower process resilience, more variable effluent quality, and a higher risk of permit noncompliance.

## Overview

The most common upset drivers are sudden changes in flow, organic loading, temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, toxic compounds, and nutrient balance. The main effect is loss of microbial activity and community balance. When that happens, key treatment functions such as carbon removal, nitrification, denitrification, and solids settling can decline quickly. Recovery may be fast for mild upsets, but severe upsets can take days to weeks because biomass must regrow and re-establish a stable population.

## What a Process Upset Is

A process upset is a sudden or sustained change that pushes a biological system away from its normal operating range. In practical terms, it means the microorganisms no longer have the environment they need to perform reliably.

Typical upset causes include:

- Hydraulic shock: sudden high flow or short retention time
- Organic shock: sudden increase in food or biochemical oxygen demand
- Toxic shock: solvents, disinfectants, metals, biocides, or other inhibitors
- Low dissolved oxygen
- pH excursion
- Temperature change
- Nutrient deficiency or imbalance
- Excess solids loss or washout

## How Biological Control Is Affected

Biological control depends on living organisms, mostly bacteria and other microorganisms. These organisms are sensitive to environmental conditions. A process upset can affect them in five main ways.

### Reduced Microbial Activity

When conditions shift outside the normal range, microbes consume substrate more slowly. This reduces treatment efficiency.

Examples include less carbon removal, less ammonia oxidation, slower denitrification, and incomplete breakdown of organics.

### Loss of Sensitive Organisms

Some organisms are more sensitive than others. Nitrifying bacteria are a common example. They grow slowly and are often the first group to fail during toxic exposure, low dissolved oxygen, or cold temperature.

The result is ammonia rises, nitrite may accumulate, and recovery takes longer than for simple carbon removal.

### Change in Microbial Population Balance

An upset can shift the microbial community toward less desirable organisms. Filamentous organisms may increase, causing poor settling. Foaming organisms may become dominant. Storage organisms may be outcompeted. Predators or higher organisms may decline if conditions become unstable.

This leads to worse clarifier performance, higher turbidity or suspended solids, and unstable sludge blanket behavior.

### Biomass Washout or Decay

High flow, toxic conditions, or poor settling can cause biomass loss. If microorganisms are washed out faster than they grow back, biological control weakens.

The result is lower mixed liquor concentration, lower solids retention time, less treatment capacity, and longer recovery period.

### Poor Control Loop Response

Biological systems are slower than chemical systems. Even after operators correct the trigger, the biology may lag. That means instrument readings and process performance may not recover immediately.

This causes delayed stabilization, oscillating control actions, and risk of overcorrection.

## Specific Impacts by Upset Type

### Hydraulic Shock

Hydraulic shock creates shorter contact time, biomass washout, and clarifier overload. The likely outcome is higher suspended solids, reduced removal efficiency, and loss of nitrification if solids age falls too low.

### Organic Shock

Organic shock causes oxygen demand to rise sharply while fast-growing heterotrophs dominate. The likely outcome is dissolved oxygen drops, nitrifiers are suppressed, and effluent biochemical oxygen demand increases.

### Toxic Shock

Toxic shock leads to microbial inhibition or death. The likely outcome is sudden treatment failure, reduced respiration, rising ammonia or chemical oxygen demand, and possible foaming or septic conditions later.

### Low Dissolved Oxygen

Low dissolved oxygen means aerobic organisms lose activity, with nitrification especially affected. The likely outcome is poor oxidation, increased ammonia, and odor risk if anaerobic zones develop unintentionally.

### pH Upset

pH upset disrupts enzyme systems, and free ammonia or free nitrous acid toxicity can worsen depending on conditions. The likely outcome is inhibited growth, reduced nitrification and denitrification, and possible biomass stress or die-off.

### Temperature Change

Temperature change shifts growth rates and reaction rates. The likely outcome is slower treatment in cold conditions, more oxygen stress in warm conditions, and seasonal instability if control is not adjusted.

## Operational Signs of Biological Upset

Common warning signs include rising effluent ammonia, rising effluent biochemical oxygen demand or chemical oxygen demand, lower dissolved oxygen than normal, sudden drop in oxygen uptake or respiration, poor settling or rising sludge volume index, foaming, odors, or color change, increased turbidity or suspended solids, and loss of nitrification or incomplete denitrification.

## Recovery Pattern

Recovery depends on the type and severity of upset. Mild upsets take hours to a few days. Moderate upsets take several days. Severe upsets with biomass loss or nitrifier loss take days to weeks.

Faster recovery usually occurs when the upset source is removed quickly, biomass inventory is preserved, aeration and solids age are stabilized, and equalization or source control is available.

## Key Takeaways

Biological control works best under stable operating conditions. Monitor dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature, ammonia, alkalinity, and solids daily to detect upsets early. Identify and isolate toxic or inhibitory discharges before they reach the biological system. Maintain adequate sludge age (typically 5-15 days for activated sludge) and biomass inventory to provide process resilience. Develop an upset response plan with specific actions for aeration adjustment, waste activated sludge control, equalization tank use, and source control measures.

# How Do Biological Control Needs Change by Season in Data Center Cooling Towers?

## Executive Summary

Biological control in data center cooling towers requires seasonal adjustment of both hypobromous acid biocide doses (ranging from 3 ppm in winter to 7 ppm in summer) and biodispersant applications (10-50 ppm), with treatment frequency increasing from 1-2 times weekly in winter to 3+ times weekly during peak summer conditions.

## Overview

Summer cooling tower operations can see bacterial populations spike 100-fold compared to winter levels, yet most facilities run the same biocide program year-round. This seasonal mismatch explains why Legionella outbreaks cluster in warmer months despite continuous chemical treatment. The challenge isn't just higher temperatures—it's the convergence of peak thermal loads, maximum nutrient availability, and reduced chemical effectiveness that creates perfect conditions for biofilm-protected microbial communities.

## Seasonal Biological Control Challenges

Winter operations present deceptively complex control requirements. Cold water suppresses most biological activity, but biofilms persist and can consolidate in low-flow zones. Intermittent system operation creates stagnant areas where slow-growing organisms establish protective matrices. The primary risk becomes underdosing—operators often reduce biocide applications when they should maintain baseline protection.

Spring brings the year's steepest biological challenge curve. Rising temperatures trigger dormant microbial populations while pollen, debris, and organic matter provide abundant nutrients. System ramp-up from winter operations disturbs settled biofilms, releasing protected organisms into circulation. Bacterial and algal growth potential increases exponentially, often overwhelming winter-level treatment programs.

Summer represents peak biological stress. Continuous operation at maximum thermal loads creates optimal growth conditions for problematic organisms like *Pseudomonas* species and *Legionella*. Warm water accelerates all biological processes while reducing biocide contact time and effectiveness. This season demands the most aggressive treatment protocols and closest monitoring.

Fall operations deal with different but significant challenges. Decaying organic matter from leaves and plant debris increases system nutrient loads while creating oxygen-depleted zones that favor anaerobic bacteria. Biofilms consolidate as temperatures cool, potentially harboring sulfate-reducing bacteria that cause corrosion and odor problems.

| **Season** | **Key Challenges** | **Biological Trends** | **Operational Impacts** |
|------------|-------------------|----------------------|------------------------|
| **Winter** | Cold water, intermittent operation, bypass modes | Bioactivity suppressed but not eliminated; slow-growing biofilms may persist | Risk of underdosing biocides, stagnant zones promote low-level growth |
| **Spring** | Rising temps, pollen load, debris, system ramp-up | Sharp increase in bacterial and algal growth potential | Biocide demand increases, filters foul, nutrients spike |
| **Summer** | Max thermal load, continuous operation, warm water | Peak bacterial (e.g. *Pseudomonas*) and algae growth, potential for Legionella | Requires aggressive biocide and dispersant dosing; maximum system stress |
| **Fall** | Decaying organics (leaves, plant matter), cooling temps | Biofilm consolidation, possible SRB bloom under anaerobic deposits | Risk of slime formation, increased organic load |

## Hypobromous Acid Control Strategy

Hypobromous acid (HOBr) delivers fast-acting, broad-spectrum oxidizing power against bacteria, algae, fungi, and protozoa. Its key advantage over hypochlorous acid lies in superior performance at the higher pH levels typical of cooling tower water (7.5-8.5). The chemistry favors HOBr formation in these conditions, maintaining biocidal activity where chlorine-based systems lose effectiveness.

Effective HOBr application relies on slug dosing rather than continuous feed. Slug doses of 3-7 ppm maintained for 2-hour contact times, applied multiple times per week, penetrate biofilms and achieve kill rates that continuous low-level dosing cannot match. Continual low feed proves less effective, especially under high biological loads or warm conditions where rapid consumption depletes residuals before achieving adequate contact.

Winter effectiveness remains high because low biomass presents minimal resistance to oxidant penetration. However, operators must guard against overcontrol and monitor for increased corrosion potential in cold water systems. Spring operations require increased slug frequency as biomass and organic loads rise, with careful monitoring for rapid oxidant demand that signals biofilm development.

Summer presents the greatest HOBr challenges. High bioactivity can overwhelm mild treatment regimens, requiring both higher doses and increased frequency—often 3 or more applications weekly. Maintaining adequate contact time becomes critical as warm water accelerates chemical reactions and biological processes. Fall operations need dose adjustments based on organic debris loads, using oxidant demand tests to validate feed levels.

| **Season** | **Effectiveness** | **Biomass Control Needs** | **Operational Notes** |
|------------|------------------|---------------------------|----------------------|
| Winter | High; low biomass = easier control | Low dosing required | Risk: overcontrol; monitor corrosion potential |
| Spring | Moderate; increasing biomass and organic load | Slug dose frequency must increase | Monitor for biofilm development and rapid demand |
| Summer | Challenging; high bioactivity overwhelms mild regimens | High-dose slug + increased frequency (≥3x/week) | Maintain contact time, test residuals, prevent Legionella |
| Fall | Moderate; high organic debris adds oxidant demand | Adjust dose based on turbidity and TOC | Use oxidant demand tests to validate feed levels |

## Biodispersant Strategy and Seasonal Application

Biodispersants serve a fundamentally different role than biocides—they don't kill microorganisms but instead break up biofilms, expose sessile organisms to oxidants, and prevent attachment to surfaces. This function becomes critical for making biocides like HOBr effective against mucoid or pigmented bacteria, algae, and slime-forming organisms that create protective matrices.

Nonionic and amphoteric biodispersants typically apply at 10-50 ppm depending on system load and seasonal requirements. The key to effectiveness lies in timing—applying dispersants 10-30 minutes before oxidizing biocides allows maximum exposure of protected organisms.

Winter dispersant needs remain moderate, focusing on preventing biofilm consolidation in low-flow or stagnant zones. Weekly pulse applications at low doses maintain film disruption without excessive chemical consumption. Spring applications become critical for removing accumulated pollen and fungal spores while creating clean surfaces for biocide action.

Summer represents peak dispersant importance. High biological activity creates rapid biofilm formation that can shield organisms from even aggressive biocide dosing. Higher dispersant doses before each biocide slug, plus additional applications during heat waves, maintain system accessibility. Programs without adequate dispersant support often fail despite high biocide dosing.

Fall dispersant applications focus on removing decaying organic matter that shelters bacteria. Pairing dispersant treatment with enhanced filtration helps control suspended solids that would otherwise provide attachment sites and nutrient sources.

| **Season** | **Dispersant Need** | **Purpose** | **Best Practices** |
|------------|-------------------|-------------|-------------------|
| Winter | Moderate | Prevent biofilm consolidation in low-flow or stagnant zones | Pulse at low doses weekly to maintain film disruption |
| Spring | High | Remove pollen/fungal spores and initiate clean surface for biocide | Apply before/with first seasonal HOBr slug |
| Summer | Critical | Maintain biocide efficacy, prevent dead zones and biomass layering | Use higher dose before each slug and during peak heatwaves |
| Fall | High | Remove decaying organic matter that shelters bacteria | Pair with filtration to control suspended solids load |

## Seasonal Dosage Protocols

Effective seasonal adjustment requires coordinated changes in both biocide and dispersant dosing, with frequency adjustments that match biological activity patterns. Winter protocols use conservative 3 ppm HOBr slugs with 10-15 ppm dispersant applications 1-2 times weekly. This maintains baseline protection without excessive chemical consumption during low-activity periods.

Spring operations step up to 4-5 ppm HOBr slugs with 20-30 ppm dispersant doses applied 2-3 times weekly. This increased intensity matches rising biological activity while establishing clean surfaces before peak summer conditions. Summer protocols require maximum intensity—5-7 ppm HOBr slugs with 30-50 ppm dispersant applications 3 or more times weekly.

Fall protocols moderate to 4-5 ppm HOBr with 20-30 ppm dispersant applied 2-3 times weekly, adjusting for organic debris loads while preparing systems for winter operation.

| **Season** | **Hypobromous Acid Slug Dose** | **Biodispersant Dose** | **Frequency (per week)** |
|------------|--------------------------------|------------------------|-------------------------|
| Winter | 3 ppm (low-end) | 10–15 ppm | 1–2 |
| Spring | 4–5 ppm | 20–30 ppm | 2–3 |
| Summer | 5–7 ppm | 30–50 ppm | 3+ |
| Fall | 4–5 ppm | 20–30 ppm | 2–3 |

## Implementation and Monitoring

Robust year-round biological control requires systematic seasonal adjustment based on measurable parameters rather than calendar dates alone. Water temperature, total viable plate counts, and biofilm-forming organism populations provide better triggers for treatment intensity changes than fixed seasonal schedules.

Monthly testing should include total viable counts, pigmented and mucoid bacteria enumeration, and biofilm-former identification. These indicators predict treatment needs more accurately than general bacterial counts. Monitoring for rebound colonies post-dosing reveals insufficient contact time or biofilm-protected zones requiring program adjustment.

Reclaimed water systems need special consideration, often requiring chlorine dioxide rotation or periodic glutaraldehyde applications to counter oxidant resistance buildup. Scheduling major cleanouts or offline chemical cleaning during transitional seasons (spring and fall) maximizes effectiveness while minimizing operational disruption.

The most critical monitoring focuses on oxidant residuals measured post-dose rather than at injection points. This confirms actual system exposure and reveals rapid consumption that indicates inadequate treatment intensity. Adjusting programs based on biofilm potential indicators—total viable plate counts, heterotrophic plate counts, turbidity, and organic carbon—provides responsive control that matches actual biological conditions rather than assumed seasonal patterns.

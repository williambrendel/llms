# How Does the Cause and Control of Iron Bacteria Differ from Total Viable Plate Count in Cooling Towers?

## Executive Summary

Iron bacteria and total viable plate count represent fundamentally different water quality problems requiring distinct control strategies—iron bacteria indicate iron-driven slime and corrosion cycles that demand physical deposit removal and iron source control, while high total viable counts signal broad microbiological control failures requiring disinfectant and solids management.

## Overview

When iron bacteria colonize a cooling tower, simply cranking up the chlorine often makes the problem worse by creating more iron precipitates and deposits. The two most common bacterial measurements in cooling systems—total viable plate count (TVC/HPC) and iron-related bacteria (IRB)—actually diagnose completely different underlying problems, even though both involve "too many bacteria."

## What Each Test Actually Measures

**Total Viable Plate Count (TVC/HPC)** provides a non-specific estimate of live, culturable heterotrophs in the bulk water. This test serves as a general water quality and biological condition indicator. A significant increase in TVC suggests a potential water quality problem affecting the entire system.

**Iron bacteria (IRB)** represents a functional group that uses iron chemistry, often oxidizing ferrous iron and forming rust-colored slime. These bacteria associate directly with biofouling and microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) through deposits and slime formation on surfaces.

## How the Underlying Causes Differ

**High TVC/HPC** typically stems from broad system conditions that affect overall microbiological control. These include insufficient disinfectant exposure or residual control, high nutrients and organics combined with warm conditions, poor solids and deposit control, and water stagnation. For cooling towers specifically, controlling scale, corrosion, and sediment through system cleaning while maintaining disinfectant residual becomes critical for managing microbial growth.

**Excessive iron bacteria** indicates more specific conditions that create and sustain iron-driven biological problems. IRB requires available iron from makeup water, corrosion products, or iron-bearing source water. These bacteria need surfaces and deposits where their slime can attach and persist. Most problematically, they create corrosion and under-deposit conditions that generate a self-reinforcing cycle: corrosion produces iron, which feeds IRB slime formation, which creates more deposits and additional corrosion sites.

Iron bacteria frequently enter through makeup water or contamination and become extremely difficult to remove once attached to surfaces. Chlorination can actually worsen iron bacteria problems by oxidizing ferrous iron to ferric iron, creating precipitated iron deposits when the system has high iron demand.

## The Key Operational Difference

High **TVC/HPC** signals that "overall microbial load in bulk water is up"—a system-wide microbiological control issue.

High **iron bacteria** indicates an "iron-driven slime and deposit problem with heightened corrosion and biofouling implications"—sometimes with only modest changes in total HPC numbers.

## Control Strategies for Each Problem

**When TVC/HPC is excessive**, the goal becomes restoring general microbiological control across the entire system. Operators should verify disinfectant feed and control systems, then confirm residual levels at representative points throughout the system, not just at a single monitoring location. Improving solids and deposit control through proper blowdown rates, filtration, basin cleanliness, and maintaining strainers and fill in good condition addresses the physical environment supporting microbial growth. Reducing stagnation and water age through better operating practices, proper cell isolation, and attention to low-flow areas eliminates microbial refugia. Use HPC and TVC measurements as trend indicators to verify that interventions are working.

**When iron bacteria levels are excessive**, the goal shifts to breaking the iron-slime-deposit cycle that sustains these problematic organisms. Physical removal of deposits and slime from basins, sumps, strainers, fill, and low-flow zones must be the first priority, followed by ensuring that loosened solids are actually removed through filtration and blowdown—because IRB are fundamentally deposit-associated problems.

Identifying and reducing iron sources requires checking makeup water iron contribution and examining corrosion sources to evaluate corrosion control program effectiveness. Increasing oxidizing disinfectant without managing iron sources can create more ferric precipitate and deposits, worsening the underlying problem.

Targeting corrosion and under-deposit conditions means treating IRB as a corrosion and biofouling issue, not simply "high bacteria." Confirming pathway control involves verifying makeup water protections and housekeeping practices to prevent debris ingress, since IRB can enter from makeup water and contamination sources.

## Applications Beyond Cooling Towers

The same diagnostic logic applies to evaporative condensers, indirect evaporative coolers, and similar aerosol-generating systems. HPC increases typically indicate broad loss of microbial control requiring system-wide interventions. IRB findings point specifically to iron availability combined with deposit niches, often tied to corrosion and solids issues, requiring the same targeted corrective priorities.

## Key Takeaway

High **HPC** calls for adjusting general microbial control through disinfectant optimization, solids management, and hydraulic improvements. High **iron bacteria** demands prioritizing physical deposit removal and iron source control—simply increasing oxidizer may worsen iron deposition unless iron and solids removal are addressed first. Monitor HPC weekly and IRB monthly, target HPC below 10,000 CFU/mL and IRB below 1,000 CFU/mL, and consider non-oxidizing biocides or specialized iron sequestrants when conventional chlorination fails to control IRB.

# When should a closed loop system be disinfected?

## Executive Summary

Closed loop systems require disinfection at three critical points: during commissioning and after major maintenance that opens the system, when monitoring reveals unacceptable bacteria or biofilm levels, and following contamination events or performance upsets that suggest loss of microbial control.

## Overview

Most closed loop systems run for months without needing disinfection because they operate with minimal water loss and limited oxygen exposure. However, when bacteria counts spike beyond acceptable limits, a single biocide dose often fails to restore control — especially if biofilm has already established on system surfaces. The difference between routine maintenance and emergency remediation often comes down to catching problems early through proper monitoring.

## Commissioning and Major Rework Events

New systems and those undergoing significant maintenance present the highest contamination risk. During initial startup, disinfection forms part of pre-commission flushing and cleaning procedures. Automated dosing systems can apply biocides along with other water treatment chemicals to the initial fill water.

Major maintenance that drains or opens the system introduces dirt, debris, and microorganisms. These events require treatment as partial recommissioning: flush the system, clean as needed, then apply biocide according to the established program and product labels.

## When Monitoring Shows Loss of Control

Regular monitoring reveals when microbial control breaks down before serious problems develop. Apply shock dose biocides when bacteria counts or biofilm indicators exceed acceptable thresholds. Closed system guidance explicitly recommends shock dosing when bacteria or biofilm levels become unacceptable.

Heavy biofilm contamination requires more aggressive intervention than a single biocide application. Established biofilm protects embedded bacteria and makes cleanup significantly more difficult than preventing growth in the first place.

## Fill Water and Make-up Water Risks

High microbiological activity in fill water can overwhelm system defenses from the start. Pre-treatment becomes necessary when source water contains high concentrations of problematic bacteria or other undesirable microbiological activity.

**What it measures:** The microbiological load introduced with new water additions

Example: If fill water contains 10,000 CFU/mL of bacteria, even a 1% makeup rate continuously seeds the system with fresh contamination.

## Contamination and Performance Upset Events

Several events justify immediate disinfection regardless of routine schedules. Cross-connections or intrusion events that introduce non-system water, dirt, or process fluids require prompt biocidal treatment.

Extended shutdowns create stagnation conditions that support microbial growth, especially at temperatures between 20-45°C. Disinfect on restart if monitoring or visual inspection suggests growth occurred during the shutdown period.

Recurring blockages in strainers or deposits consistent with biological material indicate established biofilm. Use cleaning plus biocide dosing rather than biocide alone, since biofilm removal requires mechanical action combined with chemical treatment.

## After Remedial Cleaning Work

Disinfection serves as the final verification step following remedial flushing and cleaning operations. Documentation should include chemicals used, concentrations, contact times, and final dosage of inhibitors and biocides. Complete the process with sampling that matches the system's water quality objectives.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor bacteria and biofilm indicators monthly in active systems, apply shock doses immediately when levels exceed 1,000 CFU/mL or biofilm becomes visible, and consider UV sterilization or ozone treatment for fill water sources with persistent microbiological problems.

# What are emergency response plans (internal and external) and how do they address biological outbreaks?

## Executive Summary

Emergency response plans split into two coordinated parts: internal plans control operations within your facility, while external plans manage coordination with health agencies, regulators, and the public during biological outbreaks.

## Overview

When a Legionella outbreak hits a hospital's cooling towers, the difference between containment and catastrophe often comes down to two distinct but coordinated response plans. Internal plans can shut down suspect systems within hours, while external plans ensure health departments investigate properly and the public receives accurate guidance. Most organizations discover too late that having just one "emergency plan" leaves critical gaps in either operational control or public coordination.

## Internal Emergency Response Plans

An internal emergency response plan serves as the organization's operational playbook during a crisis. It assigns specific roles, defines decision triggers, and outlines immediate protective actions that staff can execute without waiting for outside approval.

For biological outbreaks, internal plans focus on rapid source control and exposure prevention. In building water systems, this includes restricting aerosol-generating devices, taking outlets out of service, changing water use instructions, increasing monitoring, flushing, disinfection, and preserving records for investigation.

The plan establishes clear activation triggers such as confirmed illness, abnormal microbial test results, multiple related complaints, visible biofilm, loss of disinfectant control, or contamination of critical areas. Once activated, it defines immediate protective actions, assigns investigation responsibilities, and sets decision points for escalation.

### Healthcare Facility Internal Plan Example

A hospital's internal plan demonstrates these principles in practice:

**Plan purpose:** Protect patients, staff, and visitors by rapidly controlling the suspected source, reducing exposure, supporting investigation, and restoring safe operation.

**Activation triggers:** One healthcare-associated case with plausible water exposure, two or more linked cases, laboratory evidence of abnormal water colonization, sudden loss of water management program control, or public health notification.

**Incident leadership:** The facilities director leads the response, infection prevention provides clinical liaison, engineering manages system actions, safety handles worker protection, and administration approves external notifications and resource allocation.

**Immediate actions:** Restrict use of suspected aerosol-generating fixtures, place point-of-use controls where needed, suspend nonessential exposure-prone activities, review water system schematics, identify high-risk patient areas, begin internal event logging, and preserve recent monitoring records.

**Technical control actions:** Increase temperature or disinfectant control within program limits, perform targeted flushing if it supports outbreak strategy, isolate dead legs or problem zones, inspect storage and fixtures, and arrange environmental sampling through qualified personnel.

**Recovery criteria:** Complete control measures, review follow-up data, protect high-risk areas, document leadership approval, and issue corrective action report with lessons learned and program revisions.

## External Emergency Response Plans

External emergency response plans coordinate with parties outside the organization. They define when and how to notify public health agencies, regulators, emergency management, laboratories, customers, medical providers, and the public.

These plans establish external roles for incident command, media communication, technical support, mutual aid, and recovery oversight. During biological outbreaks, external plans address wider public protection through prompt reporting, coordinated investigation, risk communication, laboratory support, and decisions about public notices or service restrictions.

### Water Utility External Plan Example

A water utility's external plan illustrates coordination principles:

**Plan purpose:** Protect public health through coordinated notification, investigation, containment, risk communication, alternate water arrangements if needed, and distribution system recovery.

**Activation triggers:** Confirmed or suspected contamination from monitoring, customer illness patterns, laboratory notification, treatment failure, unexplained disinfectant loss, or healthcare agency reports.

**External coordination structure:** Notify local and state public health departments, drinking water regulators, emergency management agencies, certified laboratories, hospitals, mutual aid partners, and major institutional customers. Establish single public information and operations leads.

**Public protection actions:** Issue hazard-appropriate public instructions such as boil-water or do-not-use directions based on incident assessment and regulator advice, provide alternative water if required, notify critical users directly, and publish regular status updates.

**Technical response actions:** Secure affected zones, adjust system operations, perform site characterization and sampling, assess treatment performance, identify entry points, coordinate laboratory analysis, and develop remediation plans.

**Recovery and closeout:** Lift public restrictions only after meeting technical and public health criteria, document the incident, complete after-action review, update emergency response plans, and revise training and exercises.

## Coordinated Response to Biological Outbreaks

Both plans work together in a coordinated sequence during biological outbreaks:

**First,** activation triggers alert responders to potential problems requiring immediate attention.

**Second,** immediate protective actions reduce ongoing exposure and protect people at risk.

**Third,** investigation responsibilities divide between internal operational assessment and external epidemiological investigation.

**Fourth,** escalation decisions determine when to involve health authorities or issue public instructions.

**Fifth,** recovery criteria ensure both operational restoration and public health protection before returning to normal operations.

The internal plan answers "What do we do inside our own walls right now?" while the external plan answers "Who outside must be engaged, informed, authorized, or coordinated so the outbreak is controlled beyond our walls?"

## Key Takeaways

Organizations need both internal and external emergency response plans to handle biological outbreaks effectively. Internal plans should activate within the first operational period (typically 12 hours) and include specific technical control measures for your water systems. External plans should establish coordination protocols with local health departments and regulators before incidents occur, not during them. Test both plans through annual exercises that simulate realistic biological outbreak scenarios, and update procedures based on lessons learned from each exercise or actual incident.

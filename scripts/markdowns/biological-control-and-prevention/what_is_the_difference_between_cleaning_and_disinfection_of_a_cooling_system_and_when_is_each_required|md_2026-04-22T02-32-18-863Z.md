# What is the difference between cleaning and disinfection of a cooling system, and when is each required?

## Executive Summary

Cleaning physically removes biofilm, scale, sludge, and debris that shelter organisms and consume treatment chemicals, while disinfection applies biocides at specific concentrations and contact times to kill microorganisms in the water and on surfaces.

## Overview

A cooling tower can fail microbiologically in two distinct ways that require different responses. Deposits like biofilm and scale create protected habitats where organisms thrive despite chemical treatment, consuming biocide before it reaches its targets. Even the strongest disinfection protocols underperform when these deposits remain intact, which explains why cleaning and disinfection work as complementary controls rather than interchangeable options.

## Understanding the Two Controls

Cleaning removes contamination through physical or mechanical action. This includes biofilm, scale, debris, rust, sludge, and algae that accumulate on cooling system surfaces. The process targets the physical environment where microorganisms establish themselves.

Disinfection uses one or more biocides at defined concentrations, under specific conditions, for established contact periods. The goal is killing or inactivating pathogenic organisms present in the circulating water and on system surfaces.

NYC health guidance explicitly distinguishes these approaches: cleaning involves mechanical removal of deposits, while disinfection requires biocides applied under defined conditions and timeframes. The CDC emphasizes that scale, corrosion, and sediment controls alongside system cleaning are critical for cooling tower operations and prevention.

## Why the Distinction Matters

Deposits create protected niches where organisms shelter from chemical treatment. Biofilm and sludge act as physical barriers that shield microorganisms while simultaneously increasing the chemical demand of the system. This double effect means even aggressive biocide dosing can fail when deposits remain in place.

Disinfection addresses the organisms themselves through biocide exposure in circulating water and on surfaces. Effectiveness depends on achieving proper concentration, ensuring adequate mixing, and maintaining sufficient contact time at the target dose.

## When Cleaning is Required

Cleaning becomes necessary when visible or measurable deposits accumulate that can support microbial growth. This includes biofilm formation, scale buildup, and accumulation of debris, sludge, rust, or algae on system surfaces.

Systems experiencing recurrent biological excursions or unstable disinfectant residuals often require cleaning to restore reliable microbial control. The CDC identifies sediment and biofilm controls along with system cleaning as critical components of effective cooling tower operations.

## When Disinfection is Required

Disinfection is required as corrective action when evaluation or sampling indicates microbial concentrations exceeding control limits. NYC guidance specifies that cleaning, disinfection, or both may be required depending on findings and the facility's response plan.

High-risk events or loss of control also trigger disinfection requirements. Startup after shutdown or extended idling typically requires disinfection, often combined with cleaning. Mechanical disruption or contamination events similarly demand both cleaning and disinfection to restore system control.

## Sequencing for Maximum Effectiveness

When deposits are present, cleaning must occur before or concurrent with disinfection. Cleaning removes the material that protects organisms and consumes biocide, directly improving disinfection effectiveness.

**What it measures:** The relationship between deposit removal and biocide performance

**Example:** A cooling tower with heavy biofilm buildup may consume 50% more biocide to achieve the same residual compared to a clean system, while still harboring protected organisms within the biofilm matrix.

CDC guidance supports this integrated approach by emphasizing cleaning alongside chemical control as essential elements of comprehensive cooling tower management.

## Key Takeaways

Facilities should inspect cooling systems monthly for visible deposits and clean when biofilm, scale, or debris accumulation is evident. Disinfection should target measurable residual levels of 1-2 ppm free chlorine or equivalent biocide, maintained for minimum contact times specified in the water management plan. When both cleaning and disinfection are needed, mechanical cleaning followed by chemical disinfection provides the most reliable microbial control restoration.

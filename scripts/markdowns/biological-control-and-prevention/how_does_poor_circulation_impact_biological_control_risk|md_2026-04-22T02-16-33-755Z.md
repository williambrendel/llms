# How does poor circulation impact biological control risk?

## Executive Summary

Poor circulation increases biological control risk by creating low-flow and stagnant zones where disinfectant or biocide delivery is weak, contact time at an effective concentration is inconsistent, and sediments and biofilm can accumulate. These zones become protected growth reservoirs that can reseed the rest of the system, leading to recurring out-of-limit biological results even when bulk water looks acceptable.

## Overview

A cooling tower with perfect bulk water chemistry can still fail biological limits because of what happens in the corners nobody talks about. Dead end piping, low-velocity heat exchanger zones, and stagnant basins create protected reservoirs where organisms establish and multiply, then periodically release into the main system. Even aggressive chemical dosing often fails to reach these problem areas at effective concentrations.

## Mechanisms: why poor circulation increases biological risk

Poor circulation reduces chemical delivery to critical surfaces. Biocide and disinfectant do not reach all locations at effective concentration because low flow zones receive less fresh treated water, and chemical is consumed upstream, leaving insufficient residual downstream.

Water systems with circulation problems develop lower and more unstable disinfectant residuals. Residual collapses in stagnant pockets where reactive demand from deposits and biofilm consumes what little chemical arrives. Control becomes "peaks and valleys" even during constant feed because the system is not mixing uniformly.

Increased water age gives organisms more time to grow. Stagnant water remains in place longer, providing more time for attachment and biofilm development while allowing nutrients and temperature conditions to act without interruption.

Low shear conditions support biofilm establishment and persistence. Biofilm forms more readily when shear forces are low, and once established, biofilm is harder to inactivate because the biofilm matrix reduces penetration and creates protected micro-zones.

Poor circulation allows sediment accumulation and "conditioning layers" to form. Solids settle in low-flow areas, and these deposits provide habitat and nutrients while shielding organisms from treatment and increasing chemical demand.

## Where poor circulation typically shows up

Dead end piping and seldom-used branches create minimal turnover and weak residual, acting as persistent reseeding sources for the rest of the system.

Low points, basins, and corners accumulate solids and biological deposits, especially where flow is not sweeping the area effectively.

Heat exchanger low-velocity zones and maldistributed flow paths allow deposits to establish at inlets and sheltered regions, creating both performance loss and biological reservoirs.

## Practical consequences for a control program

Monitoring results become misleading if only bulk water is sampled. Bulk water can look acceptable while surface biofilm grows in stagnant areas, with these reservoirs releasing organisms intermittently and causing spikes in biological counts.

Control programs require more frequent "corrective dosing" without durable improvement. Slug dosing may temporarily reduce counts, but poor circulation prevents durable removal of the underlying reservoirs.

Systems experience higher chemical costs with lower reliability. Operators increase dose to compensate for poor performance, yet under-treated zones remain under-treated regardless of bulk water chemical levels.

## Risk reduction actions

Improve hydraulic circulation by removing dead ends and improving flow through rarely used sections. Loop or remove dead end piping where feasible, and increase mixing in basins and low-flow areas by adjusting return locations or adding agitation where appropriate.

Target the reservoirs directly through cleaning and flushing deposit-prone zones to remove sediment and biofilm so biocide can work effectively. Use surface-based monitoring where feasible, such as swabs or coupons at known low-flow risk points.

Verify chemical delivery at the worst-case point by measuring residual or biocide indicator where circulation is weakest. Control to the limiting location, not the easiest sample point.

## Key takeaway

Monitor disinfectant residual weekly at dead ends and low-flow zones, maintain minimum 0.5 mg/L free chlorine even in stagnant areas, and consider bypass filtration or side-stream treatment for problem zones that cannot be hydraulically improved.

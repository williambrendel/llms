# How Do Bio Dispersants Work – The Critical Assistant?

## Executive Summary

Bio dispersants disrupt the protective matrix around bacterial biofilms, making them 100-1,000 times more vulnerable to biocides than when left undisturbed.

## Overview

Bacteria hiding in biofilms can survive biocide treatments that would instantly kill their free-floating cousins. The sticky matrix they create acts like armor, but bio dispersants strip away this protection. Without this critical assistant, even aggressive biocide programs often fail against established microbial communities.

## Biofilms Create the Root Problem

Bacteria in biofilms resist both oxidizing and non-oxidizing biocides with remarkable tenacity. Species like *Pseudomonas*, mucoid bacteria, and sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) embed themselves in extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). This sticky matrix shields them from chemical attack. Mold and yeast also contribute to or hide within these biofilm layers.

Without a biodispersant, biocides mostly kill surface cells while leaving the core of the biofilm intact and viable. The protected bacteria continue reproducing, quickly repopulating the system after treatment.

## What a Biodispersant Accomplishes

Bio dispersants attack the problem at its source by targeting the EPS matrix itself. They disrupt this protective structure, exposing inner biofilm layers to biocide action. The dispersant prevents detached microbes from reattaching to surfaces, keeping them suspended so bleed and blowdown can remove them from the system.

| Function | Benefit to Biocide Program |
| --- | --- |
| Disrupts EPS matrix | Exposes inner biofilm layers to biocide action |
| Prevents reattachment | Keeps detached microbes suspended and removes them via bleed/blowdown |
| Enhances surface wetting | Allows biocides to penetrate crevices, dead legs, scale underlayers |
| Reduces slime accumulation | Improves system hygiene and heat transfer |

Enhanced surface wetting allows biocides to penetrate crevices, dead legs, and scale underlayers that would otherwise remain untreated. The reduction in slime accumulation improves both system hygiene and heat transfer efficiency.

## When to Deploy a Biodispersant

Always use biodispersants when applying non-oxidizing biocides like glutaraldehyde, DBNPA, or isothiazolone. These chemicals depend entirely on direct contact with microorganisms, making biofilm penetration essential for effectiveness.

Biodispersants become essential in reclaimed water systems, intermittent wetting applications, or cooling systems prone to stagnation. These conditions promote heavy biofilm formation that standard biocide treatments cannot penetrate.

Never use biodispersants alone. They work during biocide slug dosing as part of a coordinated treatment strategy.

## Best Practices for Biodispersant Application

Select non-ionic or amphoteric dispersants such as Nalco 7348, Drew E11, or GE Betz BDA. These formulations provide effective matrix disruption without interfering with biocide chemistry.

| Parameter | Guideline |
| --- | --- |
| Type | Non-ionic or amphoteric dispersant (e.g., Nalco 7348, Drew E11, GE Betz BDA) |
| Dose (ppm active) | 5–20 ppm, depending on system fouling load |
| Frequency | 1–3× per week, coinciding with biocide slug |
| Application timing | 30 minutes before biocide, or co-fed |

Dose between 5-20 ppm active ingredient, adjusting based on your system's fouling load. Apply 1-3 times per week, always coinciding with biocide slug treatments. Time the application 30 minutes before biocide addition, or co-feed both chemicals simultaneously.

## Key Takeaway

Test your cooling water for biofilm activity monthly using ATP or microscopic analysis, maintain biodispersant doses above 10 ppm active ingredient for heavily fouled systems, and consider switching to combination biocide-dispersant products for simplified chemical management.

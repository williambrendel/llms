# How do I remove existing biofilm?

## Executive Summary

Mechanical removal followed by chemical detachment, targeted biocide application, and thorough flushing removes existing biofilm more reliably than single-step shock dosing. One-step approaches typically produce short-lived improvement because the protective matrix shields organisms from chemicals.

## Overview

Existing biofilm resists removal because it creates a protective matrix that limits chemical penetration to the organisms underneath. A cooling tower biofilm can reduce heat transfer efficiency by 30-50% even when bulk water appears clean. The most effective removal follows a specific sequence: physical disruption exposes the biofilm, chemical detachment breaks the matrix, biocides kill exposed organisms, and flushing removes the debris.

## Locate and characterize the biofilm

Map the highest-likelihood locations first. Low shear and low flow areas harbor biofilm most readily — dead legs, capped tees, bypasses, idle branches, basin corners, sumps, low points, strainers, and screens. In heat exchangers, check inlet zones and low-velocity channels. Open systems develop biofilm at wet-dry interfaces including basin waterlines, splash zones, louvers, drift eliminators, and distribution decks. Crevices around gaskets, joints, plate heat exchanger gasket edges, valve bodies, and threaded connections also accumulate biofilm.

Determine what type of deposits you're dealing with. Slimy, gelatinous, stringy material indicates biological dominance. Hard, crystalline deposits suggest mineral scale dominance. Reddish-brown granular material points to corrosion products. Mixed layered deposits are common — biofilm often grows over existing scale or corrosion layers.

Swab a defined surface area and test for adenosine triphosphate to confirm active biomass. If scale or corrosion products appear significant, collect deposit samples for lab identification since acid and cleaner selection depends on the specific minerals present.

## Establish safety and control prerequisites

Check chemical compatibility and select proper personal protective equipment using the product label and safety data sheet. Use chemical-resistant gloves, splash goggles, and protective clothing as minimum protection for liquid handling. Add a face shield for chemical transfers. Use respiratory protection if mist or irritating vapors are likely and ventilation is inadequate.

Prevent dangerous mixing. Do not mix oxidizers with acids or ammonia-containing products unless the products are specifically designed for combined use. Do not combine incompatible biocides and cleaners in the same step without flushing between applications.

Ensure you can circulate the entire target zone. Confirm pumps function properly and valves align correctly. Identify dead legs that cannot be circulated and plan separate flushing or physical removal for these areas. Control discharge during treatment — if blowdown or drain-down would remove the biocide or cleaner before contact time is achieved, isolate the system or manage discharge during the treatment window.

## Execute the removal sequence

### Step 1: Mechanical removal

Remove bulk mass first to reduce chemical demand and expose biofilm for the chemical steps.

For open systems like cooling towers, remove debris and sludge from basins by vacuuming, scooping, or draining and cleaning if accumulation is heavy. Clean strainers, screens, and suction baskets, expecting repeated loading during the process. Brush or pressure-wash accessible wetted surfaces including basin walls and troughs where equipment allows. Remove and clean plugged nozzles and distribution components. If fill is matted or heavily fouled, clean or replace it — replacement often provides the only durable solution for deeply colonized fill.

For closed loops and internal surfaces in piping, coils, and heat exchangers, clean strainers and dirt separators first. Do not circulate cleaners through plugged strainers. Flush low points and dead ends at high velocity where feasible. For plate-and-frame heat exchangers showing high differential pressure or poor performance, isolate and clean them. If chemical circulation cannot restore performance, disassemble and physically clean the plates.

### Step 2: Chemical detachment

Use a biodispersant or surfactant package to break the matrix and lift deposits. These chemicals reduce adhesion, break the protective matrix, and suspend solids. Run circulation long enough for detachment to occur — duration depends on the specific product and system volume, so follow product directions. Maintain adequate flow to create shear since low flow will not detach effectively.

Add an organic cleaner step when deposits are thick, greasy, or mixed with organics. Use an organic deposit cleaner compatible with your system metallurgy. If mineral scale is also present, do not add acid until the oxidizer step is complete and the system has been flushed to avoid hazardous reactions.

Run side stream filtration during detachment to capture lifted solids. Increase blowdown or perform controlled dumping only after detachment has mobilized solids, and verify discharge constraints first. Clean strainers repeatedly during this phase as they will load with detached material.

### Step 3: Biocide application

Apply biocides after detachment, not instead of detachment. For oxidizing biocide application, verify a measurable residual at the worst-circulated point, not only at the feed point. If residual collapses immediately, demand remains high from remaining deposits or organics, requiring additional detachment and cleaning before more kill chemistry will be effective.

Apply non-oxidizing biocide slugs after the oxidizing step for mature or persistent biofilm, especially if prior oxidizer-only programs failed. Ensure retention time is sufficient to maintain contact time by avoiding high blowdown during the slug window.

One-time dosing without verified concentration and contact time commonly fails because biofilm survives in low-flow and under-deposit zones.

### Step 4: Flush and remove released material

Flush to export suspended biomass and deposits. For open systems, use temporary increased blowdown plus basin cleaning as solids settle out. For closed loops, perform controlled flush-and-refill or partial bleed-and-feed until turbidity and solids stabilize.

Continue side stream filtration for multiple turnover cycles after the event to capture remaining fines. Continue frequent strainer checks until loading rate returns to normal baseline levels.

## Verify successful removal

Confirm you actually removed biofilm rather than just suppressed planktonic counts. Repeat surface swabs at the same locations used for baseline measurements. Compare adenosine triphosphate results before versus after treatment. Confirm visual cleanliness at known "first foul" locations.

For systems with heat exchangers, check performance indicators including reduced approach temperature, improved cooling performance, and reduced differential pressure across heat exchangers and critical piping segments.

Monitor trends for 1-2 weeks at increased frequency. If indicators rebound quickly, a reservoir remains in dead legs, heavy deposits, poor circulation zones, or areas with inadequate residual delivery.

## Prevent rapid regrowth

Fix hydraulic and design contributors that allow biofilm to establish. Eliminate dead legs where possible. Remove unused branches rather than leaving capped stubs. Improve circulation and shear in low-flow zones. Balance flows, ensure minimum velocities, and correct bypass leakage.

Reduce solids and nutrient loading by installing or optimizing side stream filtration in open systems. Establish routine basin cleaning and debris control. Stop process leaks and organic intrusion that feed microbial growth.

Maintain stable microbial control with an oxidizing program that delivers verified residual at the limiting location. Use periodic non-oxidizing rotation if biofilm pressure remains high. Use dispersant routinely only when indicated by deposit risk — it's not always required.

## Key takeaways

Test for biofilm presence using surface swabs for adenosine triphosphate before and after treatment to verify removal rather than relying on visual assessment alone. Follow the complete five-step sequence monthly in high-fouling systems or quarterly in moderate-fouling systems. Maintain oxidizing biocide residuals of 0.5-1.0 ppm free chlorine (or equivalent) at the furthest circulation point to prevent regrowth between cleaning cycles.

# Why is Pseudomonas dangerous in cooling towers?

## Executive Summary

Pseudomonas species in cooling towers create persistent biofilms that reduce thermal efficiency, accelerate corrosion, resist standard biocide treatments, and can pose health risks to facility personnel.

## Overview

A single Pseudomonas colony can cut heat transfer efficiency by up to 30% while shrugging off chlorine doses that would eliminate most other bacteria. These resilient microorganisms don't just survive in cooling towers—they architect their own protective cities from slime, transforming smooth metal surfaces into corroded, fouled landscapes that cost facilities thousands in energy losses and equipment replacement.

## The Biofilm Problem

Pseudomonas species, particularly _Pseudomonas aeruginosa_, excel at producing slimy, adhesive biofilms on heat exchanger surfaces, fill material, and basin walls. This biofilm formation represents the primary threat to cooling tower operations.

The consequences cascade through multiple systems. Biofilms insulate heat transfer surfaces, leading directly to reduced thermal efficiency. They accelerate corrosion, particularly microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC), especially in anaerobic zones that develop beneath the biofilm layer. The protective matrix also reduces biocide penetration, creating persistent microbial contamination that standard treatment programs struggle to eliminate.

## Biocide Resistance and Adaptive Survival

Pseudomonas demonstrates high intrinsic resistance to many oxidizing and non-oxidizing biocides. Their biofilm matrix shields embedded cells from even aggressive chemical treatments, creating protected microenvironments where bacteria continue to thrive.

The situation worsens over time. Pseudomonas populations develop adaptive resistance, especially when exposed to sub-lethal biocide doses. This evolutionary pressure creates increasingly difficult-to-treat bacterial communities that require escalating chemical interventions.

## Corrosion Acceleration

Metabolically active pseudomonads consume organic and inorganic nutrients while creating localized chemical gradients beneath their biofilm colonies. This biological activity contributes directly to pitting corrosion on mild steel and stainless steel surfaces.

The corrosion occurs in distinct patches where biofilm establishes, creating a characteristic pattern of microbiologically influenced corrosion that can penetrate deep into metal surfaces before detection.

## Operational Fouling

Pseudomonas biofilms trap suspended solids within their matrix, accelerating sludge formation throughout the cooling system. This fouling increases pump load and causes progressive clogging of strainers and nozzles.

The trapped debris creates additional surface area for further microbial growth, establishing a self-reinforcing cycle of contamination and fouling that degrades system performance.

## Health and Environmental Concerns

_P. aeruginosa_ functions as an opportunistic pathogen, creating risk for immunocompromised personnel through aerosol inhalation or contact with open wounds near cooling towers. While not as dangerous as _Legionella_, Pseudomonas can outcompete beneficial bacteria and create microbial environments that support other pathogens.

In stagnant or under-treated systems, pseudomonads produce malodorous compounds, including sulfurous or musty smells that are often mistaken for general tower maintenance issues rather than specific bacterial contamination.

## Effective Control Strategies

Successful Pseudomonas control requires aggressive, multi-pronged treatment programs. Facilities should maintain free chlorine (or HOBr) at ≥ 1.0 ppm continuously, with periodic slug doses reaching 3–5 ppm to penetrate established biofilms.

**What it measures:** The minimum oxidizer concentration needed to suppress Pseudomonas growth and biofilm formation

Formula:
```
Effective Control = Continuous Oxidizer (≥1.0 ppm) + Periodic Slug Dose (3-5 ppm)
```

**Example:** A 1,000-ton cooling tower maintains 1.5 ppm free chlorine daily, then applies a 4 ppm chlorine slug dose twice weekly to prevent biofilm establishment on heat exchanger surfaces.

The most effective approach combines oxidizing biocides with non-oxidizing antimicrobials and biodispersants in a synergistic program. This multi-chemical strategy attacks different bacterial vulnerabilities simultaneously while breaking down protective biofilm matrices.

## Taking Action

Monitor biofilm development through ATP testing, dip slide counts, and visual inspection of heat exchanger surfaces weekly. Establish baseline measurements during clean conditions, then track increases that indicate developing Pseudomonas populations. When ATP levels exceed 1,000 relative light units or dip slide counts show gram-negative rod bacteria, implement immediate slug dosing with chlorine at 5 ppm for 4-6 hours, followed by biodispersant treatment to remove loosened biofilm material.

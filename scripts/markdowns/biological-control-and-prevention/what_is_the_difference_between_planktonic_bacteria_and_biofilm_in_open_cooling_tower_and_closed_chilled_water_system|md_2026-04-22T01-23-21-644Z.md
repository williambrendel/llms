# What is the difference between planktonic bacteria and biofilm in open cooling tower and closed chilled water systems?

## Executive Summary

Planktonic bacteria float freely in water while biofilm bacteria attach to surfaces within a protective matrix, with biofilm typically driving Legionella risk more than planktonic counts alone because it shelters bacteria from disinfectants and supports harmful protozoa.

## Overview

Your water test results might show acceptable planktonic bacteria levels on any given day, yet significant biofilm could still be thriving in your cooling tower basin or chilled water system. This disconnect explains why some facilities experience Legionella outbreaks despite seemingly good water quality numbers. The difference between these two bacterial states determines not just your sampling strategy, but your entire approach to microbial control.

## Understanding Planktonic vs. Biofilm Bacteria

**Planktonic bacteria** exist as free-floating cells suspended throughout the bulk water. Most grab-sample water tests measure these organisms, depending on the testing method used. These suspended bacteria face direct exposure to biocides and disinfectants, making them relatively easier to eliminate than their surface-dwelling counterparts.

**Biofilm bacteria** take a completely different approach to survival. These organisms attach themselves to surfaces like pipe walls, basin surfaces, fill media, and heat exchanger tubes. Once attached, they embed themselves within a self-produced matrix called extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). This biofilm structure traps nutrients, sediment, and corrosion products while sheltering bacteria from disinfectants. The biofilm environment also supports protozoa and amoebae that can amplify Legionella populations.

## Why This Difference Drives Legionella Risk

Legionella risk stems primarily from biofilm ecology rather than planktonic counts alone. The biofilm matrix provides protection from both oxidizing and some non-oxidizing biocides. Within this protected environment, biofilm supports amoebae populations, and Legionella can multiply inside these single-celled hosts. Perhaps most concerning, biofilm periodically releases sloughing material that seeds the bulk water, causing intermittent spikes in bacterial populations that may not correlate with your regular sampling schedule.

## Open Cooling Tower Dynamics

Biofilm typically dominates as the primary bacterial reservoir in open cooling towers. These systems face constant challenges from their exposure to air, which introduces dust and organic matter. The nutrient-rich environment, combined with high surface area from fill media and numerous niches in basins, distribution decks, and drift eliminators, creates ideal biofilm habitat.

This reality means you can achieve acceptable planktonic test results while harboring significant biofilm in your basin and fill media. Effective Legionella prevention programs must address both planktonic growth through biocide control and biofilm formation through cleaning, filtration, scale and corrosion control, and regular physical inspections. The CDC specifically emphasizes that biofilm and sediment enable Legionella growth while reducing disinfectant effectiveness in cooling towers.

## Closed Chilled Water System Considerations

Closed loop systems often maintain lower nutrient levels and operate at cooler temperatures, which naturally limits planktonic growth when the system remains truly closed and well-managed. However, risk increases significantly under specific conditions: warm zones operating within the 68°F to 116°F Legionella growth range, stagnant water areas, systems that aren't truly closed due to open or vented expansion tanks with frequent makeup water, and accumulation of corrosion products or sludge that creates biofilm habitat.

Even closed loops showing low planktonic counts can harbor localized biofilm in low-flow areas, strainers, coils, or poorly flushed branch lines. Control strategies for these systems typically focus on corrosion and scale control combined with system cleanliness, supplemented by targeted microbial control where conditions warrant intervention.

## Measurement and Assessment Approaches

**Planktonic sampling** requires straightforward bulk water samples from your system. **Biofilm assessment** demands a more comprehensive approach including surface sampling with swabs or coupons, visual inspection findings documenting slime or fouling, monitoring differential pressure trends across strainers, filters, and heat exchangers, tracking turbidity and solids levels, and potentially using biological activity reaction test (BART) indicators where appropriate.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor both planktonic bacteria through weekly water sampling and biofilm development through monthly visual inspections and quarterly surface sampling, targeting biofilm removal when visual fouling appears or when differential pressure across heat exchangers increases by 20% above baseline, and consider implementing physical cleaning combined with biofilm-penetrating biocides rather than relying solely on oxidizing chemicals.

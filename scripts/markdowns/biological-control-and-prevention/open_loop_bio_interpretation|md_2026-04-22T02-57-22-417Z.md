# How do you interpret bio test results for open cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Open cooling systems require specific microbial control targets and biocide strategies based on the type and concentration of microorganisms present. Most systems need 5 ppm hypobromous acid for 2 hours three times weekly, with specialized non-oxidizing biocides for resistant organisms like Pseudomonas and sulfate-reducing bacteria.

## Overview

A single colony-forming unit of E. coli in your cooling water sample means immediate action—zero tolerance for pathogens separates open systems from closed ones. Open cooling towers face constant contamination from airborne microbes, making biological monitoring and targeted treatment essential for preventing equipment damage, health risks, and operational failures.

## Microbial Control Targets

Open cooling systems operate under different biological pressures than closed systems. The constant exposure to environmental contamination means you're managing populations, not eliminating them entirely.

**What it measures:** Acceptable microbial population levels that prevent system damage while acknowledging environmental reality

| **Species** | **Good Control Target (CFU/mL)** | **Not to Exceed Limit (CFU/mL)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total Viable Plate Count (TVPC) | <10,000 | 100,000 |
| Pigmented Bacteria | <1,000 | 10,000 |
| Mucoid Bacteria | <1,000 | 10,000 |
| Total Coliforms | 0 | 1 |
| E. coli | 0 | 1 |
| Pseudomonas | <1,000 | 10,000 |
| Sulfate Reducing Bacteria (SRB) | <100 | 1,000 |
| Mold | <100 | 1,000 |
| Yeast | <100 | 1,000 |

Total viable plate count serves as your general system health indicator. Numbers above 100,000 CFU/mL signal loss of biological control and imminent fouling problems. Pigmented and mucoid bacteria create visible problems—staining and slime formation respectively—but also indicate broader system imbalances that attract other problematic organisms.

Coliforms and E. coli receive zero tolerance because they indicate fecal contamination and direct health risks. Pseudomonas forms persistent biofilms and resists standard biocides. Sulfate-reducing bacteria operate in anaerobic pockets, corroding steel through microbially influenced corrosion (MIC). Mold and yeast create spores that plug filters and present respiratory hazards.

## Problems Caused by Each Species

Understanding what each organism does to your system determines treatment urgency and strategy.

| **Species** | **Problems Caused** |
| --- | --- |
| Total Viable Plate Count | General biofouling, corrosion under deposits, loss of heat transfer |
| Pigmented Bacteria | Surface staining, indicator of system imbalance |
| Mucoid Bacteria | Biofilm formation, fouling, can trap other bacteria |
| Total Coliforms | Indicator of fecal contamination or poor sanitation |
| E. coli | Pathogenic; direct health risk and contamination marker |
| Pseudomonas | Biofilm former, resistant to many biocides, health risk |
| Sulfate Reducing Bacteria (SRB) | Anaerobic corrosion, especially of steel (MIC) |
| Mold | Spore-forming; filter plugging, health concerns |
| Yeast | Organic fouling, allergenic and spoilage issues |

Biofilm-forming organisms like Pseudomonas and mucoid bacteria create the most persistent problems. Their protective matrix shields other bacteria from biocides and creates localized corrosion sites. Sulfate-reducing bacteria work differently—they consume sulfates in oxygen-depleted areas under deposits, producing hydrogen sulfide that directly attacks metal surfaces.

## Biocide Selection and Dosing Strategy

Your bio test results dictate which biocides to use and how to apply them. Different organisms require different chemical approaches.

| **Species** | **Preferred Biocide(s)** | **Biocide Type** | **Feed Strategy** | **Recommended Dose (ppm active)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Total Viable Plate Count | Hypobromous acid | Oxidizer | Slug feed M/W/F for 2 hrs | 3 (Min), 5 (Target), 7 (Max) |
| Pigmented Bacteria | Hypobromous acid + Iso | Oxidizer + Non-oxidizer | HOBr slug M/W/F + Isothiazolone weekly | HOBr: 5, Iso: 5–10 |
| Mucoid Bacteria | Hypobromous acid + Iso | Oxidizer + Non-oxidizer | HOBr slug M/W/F + Isothiazolone weekly | HOBr: 5–7, Iso: 5–10 |
| Total Coliforms | Hypobromous acid | Oxidizer | Slug feed M/W/F for 2 hrs | HOBr 5–7 |
| E. coli | Hypobromous acid | Oxidizer | Slug feed M/W/F for 2 hrs | HObr 5–7 |
| Pseudomonas | HOBr + DBNPA or Iso | Oxidizer + Non-oxidizer | HOBr slug M/W/F + weekly non-oxidizer slug | HOBr: 7, DBNPA: 5–10 or Iso: 10 |
| Sulfate Reducing Bacteria (SRB) | Glutaraldehyde | Non-oxidizer | Slug weekly (or as needed for MIC hotspots) | 100–200 |
| Mold | HOBr + Iso | Oxidizer + Non-oxidizer | HOBr M/W/F + Isothiazolone weekly | HOBr: 5–7, Iso: 5–10 |
| Yeast | Glutaraldehyde | Non-oxidizer | Slug weekly | 50–150 |

Hypobromous acid (HOBr) serves as the primary oxidizing biocide for most organisms. The Monday/Wednesday/Friday slug feeding pattern provides high-intensity contact while allowing system recovery between treatments. Continuous low-level feeding fails because organisms develop resistance to constant low concentrations.

Non-oxidizing biocides like DBNPA, isothiazolone, and glutaraldehyde penetrate biofilms and reach organisms in protected environments. Glutaraldehyde specifically targets sulfate-reducing bacteria because oxidizers cannot reach anaerobic sites where SRBs thrive.

## Critical Dosing Considerations

The difference between 3 ppm and 7 ppm hypobromous acid determines treatment success or failure. These numbers reflect real-world system conditions, not laboratory ideals.

Use 3 ppm HOBr only for systems with no existing biofilm or contamination—essentially new or recently cleaned systems under ideal conditions. Most operating systems require 5 ppm HOBr for 2 hours as the realistic target dose.

Increase to 7 ppm HOBr when bio tests show positive cultures, when systems use reclaimed water, when circulation patterns create dead zones, or when biofilm-prone organisms like Pseudomonas appear. Systems with high organic loading, frequent cycling, or resistant biofilms also need the higher dose.

Pseudomonas and mucoid organisms demand combination treatment. The fast-acting oxidizer disrupts cell walls while biofilm-penetrating agents like DBNPA or isothiazolone reach organisms within protective matrices. Single-agent treatment fails against these resistant species.

## Key Takeaways

Test cooling water biologically every two weeks during peak season, weekly during startup or contamination events. Maintain 5 ppm hypobromous acid residual for 2 hours three times weekly as your baseline program. Switch to combination oxidizer plus non-oxidizer treatment when Pseudomonas, mucoid bacteria, or SRBs exceed control targets, and increase monitoring frequency to weekly until populations return to acceptable levels.

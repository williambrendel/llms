# How Does the Cause and Control of Algae Differ from Total Viable Plate Count in Cooling Towers and Other Evaporative Applications?

## Executive Summary

Algae and total viable plate count respond to completely different drivers in cooling towers — algae thrives on sunlight and nutrients at specific surfaces, while high bacterial counts indicate system-wide loss of microbial control.

## Overview

A cooling tower can have excellent bacterial control yet still develop thick algae mats that clog strainers and create biofilm havens for pathogens. This happens because algae needs sunlight and surface attachment points that have nothing to do with the bulk water conditions that drive general bacterial growth. Understanding this difference transforms how operators prioritize their control strategies.

## What Each Measurement Reveals

**Total viable plate count (TVC/HPC)** measures live, culturable heterotrophs — mostly bacteria, with some yeasts and molds depending on the method. Operators use TVC to track general biological conditions across the entire system. A significant increase signals a water quality problem affecting the whole tower.

**Green algae** are photosynthetic organisms that standard HPC methods miss entirely. They require different media and incubation conditions, so bacterial counts can look normal while algae flourishes. Algae presence indicates three specific conditions: available light, sufficient nutrients, and wet surfaces where they can attach and grow.

## How Growth Drivers Differ

**High TVC/HPC stems from system-wide problems.** Inadequate disinfectant exposure, poor distribution, high organics with warm conditions, and accumulated deposits create ideal bacterial habitat. Stagnation and poor turnover compound these issues. CDC guidance emphasizes that sediment, biofilm, temperature, water age, and disinfectant residual drive the same bacterial growth patterns across cooling tower systems.

**Algae growth follows localized patterns.** Sunlight hitting tower decks, basin edges, louvers, or clear sumps provides the energy source. Inorganic nutrients — particularly phosphorus and nitrogen from makeup water, dust, process contamination, or windblown debris — fuel photosynthesis. Algae attach to fill edges and basin walls where water flows create the right conditions. This creates the classic scenario where algae problems concentrate in sunny spots even when bulk water bacterial counts remain acceptable.

## Control Strategies Target Different Conditions

**Controlling high TVC/HPC requires restoring overall microbial control.** Operators verify oxidant feed systems and confirm effective control at representative monitoring points throughout the system. Managing sediment and biofilm becomes critical — this means aggressive cleaning, solids removal, and improved filtration or blowdown programs. Reducing water age by eliminating dead zones and stabilizing operations addresses the underlying conditions bacteria need to multiply.

**Algae control targets the specific conditions algae requires.** Light control delivers the highest leverage. Shading or covering sunlit basins and sumps where feasible cuts off algae's energy source. Improving louvers and screens reduces direct sunlight penetration to basins. These physical changes can dramatically reduce algae without touching the biocide program.

Physical removal becomes essential for filamentous algae, which forms mats and strands that clog strainers and trap sediment. Mechanical cleaning of basins, strainers, and affected fill surfaces removes established growth. Filtration and blowdown must remove loosened biomass before it re-seeds new areas.

**What it measures:** The relationship between algae type and required cleaning intensity

Formula:
```
Filamentous algae = Surface mats + Plugging → Mechanical cleaning priority
Non-filamentous algae = Water discoloration + Suspended growth → Chemical control priority
```

**Example:** A tower with 2-inch thick algae mats on the basin walls (filamentous) needs physical scraping and high-pressure washing before any chemical treatment. The same tower with green-tinted water but clean surfaces (non-filamentous) responds better to consistent oxidant control plus targeted algaecides.

Nutrient and solids management prevents algae establishment. Keeping windblown debris out and maintaining basin housekeeping eliminates nutrient sources. Optimizing solids removal through filtration and blowdown prevents algae biomass from becoming food and shelter for broader biofilm communities.

Chemistry programs require algaecide strategies beyond routine oxidant control. Algae can rebound quickly when light and nutrients persist, so site-specific algaecide selection and rotation becomes necessary. Non-filamentous blooms respond to consistent oxidant control combined with algaecides. Filamentous algae often defeats chemical-only approaches — operators get temporary results that don't last without physical cleaning and light reduction.

## The Biofilm Connection

Algae matters in aerosolizing systems even though it's not the primary health pathogen operators worry about. Algae contributes directly to biofilm formation and deposit accumulation — the exact conditions that enable microbial amplification, including Legionella growth, in cooling towers. A tower with algae problems creates the habitat conditions that make bacterial control more difficult and pathogen risks higher.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor algae and bacterial counts separately with different frequencies — weekly visual algae inspections of sunlit surfaces, monthly TVC sampling from representative system points. Target algae control when visible growth appears on surfaces or when filamentous material clogs strainers. Prioritize light reduction and physical cleaning over chemical-only approaches for persistent algae problems, especially filamentous types that form structural mats.

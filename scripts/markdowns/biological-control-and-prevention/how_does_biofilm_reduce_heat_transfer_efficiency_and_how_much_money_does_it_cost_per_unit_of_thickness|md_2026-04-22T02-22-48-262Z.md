# How does biofilm reduce heat transfer efficiency, and how much money does it cost per unit of thickness?

## Executive Summary

Biofilm reduces heat transfer by adding an insulating layer with thermal conductivity around 0.6 watts per meter-kelvin, creating measurable resistance even at 20 micrometers thickness (increasing resistance by about 7%). Cost per thickness varies dramatically by system—a 1,000-kilowatt cooling system might face $556,800 per year per millimeter of biofilm, but actual costs depend on your specific heat exchanger duty, operating hours, and electricity rates.

## Overview

A biofilm layer thinner than human hair can cost thousands of dollars annually in a typical industrial cooling system. The 20-micrometer biofilm that increases heat transfer resistance by 7% measures roughly one-quarter the thickness of a sheet of paper, yet this microscopic layer forces chillers to work harder, pumps to run longer, and facilities to consume more electricity around the clock.

## How Biofilm Creates an Insulating Barrier

Biofilm acts like a thermal blanket wrapped around heat exchanger surfaces. The microbial matrix has a thermal conductivity of approximately 0.6 watts per meter-kelvin—similar to water but far lower than clean metal surfaces.

**What it measures:** The additional thermal resistance created by biofilm deposits

Formula:
```
Added resistance (m²·K/W) = biofilm thickness (m) ÷ biofilm thermal conductivity (W/m·K)
```

**Example:** A 100-micrometer biofilm layer with 0.6 W/m·K thermal conductivity adds 0.0001667 m²·K/W of resistance—enough to reduce heat transfer performance by roughly 30% in typical condensers.

This added resistance forces the overall heat transfer coefficient to drop. Heat exchangers operate on the principle that total thermal resistance equals the sum of all individual resistances: fluid films, metal walls, and any fouling layers. When biofilm increases total resistance, the system transfers less heat for the same temperature difference.

The impact grows disproportionately because clean heat exchanger surfaces already have relatively low resistance. Adding even thin biofilm layers creates noticeable performance degradation—the documented 7% resistance increase from just 20 micrometers demonstrates how quickly problems compound.

## Calculating Real-World Cost Impact

The physics connecting biofilm thickness to energy costs follows a predictable path, but the dollar amount depends entirely on your system specifications.

Consider a 1,000-kilowatt cooling system with a chiller coefficient of performance of 5, operating 8,000 hours annually at $0.12 per kilowatt-hour electricity rates. Clean operation requires 200 kilowatts of electrical input. A 100-micrometer biofilm layer that reduces heat transfer performance by 29% forces the system to consume approximately 58 additional kilowatts continuously.

The annual energy penalty reaches 464,000 kilowatt-hours, costing $55,680 per year. This translates to roughly $557 per micrometer of biofilm thickness, or $556,800 per millimeter.

Systems with higher cooling duties, longer operating hours, or premium electricity rates face proportionally higher costs. A pharmaceutical facility running 24/7 with peak electricity pricing might see costs double or triple. Conversely, smaller systems with shorter operating seasons experience lower absolute penalties.

## Measuring and Managing Biofilm Costs

Track biofilm thickness monthly using non-invasive ultrasonic measurements or thermal performance monitoring. Most facilities should target biofilm thickness below 50 micrometers to avoid significant energy penalties.

Consider automated cleaning systems when biofilm costs exceed $10,000 annually per heat exchanger, or when manual cleaning frequency exceeds weekly intervals. UV disinfection, ultrasonic cleaning, or enhanced biocide programs typically cost less than the energy penalties from unchecked biofilm growth.

Calculate your specific cost per thickness using your actual cooling load, chiller efficiency, operating schedule, and local electricity rates—the relationship between thickness and cost remains consistent, but the dollar amounts vary dramatically across facilities.

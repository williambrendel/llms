# How do audits evaluate biological programs?

## Executive Summary

Audits evaluate biological control programs by checking whether the program identifies risk points, sets measurable control limits, monitors those limits, documents results, corrects failures, and verifies that the program actually works.

## Overview

A strong biological program usually answers three audit questions clearly: Did the site choose the right controls for the system? Did the site monitor them often enough and record them correctly? When control was lost, did the site respond quickly and prove recovery? If the answer to any of those three is no, the audit usually finds the program ineffective even if chemicals were being added.

## How Audits Test Biological Programs

Audits usually test six things across any water system type.

### Program Design
The audit confirms there's a written program with a current system description, flow diagram, responsible personnel, and identified risk areas. For cooling towers, this should address scale, corrosion, sediment, water age, temperature, and disinfectant residuals.

### Technical Adequacy
Auditors verify that the selected controls are technically appropriate for the system type. Open recirculating systems like cooling towers require close attention because they can concentrate nutrients and create aerosols, while closed systems usually have lower exposure risk but can still develop biofilm, microbiologically influenced corrosion, and poor chemistry control.

### Monitoring Discipline
The audit checks whether parameters are actually measured at the required frequency, by the required method, and recorded completely. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention states that an effective water management program includes system analysis, control measures, monitoring, corrective action, verification, and validation.

### Response Quality
The program must specify what happens when a limit is missed, and records must show timely corrective action. Corrective action is a required program element in current guidance.

### Verification and Validation
Verification asks whether the program was followed as written. Validation asks whether it reduced risk in practice. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that validation confirms whether the program controls hazardous conditions as intended.

### Record Integrity
Logs must be complete, signed, dated, trended, reviewed, and retained long enough to support trend analysis and investigations.

## Open-Loop System Audit Checklist

For open recirculating cooling towers, auditors typically check these elements:

### Document Control
- Current written biological control or water management program is available
- Current schematic shows basin, fill, drift eliminators, heat exchanger, side-stream filtration if present, chemical feed points, bleed-off, make-up water, and sampling points
- Program names responsible staff and backup staff
- Review date is current

### Hazard Identification
- Program addresses sediment, scale, corrosion, stagnation, water age, sunlight where relevant, temperature, and disinfectant residual
- Areas with poor flow, dead zones, splash surfaces, and aerosol release are identified
- Seasonal start-up and shutdown risks are addressed

### Control Measures and Limits
- Biocide program is defined, including product, dose basis, feed method, target range, and frequency
- Secondary controls are defined for scale, corrosion, and solids control
- Blowdown or bleed control is defined
- Control limits are written for at least disinfectant residual, conductivity or cycles of concentration, pH if relevant to chemistry program, and visible cleanliness

### Monitoring
- Daily or defined-operating-day checks are documented for critical chemistry parameters
- Routine dip slide, adenosine triphosphate, or other microbiological trend method is specified if used
- Sampling points are consistent and justified
- Meters and test kits have current calibration or quality checks
- Logs include date, time, result, initials, and follow-up

### Physical Condition
- Basin is free of excessive sludge, scale, and debris
- Drift eliminators are intact
- No obvious dead legs, bypasses, or low-flow zones are ignored
- Filtration equipment is operating as intended
- Tower inspection frequency is defined and followed

### Corrective Action
- Out-of-limit responses are written, specific, and time-bound
- Records show actual use of corrective action when limits were missed
- Repeat deviations trigger escalation, root-cause review, and program adjustment
- Emergency response steps exist for suspected disease case linkage or gross loss of control

### Verification and Validation
- Internal review of logs is performed at a defined frequency
- Cleaning and disinfection records are present for start-up, shutdown, and scheduled maintenance
- Trend review shows whether microbial indicators and chemistry remain stable over time
- Validation method is defined, such as environmental testing strategy, disease surveillance link, or another risk-confirmation approach appropriate to the facility

## Closed-Loop System Audit Checklist

For closed chilled-water or heating-water loops, auditors focus on these areas:

### Document Control
- Current written program is available for chemistry and biological control
- Current loop schematic identifies expansion tank, heat exchangers, dosing points, side-stream filter if present, dead legs, drain and vent points, and sampling points
- Program identifies metals present, such as steel, copper, aluminum, stainless steel, or mixed metallurgy, because these affect corrosion risk

### Risk Identification
- Program recognizes that closed loops usually have lower external contamination and aerosol exposure than cooling towers, but still can suffer oxygen ingress, nutrient introduction, stagnant sections, biofilm growth, and microbiologically influenced corrosion
- System areas prone to low flow, temporary isolation, poor venting, or repeated make-up water additions are identified
- Commissioning, refill, repair, and partial drain-down events are treated as contamination risks

### Control Measures and Limits
- Required inhibitor program is specified and matches system metallurgy
- Make-up water control is defined, because unexpected make-up can signal leaks and oxygen entry
- Air elimination and pressurization controls are functioning
- Control limits are written for key chemistry such as pH, conductivity, inhibitor concentration, dissolved oxygen where used, and suspended solids or turbidity where relevant
- The site has a defined trigger for microbiological investigation, such as odor, slime, falling heat-transfer performance, under-deposit corrosion findings, or laboratory counts above the site action level

### Monitoring
- Routine chemistry testing is documented at defined intervals
- Inhibitor residual or concentration is trended
- Corrosion monitoring is performed, such as coupon data or approved equivalent method
- Microbial assessment is included when risk factors exist or when the loop has a history of fouling or corrosion
- Sampling method avoids false results caused by poorly selected sample points or stagnant sample ports

### Physical Condition and Performance
- No visible slime, black deposits, or foul odor at strainers, vents, low points, or removed sections
- Strainers and filters are inspected and findings recorded
- Heat transfer performance and pressure drop are reviewed for signs of fouling
- Repair history is reviewed for recurring leaks, air ingress, or rapid loss of inhibitor

### Corrective Action
- Program defines response to low inhibitor, high oxygen entry, high corrosion rate, microbial growth findings, or evidence of fouling
- Corrective action may include leak repair, flushing, cleaning, inhibitor rebalance, targeted biocide treatment if compatible with the system, and increased follow-up sampling
- Records show the system returned to control after intervention

### Verification and Validation
- Review confirms monitoring occurred at required frequency
- Trend review shows stable chemistry and acceptable corrosion performance over time
- Post-correction data confirm that biofilm, corrosion, or water quality indicators improved
- The program is reviewed after major repairs, repeated contamination, or seasonal operational changes

## Key Takeaways

Successful biological control programs pass audits by demonstrating appropriate system-specific controls, consistent daily monitoring with complete documentation, and rapid corrective action with verified recovery when limits are exceeded. For cooling towers, monitor disinfectant residual and conductivity daily while maintaining current system schematics and cleaning schedules. For closed loops, track inhibitor concentration and make-up water usage weekly while investigating any signs of biofilm, corrosion, or performance decline immediately.

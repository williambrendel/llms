# How to control biofilm in open cooling towers and closed loop cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Biofilm control requires a multi-layered approach combining mechanical cleaning, oxidizing biocides, non-oxidizing biocides in rotation, and bio-dispersants, with open cooling towers demanding continuous monitoring and closed-loop systems focusing on oxygen control and periodic treatment.

## Overview

Biofilm makes bacteria up to 1,000 times more resistant to biocides than free-floating cells. This creates a protective shield that accelerates corrosion, scaling, and heat-transfer loss while serving as a primary breeding ground for Legionella in open systems. The challenge intensifies because most facilities rely solely on biocides without addressing the mechanical and chemical foundations that allow biofilm to establish in the first place.

## Why biofilm control matters in both systems

Biofilm protects bacteria from biocides, making them up to 1,000 times more resistant than planktonic cells. This protective matrix accelerates corrosion, scaling, and heat-transfer loss throughout the system. In open cooling towers, biofilm creates the primary risk factor for Legionella proliferation, turning routine maintenance into a public health concern.

## Open cooling towers: highest risk systems

### Mechanical control forms the foundation

Routine basin cleaning and sludge removal prevents biofilm attachment sites from accumulating. Side-stream filtration running at 5-10% of total flow targets solids removal down to 50 micrometers or smaller. Drift eliminator maintenance prevents water loss and contamination.

Biofilm requires both organic and inorganic solids as attachment sites. Without mechanical removal of these materials, chemical treatment becomes significantly less effective.

### Oxidizing biocides provide primary control

The most common oxidizing biocides operate at specific residual levels:

| **Biocide** | **Typical Residual** |
| --- | --- |
| Free chlorine continuous | 0.5–1.0 mg/L (continuous) |
| Free chlorine slug | 3 to 5 ppm 3x / Week |
| Free Bromine continuous | 0.5–1.5 mg/L as bromine ( 2.25 x free chlorine test ) |
| Free Bromine slug | 3 to 5 ppm 3x / Week bromine ( 2.25 x free chlorine test ) |
| Chlorine dioxide | 0.1–0.3 mg/L |
| Monochloramine | 1–3 mg/L |

Continuous low-level feed works best in well-controlled systems, while slug dosing handles systems with operational challenges. Maintain ORP at 650 mV or higher, though this varies by system based on free chlorine testing results. Control pH below 8.0 where chlorine reaches maximum effectiveness.

### Non-oxidizing biocides penetrate established biofilm

Isothiazolin, glutaraldehyde, DBNPA, and THPS rotate through treatment programs because they penetrate established biofilm where oxidizers cannot reach. Apply these through slug dosing 1-2 times per week, alternating products every 1-3 months to prevent bacterial resistance.

### Bio-dispersants break the biofilm matrix

Bio-dispersants break down the extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) that form biofilm's protective matrix. This exposes bacteria to biocides that would otherwise remain ineffective. Dose bio-dispersants before biocide slugs on a weekly or bi-weekly schedule. Without bio-dispersants, biocides lose significant effectiveness against established biofilm.

### Monitoring verifies program effectiveness

BART Tests HAB track heterotrophic plate count trends, while ATP testing provides rapid bioactivity indicators. Legionella testing follows risk-based protocols. HPC trending upward signals loss of biofilm control, and BART HAB spikes indicate EPS buildup requiring immediate attention.

## Closed-loop cooling systems: oxygen control strategy

### System cleanliness and oxygen control

Biofilm in closed systems develops mainly from oxygen ingress through leaks and system penetrations. Even small leaks sustain microbial growth throughout the loop. Repair leaks immediately, maintain positive system pressure, and use deaeration equipment where applicable.

### Initial cleaning and passivation

High-alkaline cleaners with dispersants circulate through the system before flushing completely. Follow this with metal passivation using nitrite, molybdate, or phosphate-based treatments. Existing biofilm must be removed before ongoing treatment programs work effectively.

### Closed-loop biocide programs

Nitrite-based corrosion inhibitors maintain 800-1,200 mg/L as NO₂⁻ while supplemental biocides like isothiazolin, glutaraldehyde, or DBNPA dose quarterly to semi-annually depending on system conditions.

### Filtration and monitoring requirements

Side-stream filtration at 1-3% of total flow removes particles and biological matter. Annual microbial testing and visual inspection of strainers and heat exchangers track system health. Monitor nitrite depletion and iron increases as early indicators of biological activity, along with any slime or odor development.

## Key takeaways for effective biofilm control

Test HPC and ATP levels monthly in open systems and quarterly in closed systems to catch biofilm development early. Maintain free chlorine residuals above 0.5 mg/L continuously or slug dose to 3-5 ppm three times weekly in open towers. Consider UV or ozone systems as alternatives where chemical biocides face regulatory restrictions or operational challenges.

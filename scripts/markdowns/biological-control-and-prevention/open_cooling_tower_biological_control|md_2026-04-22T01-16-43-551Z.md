# How to Control Bacteria and Biological Growth in an Open Cooling Tower?

## Executive Summary

Effective biological control in open cooling towers requires a multi-layered approach combining oxidizing biocides, non-oxidizing rotation treatments, physical system cleanliness, and comprehensive monitoring using BART tests, ATP measurements, and operational indicators.

## Overview

A single cooling tower can harbor millions of bacteria per milliliter, yet most facilities rely on outdated testing methods that miss the real problem. While traditional heterotrophic plate counts might show acceptable numbers, biofilms and specialized bacterial communities continue thriving in protected niches, consuming biocides and creating the perfect conditions for corrosion and fouling.

## Measuring and Trending the Right Indicators

Successful biological control starts with monitoring multiple biological metrics rather than relying on a single test. Each measurement reveals different aspects of microbial activity in your system.

BART (Biological Activity Reaction Tests) serves as your primary diagnostic tool for identifying and tracking problem bacterial groups commonly found in cooling systems. Use BART kits to detect SRB (sulfate-reducing bacteria), IRB (iron-related bacteria), HAB (heterotrophic aerobic bacteria), and SLYM (slime-forming bacteria). The real value comes from trending results over time using consistent sampling locations and procedures. Deploy BART tests as a diagnostic tool when you suspect biofilm formation, under-deposit growth, microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) risk, or recurring slime and odor issues.

ATP testing provides a fast indicator of total biological activity, making it invaluable for day-to-day trending and verifying that biocide treatments actually worked. Combine this with HPC (heterotrophic plate count) dipslides for simple weekly trending of heterotrophic bacteria, though these tests can miss biofilm problems.

Monitor operational indicators including pH, conductivity/cycles, temperature, and flow/pressure drop across strainers and heat exchangers. Visual and physical signs tell the complete story: slime, algae, odor, plugged nozzles or fill, basin sludge, and clogged strainers all indicate biological control failures.

## Running a Biocide Program for Open Recirculating Systems

Your biocide program requires both primary control and supplemental treatments to address different bacterial populations and growth patterns.

Oxidizing biocides provide primary control through chlorine, bromine, or stabilized bromine programs. Execute these programs by slug feeding on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday schedules if your system lacks cleanliness, or maintain a measurable residual at representative sampling points throughout the system—not just near the feed point. Control pH to support oxidizer effectiveness, remembering that chlorine performance depends heavily on pH while bromine shows less pH sensitivity. Avoid using ORP (oxidation-reduction potential) as supporting data without running a local ORP versus free halogen curve, and always confirm results with residual testing.

Non-oxidizing biocides serve as rotation or supplemental treatments to penetrate slime layers and reduce resistant organisms. Choose from isothiazolin, DBNPA, glutaraldehyde, or quaternary ammonium compounds based on your site requirements and discharge limitations. Apply these as slug feeds with adequate contact time, avoiding feeds immediately before major blowdown events.

Biodispersants disrupt biofilm formation and improve biocide contact with protected bacterial communities. Feed biodispersants routinely or before non-oxidizing slug treatments to maximize effectiveness.

## Keeping the System Clean

Biological control extends far beyond chemical treatment. Physical cleanliness directly impacts your program's success.

Install side-stream filtration handling 3–10% of recirculation flow to reduce suspended solids that shelter bacteria and consume oxidizer. Maintain strainers, ensure uniform tower distribution, and eliminate dry spots on fill media where bacteria concentrate.

Establish routine basin housekeeping procedures: remove sludge and leaf litter, clean sumps regularly, and verify drift eliminators and louvers function properly. Schedule periodic online or offline cleaning when biofilm becomes established, managing the process to protect heat exchangers from released debris.

## Reducing Nutrients and Entry Pathways

Identify and repair process leaks introducing oils, glycols, organics, or wastewater into your cooling system. These contamination sources frequently explain "why nothing works" situations where biological problems persist despite proper chemical treatment.

Manage makeup water turbidity and organic content, and protect basins from excessive sunlight where feasible to control algae growth.

## Verifying Execution

Program failure often stems from execution problems rather than chemistry issues. Confirm feed equipment calibration, injection locations, mixing effectiveness, interlocks, and control logic function as designed.

Sample at worst-case locations including the far end of circulation loops, lowest flow zones, and return headers where biological activity typically peaks. Trend results and adjust programs using residual measurements combined with ATP, BART, and fouling/pressure drop data.

## Four Root Causes of Biological Problems

Insufficient biocide exposure represents the most common failure mode, involving inadequate dose, frequency, contact time, or pH effectiveness. Typical signs include persistent ATP and HPC counts, rapid rebound after treatments, and inconsistent residuals throughout the system. BART tests help reveal persistent niche organisms like slime formers or SRB/IRB populations consistent with biofilm protection or chemical underfeed.

High biological demand from contamination creates the second major problem category. Airborne organics, basin sludge, high-turbidity makeup water, and especially process leaks provide nutrients and solids that overwhelm biocide capacity. BART results often show broader microbial activity or specific bacterial groups increasing when nutrients and solids rise.

Biofilm-supporting hydraulics and surfaces create protected environments where bacteria thrive despite adequate chemical treatment. Warm water, sunlight exposure, low-flow or stagnant zones, under-deposit areas, fouled rough surfaces, and inadequate filtration all contribute to biological control failures. BART results repeatedly indicating slime formers or anaerobic bacteria often align with protected biofilm and under-deposit environments.

## Key Takeaways

Test your cooling water weekly using both ATP and BART methods, maintaining ATP levels below 1,000 relative light units and addressing any BART positive results within 48 hours. Implement side-stream filtration at 5% minimum recirculation rate, and establish a biocide rotation program alternating oxidizing treatments three times weekly with monthly non-oxidizing slug doses.

# What microorganisms commonly grow in open cooling tower and closed loop water systems?

## Executive Summary

Open cooling towers support heavy microbial growth including Legionella, heterotrophic bacteria, algae, and biofilm-forming organisms, while closed loops primarily face problems from denitrifying bacteria and sulfate-reducing bacteria in low-flow zones and under deposits.

## Overview

Cooling towers act like giant air scrubbers that concentrate nutrients and microbes, creating perfect conditions for biological growth that can reach millions of organisms per milliliter. Even "clean" closed loops can harbor dangerous anaerobic bacteria in dead legs and deposit layers where oxygen can't reach. The difference between these systems determines which organisms thrive and what problems they cause.

## Open Cooling Tower Microorganisms

Open cooling towers face the heaviest biological challenge because they continuously scrub microorganisms from air while concentrating nutrients. These systems support both planktonic growth in bulk water and biofilm formation on surfaces.

**Heterotrophic aerobic bacteria** represent the most common problem organisms. Species like Aeromonas, Acinetobacter, and Bacillus form biofilms that increase oxidant demand and cause under-deposit corrosion. The industry uses 10,000 CFU/mL as a widely accepted "action needed" threshold indicating loss of control.

**Pseudomonas species**, particularly P. aeruginosa, create especially persistent biofilms once established. These strong biofilm formers drive high disinfectant demand and contribute significantly to fouling and corrosion. While no universal CFU threshold exists for Pseudomonas, keeping levels below 1,000 CFU/mL represents good practice.

**Legionella species** pose the greatest public health risk, causing Legionnaires' disease when aerosolized droplets reach building occupants. Control standards classify 10-99 CFU/mL as poorly controlled systems, while levels at or above 100 CFU/mL indicate uncontrolled conditions requiring immediate action.

**Coliform bacteria** including E. coli, Klebsiella, and Enterobacter indicate contaminated makeup water and higher organic loading. Target levels should remain below 2.2 CFU/mL for total coliforms with non-detect results for fecal coliforms.

**Algae and cyanobacteria** thrive on sunlit wet surfaces, forming green mats that clog fill and screens. Common species include Chlorella, Scenedesmus, Pediastrum, and Oocystis. These organisms increase biofilm food sources and oxidant demand. Management relies on visual observation rather than CFU counts, with visible growth serving as the practical trigger for action.

**Anaerobic bacteria** grow under deposits despite bulk water aeration. Sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) create black deposits, produce hydrogen sulfide odor, and cause microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) through pitting. Bulk water CFU counts don't reliably indicate SRB problems since these organisms live under deposits. BART-style tests showing reactions within one day suggest very high populations in the millions of CFU/mL.

**Iron-related bacteria (IRB)** produce voluminous rust-colored slime that plugs systems and contributes to tubercle formation and MIC risk. Diagnosis typically relies on deposit analysis and microscopy rather than standardized CFU thresholds.

## Closed Loop System Microorganisms

Closed loops generally maintain lower biological activity, but when contamination occurs, it concentrates in dead legs, low-flow zones, and under deposits where conditions favor dangerous anaerobic growth.

**Denitrifying bacteria** pose the primary threat in closed loops, especially those using nitrite inhibitor programs. These organisms consume nitrite inhibitors and contribute to under-deposit corrosion activity. Any persistent detection combined with nitrite loss or iron sludge formation indicates a developing problem requiring immediate attention.

**Sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB)** cause MIC, pitting, black sludge formation, and odor problems in closed loops. Target levels should remain below 1 CFU/mL, though practically any SRB detection raises concern because it indicates anaerobic niches where these organisms can multiply rapidly.

**Iron-cycling bacteria** accelerate deposit formation and contribute to tubercles and MIC risk in very low dissolved oxygen environments. While standardized thresholds don't exist, confirmed presence in deposits combined with rising iron levels, turbidity, or strainer plugging indicates significant problems.

**Slime-forming heterotrophic bacteria**, often including Pseudomonas species, create biofilms that shelter MIC consortia and foul coils, valves, and strainers. Closed loops should target levels below 100 CFU/mL, though no universal CFU threshold predicts system failure.

**Fungi** appear less commonly in closed loops but can occur in contaminated systems or those using glycol. No standardized CFU thresholds exist, but persistent detection with visible deposits indicates contamination and inadequate system cleanliness.

## System-Specific Control Priorities

**Cooling tower management** should focus on Legionella risk control combined with broad biofilm management targeting bacteria, algae, and protozoa. The interconnected nature of tower microbiomes means comprehensive biological control programs work better than targeting individual organism types.

**Closed loop management** should prioritize denitrifying bacteria and SRB detection when biology appears, combined with aggressive deposit control targeting iron sludge and biofilm formation. The enclosed nature of these systems means even small biological populations can cause significant problems if they establish in the right locations.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor cooling towers weekly for heterotrophic plate counts above 10,000 CFU/mL and Legionella above 10 CFU/mL, while testing closed loops monthly for any detectable SRB or denitrifying bacteria. Implement comprehensive biocide programs with dispersants for towers and immediate system cleaning with biocide treatment for any confirmed biological activity in closed loops.

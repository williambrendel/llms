# What causes biofouling in heat exchangers and how can it be prevented?

## Executive Summary

Biofouling in heat exchangers results from microorganisms forming biofilm on internal surfaces, thriving in warm temperatures with available nutrients and oxygen. Prevention requires coordinated microbial control through biocides, nutrient and solids removal via filtration, proper hydraulics to maintain adequate velocity, and regular cleaning before mature biofilm develops.

## Overview

A thin layer of biofilm just 0.1 millimeters thick can reduce heat transfer efficiency by 20% or more. Microorganisms don't simply float through your heat exchanger—they actively attach to surfaces, multiply, and create protective slime layers that act like thermal blankets. Once established, these biological deposits become increasingly difficult to remove and can trigger corrosion underneath.

## Causes of Biofouling

### Microbial Attachment and Biofilm Formation

Initial attachment occurs on wetted surfaces when bacteria adhere to metal or polymer surfaces. These cells produce extracellular polymeric substances that form a protective matrix, creating what becomes a persistent biofilm community.

### Favorable Growth Conditions

Temperature supports rapid microbial growth. Warm process fluids and cooling water accelerate cell replication, turning a few attached organisms into substantial deposits within days.

Nutrients fuel this growth through natural organic matter in make-up water, airborne debris in open systems, and process leaks that introduce organics. Oxygen availability, particularly from aeration in open cooling systems, supports aerobic organisms that often dominate biofilm communities.

### Deposition and Surface Conditioning

Particulates and silt deposit first, creating a "conditioning layer" that microbes then colonize. Suspended solids provide attachment sites, while scale and corrosion create surface roughness that makes removal more difficult.

### Hydraulic Contributors

Low velocity and dead zones reduce shear forces that would otherwise prevent attachment. Poor distribution creates localized stagnation where organisms can establish without mechanical disruption.

### Inadequate Control Program

Insufficient disinfectant or biocide exposure allows organisms to proliferate. This happens through underfeed, short contact time, poor mixing, or when biocide demand gets consumed by organics and existing deposits. Organisms within established biofilm develop resistance and protection from treatment chemicals.

## Consequences

Heat transfer loss occurs as biofilm adds thermal resistance, leading to higher energy use and potential inability to meet cooling or heating loads. Flow restriction and pressure drop become severe in plate or microchannel exchangers where even small deposits can plug narrow channels.

Under-deposit corrosion risk increases significantly, as microbiologically influenced corrosion can cause rapid metal loss in localized areas beneath biofilm.

## Prevention and Control Program

### Control Microorganisms

Maintain oxidizing biocide or disinfectant programs with target residual levels and adequate contact time. Verify effectiveness through routine monitoring and trending of microbial indicators.

Non-oxidizing biocide rotation helps control organisms less susceptible to oxidizers and reduces selection pressure from single-product use. Biodispersant and surfactant applications break up early biofilm and improve biocide penetration into established deposits.

### Control Nutrients and Solids

Side-stream filtration or full-flow filtration removes silt that seeds deposits and reduces conditioning layer formation. Manage blowdown and cycles of concentration in open systems to limit buildup of dissolved and suspended materials.

Prevent process leaks by repairing exchanger tube or plate leaks that introduce organics, which serve as nutrients for microbial growth.

### Optimize Hydraulics and Operation

Maintain adequate velocity and turbulence to reduce attachment and deposit retention. Eliminate dead legs and low-flow zones by improving distribution and balancing flow throughout the system.

Avoid extended stagnation during shutdown by using proper layup procedures with biocide protection, whether wet or dry storage methods.

### Materials and Design Considerations

Select less fouling-prone configurations where feasible, providing access for inspection and cleaning. Avoid extremely low channel clearances when water quality varies, as these become impossible to clean effectively.

### Inspection, Cleaning, and Verification

Establish routine mechanical cleaning schedules using tube brushing, pigging, or clean-in-place methods as applicable to your exchanger type. Apply chemical cleaning when deposits persist, using cleaners compatible with organics and biofilm, followed by passivation when required.

Implement monitoring programs tracking microbial indicators such as heterotrophic plate count or adenosine triphosphate levels. Trend differential pressure and approach temperature to trigger cleaning before performance degrades significantly. Conduct visual inspection of strainers, basins, and exchanger internals when possible.

## Practical Minimum Controls

For open recirculating cooling water feeding heat exchangers, start with continuous or frequent oxidizing disinfectant residual control combined with periodic non-oxidizing biocide application. Install side-stream filtration sized to meaningfully reduce suspended solids.

Apply biodispersant coordinated with biocide timing to maximize effectiveness. Trend heat transfer performance using approach temperature and pressure drop as triggers for cleaning interventions.

## Key Takeaway

Implement biocide residual monitoring weekly, maintain filtration to keep suspended solids below 10 mg/L, and clean heat exchangers when approach temperature increases by 2°F above baseline. Consider ultrasonic or automated brush cleaning systems for critical applications where manual cleaning proves insufficient.

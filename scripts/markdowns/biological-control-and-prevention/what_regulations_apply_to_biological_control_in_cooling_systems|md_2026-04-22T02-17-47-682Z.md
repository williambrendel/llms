# What regulations apply to biological control in cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Biological control in cooling systems falls under multiple regulatory frameworks: federal pesticide law (FIFRA), workplace safety requirements, state and local public health rules, and adopted engineering standards. New York City exemplifies direct regulation, requiring monthly Legionella sampling starting May 7, 2026.

## Overview

A single cooling tower might face five different sets of requirements from four different agencies. Federal pesticide law makes biocide labels legally binding. OSHA can enforce workplace safety through its General Duty Clause. Local jurisdictions increasingly mandate registration, testing, and maintenance plans.

## Federal Pesticide Regulation

Cooling-system biocides fall under the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA). This makes the product label legally significant for determining compliant use. Label directions specify dose rates, contact times, feed frequency, and application limitations.

Misuse of a registered biocide creates both a treatment failure and a compliance violation. The EPA requires biocides to be used according to their labeling, which means operators cannot exceed maximum doses or ignore safety requirements.

## Workplace Safety Requirements

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration lacks a cooling-tower-specific standard but enforces the General Duty Clause where Legionella creates a recognized workplace hazard. OSHA identifies cooling towers, evaporative condensers, and fluid coolers as systems that create ideal conditions for microbial growth.

Employers must control microbial hazards through reasonable, documented measures. The obligation focuses on hazard control rather than prescribing specific chemistry programs.

## State and Local Public Health Rules

State and local regulations often exceed federal requirements in specificity. Common mandates include:

- Cooling tower registration
- Written maintenance or water management plans  
- Cleaning and disinfection at specified intervals
- Legionella sampling and corrective action protocols
- Inspection and recordkeeping requirements

### New York City Example

New York City operates the most comprehensive cooling tower regulatory program. Local Law 159 of 2025 takes effect May 7, 2026, changing Legionella sampling from every 90 days to monthly. The law requires:

- Registration of cooling towers, fluid coolers, and evaporative condensers
- Maintenance programs describing operational and administrative strategies to prevent Legionella growth
- Chemical treatment sufficient to control Legionella, minimize biofilms, and prevent scaling and corrosion
- Cleaning and disinfection before startup and at required intervals
- Compliance inspections every 90 days

## Engineering Standards and Guidelines

American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) Standard 188 establishes minimum legionellosis risk-management requirements for building water systems. This standard becomes enforceable when adopted by regulation, code, contract, or owner requirement.

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance shapes expected practices for monitoring, cleaning, disinfection, corrective action, and documentation. These guidelines influence what regulators and courts consider reasonable control practice.

## Common Regulatory Elements

Where direct rules exist, they typically address:

- System registration with local authorities
- Written maintenance or water management plans
- Routine treatment with registered biocides
- Scheduled cleaning and disinfection
- Monitoring and recordkeeping protocols
- Legionella testing requirements
- Corrective action when limits are exceeded

Regulations treat biological control as a management system rather than just chemical feed practice. They emphasize mechanical condition, cleaning protocols, monitoring frequency, and documentation quality.

## Practical Compliance Strategy

For any specific site, review requirements in this order:

1. Local cooling-tower ordinances or public health rules
2. State requirements
3. Workplace safety obligations  
4. Federal pesticide label compliance
5. Adopted standards and owner requirements

The most defensible program uses registered biocides correctly, maintains written water management plans, documents monitoring and corrective action, and meets all local testing, cleaning, and recordkeeping requirements.

## Key Takeaway

Establish a written water management plan that documents monthly Legionella testing (or local requirement), maintains biocide treatment within EPA label limits, and keeps detailed records of all monitoring, cleaning, and corrective actions. This approach satisfies the overlapping federal, state, and local requirements that govern cooling system biological control.

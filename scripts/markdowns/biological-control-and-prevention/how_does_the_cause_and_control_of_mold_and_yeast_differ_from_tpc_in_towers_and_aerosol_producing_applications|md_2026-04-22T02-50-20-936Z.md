# How Does the Cause and Control of Mold and Yeast Differ from Total Viable Plate Count in Cooling Towers?

## Executive Summary

Mold and yeast problems stem from surface growth in protected niches and require mechanical cleaning, while high total viable plate counts indicate system-wide biological control failure that responds to improved biocide residual and solids management.

## Overview

A cooling tower can show acceptable bulk water bacteria counts while fungal mats quietly colonize fill surfaces and wooden structures. This creates two distinct failure modes that operators often confuse: widespread bacterial contamination versus localized fungal establishment. Wood cooling towers face an additional structural risk when fungi attack load-bearing components.

## Understanding What Each Test Reveals

Total viable plate count measures live, culturable heterotrophs in bulk water samples. This general indicator reveals potential water quality problems but cannot identify specific organisms. A significant TVC increase signals that something has changed in the system's biological control.

Mold and yeast results specifically detect fungal growth. In cooling towers, positive fungal counts often point to surface growth and deposits rather than planktonic organisms. Wood towers showing elevated fungi face potential wood decay and structural damage beyond typical biofouling concerns.

## Root Causes: System-Wide vs. Niche-Driven Problems

Excessive TVC indicates broad "loss of control" conditions affecting the entire system. Insufficient or poorly distributed biocide residual allows bacterial populations to explode. High nutrient loading from airborne dust or process contamination feeds microbial growth. Elevated suspended solids and deposits create protected spaces where bacteria multiply. Stagnation and poor turnover prevent effective biocide contact throughout the system.

Excess fungi develop under more specific conditions that favor fungal establishment over bacterial growth. Persistent wet surfaces and deposits create ideal fungal habitat in slime layers, fouled fill, and basin sludge. High organic loading from airborne debris provides fungal nutrition. Wooden cooling tower components become direct targets for fungal attack, creating structural risks distinct from general microbial fouling.

This creates a critical operational reality: acceptable bulk-water TVC can coexist with problematic fungal mats growing on tower surfaces.

## Control Strategies: Addressing the Right Problem

When TVC exceeds acceptable levels, restore bulk-water microbiological control. Verify biocide feed systems and ensure effective residual control at representative monitoring points. Improve solids and deposit control through optimized blowdown rates, enhanced filtration performance, and regular basin cleaning. Correct operational contributors including stagnant zones and intermittent operation practices. Track TVC trends to confirm that interventions achieve sustained improvement.

Excessive mold and yeast requires removing surface reservoirs and addressing fungi-specific risks. Mechanical cleaning and deposit removal typically provides higher leverage than increasing oxidizer dosing, because fungi persist in protected surface growth within mats and slime layers. Target specific niches by inspecting and cleaning fouled fill, basins, sumps, drift eliminator areas, and low-flow zones where mats establish.

Wood cooling towers demand additional intervention when fungi appear. Fungal control may require wood preservative treatment beyond standard bacterial control measures. Reduce organic loading and sunlight-driven growth through improved housekeeping, intake screening, and maintained filtration systems.

## Practical Diagnosis and Response

High TVC signals that overall biological control is deteriorating. Focus immediately on residual control, solids management, and operational improvements. The problem affects the entire water system and requires systematic intervention.

High mold and yeast indicates surface mats and persistent niches have developed, with possible wood structural risk. Prioritize physical cleaning and niche elimination first, then implement material-specific controls. Only after addressing these physical problems should operators verify that the general biocide program remains stable.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor both TVC and fungi weekly in cooling towers, especially wood systems. When TVC exceeds 10,000 CFU/mL, immediately verify biocide residual and improve solids control. When fungi exceed 100 CFU/mL, schedule mechanical cleaning of fill and basin areas, and inspect wooden components for structural integrity.

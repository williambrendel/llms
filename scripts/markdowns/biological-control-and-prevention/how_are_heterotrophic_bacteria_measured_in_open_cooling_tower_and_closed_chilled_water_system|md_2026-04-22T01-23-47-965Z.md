# How are heterotrophic bacteria measured in open cooling tower and closed chilled water systems?

## Executive Summary

Heterotrophic bacteria are measured primarily through Heterotrophic Plate Count (HPC) in laboratory settings and ATP testing or dip slides for field monitoring, with open cooling towers requiring more frequent field trending while closed systems rely more on periodic lab confirmation.

## Overview

A single water sample can harbor millions of bacteria, yet the methods used to count them in cooling towers versus closed chilled water systems follow completely different strategies. Open towers demand rapid field screening because conditions change hourly, while closed loops can rely on less frequent but more precise laboratory analysis since their environment stays relatively stable.

## Laboratory Methods for Heterotrophic Plate Count (HPC)

Laboratory HPC methods provide the most standardized approach for measuring live, culturable organisms, reported as CFU/mL (colony forming units per milliliter).

**Pour plate and spread plate methods** work by plating a measured sample volume on general nutrient medium. After incubation, technicians count visible colonies and calculate CFU/mL based on the original sample volume.

**Membrane filtration** filters a known volume through a membrane, then places that membrane on agar for incubation and colony counting. Labs choose this method when testing larger volumes or expecting low bacterial counts.

**ISO 6222 colony count** follows international standards for enumerating culturable microorganisms in nutrient agar. This method commonly tests at both 22°C and 37°C, reporting results as CFU/mL.

Some validated methods estimate heterotrophs using **enzyme substrate reactions** rather than colony counting, though these appear less frequently in standard practice.

## Field Methods for Rapid Assessment

Field methods sacrifice precision for speed, allowing operators to catch problems before they become costly failures.

**Dip slides** use sterile slides coated with culture media. Operators dip the slide into tower water, incubate it, then compare colony growth to a reference chart to estimate aerobic bacteria levels. This provides a quick operational indicator of general microbial conditions in cooling systems.

**ATP bioluminescence testing** measures adenosine triphosphate as a proxy for total biological activity, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and other ATP sources. The process involves collecting a water sample, adding reagent, and reading with a luminometer. Results appear as RLU (relative light units) that can convert to ATP concentration using standards.

ATP testing excels at rapid screening and trending of biofouling risk. It detects rising biological activity that might not immediately show up in culture methods. However, various interferences can affect readings, and RLUs are primarily comparable within the same testing program and instrument.

**BART (Biological Activity Reaction Test)** specifically the HAB-BART variant, measures presence and activity of heterotrophic aerobic bacteria through reaction indicators rather than direct CFU counts. Operators fill the BART vial with sample, incubate at ambient conditions, then observe reaction patterns like dye changes over time.

BART provides presence/absence confirmation and activity indication based on reaction timing and patterns. Results are qualitative or semi-quantitative rather than precise CFU/mL measurements, making them useful for simple field confirmation when equipment is limited.

## Application Strategies by System Type

**Open cooling towers** face variable loading and rapid biofilm formation potential, making frequent field trending essential. Most programs combine dip slides for culturable trends, ATP for fast biomass monitoring, and BART for simple activity indication when needed. This multi-method approach helps catch loss of microbial control early, before expensive equipment damage occurs.

**Closed chilled water systems** operate in more stable environments, allowing different measurement strategies. ATP testing works well for rapid screening, especially after outages, maintenance, or when sludge and biofilm are suspected. BART serves as a quick activity check, while dip slides see less use unless the loop experiences known biological activity or frequent contamination from makeup water or leaks.

Laboratory HPC through plate count or membrane filtration provides periodic confirmation for closed systems. Since these loops maintain lower and more stable microbial populations when properly sealed, less frequent but more precise laboratory analysis often proves more cost-effective than constant field monitoring.

## Measurement Limitations and Considerations

HPC methods only capture live, culturable organisms that grow on the specific medium used. Many bacteria in water systems cannot be cultured using standard methods, meaning HPC likely underestimates total bacterial populations.

ATP testing measures total biological activity rather than specifically bacterial populations, so results include fungi, protozoa, and other ATP sources. Various chemical interferences can affect readings, and RLU values are primarily useful for trending within consistent testing programs.

Field methods like dip slides and BART provide operational indicators rather than precise quantification. Their value lies in rapid trending and early warning rather than exact population measurement.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor open cooling towers weekly using dip slides or ATP testing to catch microbial problems before they cause equipment damage. Maintain HPC counts below 10,000 CFU/mL in cooling towers and below 1,000 CFU/mL in closed systems. Consider ATP bioluminescence as your primary field screening tool since it provides results in minutes rather than days required for culture methods.

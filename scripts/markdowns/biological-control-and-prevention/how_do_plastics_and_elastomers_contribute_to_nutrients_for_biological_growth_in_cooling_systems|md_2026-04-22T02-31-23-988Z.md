# How do plastics and elastomers contribute to nutrients for biological growth in cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Plastics and elastomers feed microorganisms in cooling systems by leaching organic chemicals like plasticizers, processing aids, antioxidants, and oils into the water. Studies show these materials release measurable amounts of assimilable organic carbon that bacteria use for growth, with oil-rich elastomers supporting particularly dense biofilm formation compared to metal surfaces.

## Overview

A single cooling tower contains thousands of square feet of polymer surfaces that continuously shed microscopic amounts of organic compounds into the water. Research comparing biofilm growth on different materials found ethylene propylene diene monomer rubber developed significantly denser microbial communities than copper or stainless steel under identical test conditions. The effect becomes pronounced in oil-rich elastomer formulations where microorganisms can directly metabolize rubber oils as a carbon source.

## Plastic Material Nutrient Contribution

Synthetic polymeric materials release dissolved organic molecules that bacteria readily consume. Reviews of plumbing systems demonstrate that plastic pipes and components leach assimilable organic carbon directly into flowing water. A recent wastewater treatment study confirmed that biofilm carbon sources come partially from upstream water chemistry and partially from the plastic infrastructure itself.

The primary contributors include plasticizers that migrate from flexible materials, processing aids used during manufacturing, antioxidants and stabilizers that prevent polymer degradation, and low-molecular-weight oligomers left over from incomplete polymerization. Research on polymeric tubing specifically measured plasticizer migration rates and directly linked these compounds to increased biomass formation potential in laboratory tests.

## Elastomer Material Nutrient Contribution

Rubber compounds leach a broader spectrum of organic compounds than rigid plastics. Materials migration studies document dozens of different organic chemicals released from common elastomer formulations, with many showing direct correlation to increased microbiological activity in water systems.

Oil-rich elastomer formulations present the highest biological risk. Controlled studies on ethylene propylene diene monomer rubber surfaces found that microorganisms both adhered more readily and achieved higher growth rates on oil-rich formulations compared to low-oil or oil-free alternatives. The rubber oils themselves serve as direct carbon and energy sources for bacterial metabolism.

## Cooling System Amplification Effects

Cooling towers magnify these effects through sheer surface area exposure. Modern cooling tower fill materials can provide over 40 square feet of wetted polymer surface per cubic foot of tower volume. Even minimal leaching rates become significant when multiplied across these massive surface areas under continuous water contact.

Elastomer seals, gaskets, and flexible connections create additional risk zones where leached organics concentrate in low-shear environments. These protected micro-zones allow organic compounds to accumulate at surface boundary layers where biofilm formation initiates. The combination of nutrient concentration and flow protection creates ideal conditions for rapid microbial colonization.

## Practical Implications

Monitor dissolved organic carbon levels monthly in systems with extensive polymer components, targeting levels below 1 mg/L to limit biological growth potential. Consider low-leaching polymer formulations specifically designed for water contact applications, particularly for large-surface-area components like cooling tower fill. Replace oil-rich elastomer seals with synthetic alternatives that use mineral fillers rather than organic oils when feasible.

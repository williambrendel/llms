# What is passivation, when is it needed and how is it done?

## Executive Summary

Passivation is the controlled formation of a stable, protective oxide film on metal surfaces to reduce corrosion in cooling systems. It's required at new system startup, after major repairs or cleaning, and after extended downtime, using specific chemical treatments over 24-48 hours for closed loops or 7-14 days for open towers.

## Overview

A properly passivated cooling system can extend equipment life by 30-50%, while skipping this critical step costs far more than the few days it takes. The difference between 1 mpy and 5 mpy corrosion rates means losing an extra 40 mils of steel wall thickness over just 10 years. Most passivation failures happen because someone rushed the startup or skipped the pre-cleaning steps.

## Understanding Passivation

Passivation creates a controlled protective oxide film on metal surfaces, primarily targeting carbon steel in cooling systems. The process forms magnetite (Fe₃O₄) on carbon steel and chromium oxide on stainless steel. This protective layer reduces flash rusting, general corrosion, pitting initiation, and iron transport into system water.

The controlled formation distinguishes passivation from random oxidation. Without proper passivation, metals corrode unpredictably, creating loose iron oxides that settle and accelerate further corrosion through differential aeration cells.

## When Passivation is Required

### Open Cooling Towers

Open systems need passivation at new system startup, after major mechanical repair or tube replacement, after aggressive acid cleaning, and after extended dry layup exceeding 30-60 days. When heavy iron release persists above 3-5 ppm, passivation becomes necessary. Open systems face continuous oxygenation, making proper initial oxide formation critical for long-term protection.

### Closed Loop Systems

Closed loop systems require passivation for new chilled water or hot water systems, after chemical cleaning, after oxygen ingress events, when systems are drained and refilled, and after anticorrosion treatment replacement. These systems typically maintain more stable conditions, but any disruption to the protective environment demands re-passivation.

## Pre-Passivation Cleaning

Passivation fails if oil, grease, mill scale, or biofilm remains on metal surfaces. Oil blocks oxide formation and creates conditions for under-deposit corrosion.

### Oil and Grease Removal

Remove oils using alkaline degreasing with 1-3% sodium metasilicate and 0.5-1% non-foaming surfactant at 120-140°F when possible. Circulate for 4-8 hours. Alternatively, use 1-2% trisodium phosphate (TSP). Continue rinsing until oil drops below 5 mg/L with no visible sheen.

### Mill Scale Removal

Heavy mill scale requires acid cleaning with 3-5% inhibited HCl or 5-10% sulfamic acid. Circulate for 4-8 hours while maintaining inhibitor concentration. Follow with thorough neutralization and rinsing. Mill scale creates localized pitting sites if left in place.

### Debris Removal

Remove construction debris, welding slag, and clean all strainers before beginning passivation procedures.

## Carbon Steel Passivation Methods

### Method 1: Nitrite-Based Passivation (Closed Loops)

This method works best for chilled and hot water systems. Use sodium nitrite at 800-1,200 ppm as NO₂, maintain pH between 8.5-10.0, and keep alkalinity above 100 ppm. Adding 50-100 ppm sodium molybdate provides secondary inhibition.

Fill the system with clean water, adjust pH to 9.0-9.5, and add sodium nitrite to 1,000 ppm. Circulate for 24-48 hours while monitoring nitrite residual daily. Maintain oxygen presence during the formation period. After 48 hours, reduce to operating levels of 600-800 ppm. The process requires minimum 24 hours, with 48 hours preferred, at temperatures between 70-120°F.

### Method 2: High-Alkalinity Oxygen Passivation (Open Towers)

Use this method when nitrite is not acceptable. Maintain pH between 8.5-9.5, calcium hardness at 100-200 ppm, orthophosphate at 5-10 ppm, and keep the Langelier Saturation Index between +0.2 to +0.5. Maintain mild oxidizer residual at 0.5-1.0 ppm free chlorine.

Bring the system online and maintain the specified chemistry for 7-14 days while monitoring iron daily. This method requires minimum 7 days, with 14 days preferred.

## Stainless Steel Passivation

After fabrication or acid cleaning, stainless steel needs chemical passivation. Use 4-10% citric acid at pH 2-3 with 4-24 hours circulation. Alternatively, use 20-25% nitric acid, though this is less common due to safety concerns. Follow with neutralization and thorough rinsing.

## Glycol Systems

Passivate before adding glycol when possible. Use nitrite at 1,000 ppm and circulate for 48 hours. Drain partial volume, then add inhibited glycol with nitrite at 800-1,200 ppm equivalent and pH 8.5-10. Glycol slows oxide formation, making passivation less effective if done after glycol addition.

## Verification and Troubleshooting

Successful passivation shows iron levels dropping below 1-2 ppm for open towers and below 0.5 ppm for closed loops. Look for stable nitrite residual, no flash rusting, no orange discoloration, and corrosion coupon rates below 1-2 mpy for closed loops.

Common failure causes include oil contamination not removed, pH below 8.0, nitrite below 600 ppm, no oxygen present during formation, inadequate circulation, excess chloride above 100 ppm in closed loops, and high copper ion presence.

## Time Requirements and Economics

| **System Type** | **Method** | **Time** |
|---|---|---|
| Closed loop (new) | Nitrite 1,000 ppm | 24–48 hrs |
| Open tower startup | High pH + orthophosphate | 7–14 days |
| After acid cleaning | Nitrite method | 48 hrs |
| Stainless steel | Citric acid | 4–24 hrs |

Poor passivation creates expensive consequences. When corrosion rates reach 5 mpy instead of 1 mpy, steel loses an extra 40 mils over 10 years. For typical pipe walls of 0.120-0.180 inches, this represents 28-40% excess wall loss, dramatically shortening equipment life.

## Key Takeaways

Always pre-clean oils and mill scale, verify pH and inhibitor concentrations, and monitor iron daily during the first week. Document the passivation period and resist pressure to rush startup. Proper passivation takes 24-48 hours for closed loops using 1,000 ppm nitrite at pH 9.0-9.5, or 7-14 days for open towers using high-alkalinity chemistry with 5-10 ppm orthophosphate. Check iron levels weekly until they stabilize below 0.5 ppm for closed systems or 2 ppm for open towers.

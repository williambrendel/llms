# How is glycol added to HVAC systems and what should you test for?

## Executive Summary

Glycol is typically added to HVAC closed loops at 25-50% concentration using transfer pumps, either as a premixed solution or as inhibited concentrate that gets diluted in-system. Regular testing for glycol percentage, pH, reserve alkalinity, and inhibitor levels prevents corrosion from glycol breakdown products.

## Overview

A single drum of inhibited glycol concentrate contains roughly 96% glycol plus corrosion inhibitors, but that concentration drops to 25-50% once diluted in your HVAC system. This dramatic dilution means your inhibitor package gets diluted too—and glycol breaks down over time into acids that can eat your system alive if you're not monitoring the right parameters.

## Adding Glycol to Closed-Loop HVAC Systems

Three methods dominate field practice, depending on whether you're filling an empty system or topping off an existing one.

**Premixed tank method** works best for new installations. Technicians mix glycol with water to target concentration in a clean drum, then pump the mixture into the loop using a transfer pump through fill connections near the expansion tank or pump suction side. Air gets vented during this process.

**Concentrate dilution** suits systems that need major glycol additions. Inhibited glycol concentrates get pumped directly into the system, then circulated while technicians test concentration levels with a refractometer until they hit the target percentage.

**Top-off additions** handle minor volume needs. Small amounts go through the expansion tank or fill connection, followed by circulation and verification testing.

## Glycol Concentration and Inhibitor Chemistry

Commercial inhibited glycol concentrates pack 95-96% glycol plus corrosion inhibitors into drums. Dow's DOWFROST contains "96.0 percent propylene glycol plus corrosion inhibitors," while Chemaqua technical bulletins show inhibited glycols typically contain 4-6% inhibitors with 94-96% glycol making up the remainder.

HVAC systems run at much lower concentrations. Freeze protection requirements drive the final percentage, but most systems operate at 25-50% glycol by volume. The critical floor sits at 25-35% minimum concentration—below this threshold, inhibitor levels drop too low to protect against corrosion.

The inhibitor package in most HVAC inhibited glycols contains three key components: phosphate (often dipotassium phosphate) for buffering and corrosion inhibition, borate for reserve alkalinity, and azoles like benzotriazole for copper-alloy protection.

## Glycol Breakdown and Biocidal Properties

Glycols degrade over time, especially with oxygen ingress and heat exposure. This breakdown produces small organic acids: formic, acetic, glycolic, glyoxylic, oxalic, lactic, and pyruvic acids. These acids lower pH and accelerate corrosion if the inhibitor system can't neutralize them.

Glycol shows limited biocidal activity at HVAC concentrations. While some formulations demonstrate antimicrobial properties under certain conditions, 25-50% glycol solutions shouldn't be treated as self-disinfecting. Some bacteria actually use glycol as a food source, creating contamination and additional acid byproducts. Glycol may provide biostatic effects under specific conditions, but it's not a dependable biocide for closed loops.

## Testing Parameters and Control Ranges

Field and laboratory testing focuses on seven critical parameters:

| Parameter | Purpose | Method |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| **% glycol/freeze point** | Confirms freeze protection and adequate inhibitor levels | Glycol refractometer (field) |
| **pH** | Monitors corrosion control; low pH signals degradation | pH meter/strip (field) |
| **Reserve alkalinity** | Measures buffering capacity against acids | Titration (ASTM D1121 concepts) |
| **Phosphate residual** | Confirms phosphate inhibitor levels | Colorimetric field kit or lab |
| **Azole residual** | Verifies copper-alloy protection | Lab analysis (limited field options) |
| **Metals (Fe, Cu)** | Detects corrosion; rising metals indicate inhibitor depletion | Lab (ICP or equivalent) |
| **Hardness/sulfate** | Prevents inhibitor precipitation and fouling | Lab or field kits |

## Reserve Alkalinity Measurement

Reserve alkalinity measures a glycol solution's buffering capacity—how much acid it can neutralize before pH drops into corrosive ranges.

**What it measures:** The solution's ability to resist pH changes when acids form from glycol breakdown.

Formula:
```
RA = mL of 0.100 N HCl required to titrate 10 mL sample to pH 5.5
```

**Example:** If your glycol sample requires 4.2 mL of 0.100 N HCl to reach pH 5.5, your reserve alkalinity is 4.2, indicating good buffering capacity within the typical 3.0-6.0 target range.

Higher reserve alkalinity means more buffering reserve remains available. Falling reserve alkalinity warns of glycol degradation, contamination, or inhibitor depletion before severe corrosion becomes obvious.

## Control Ranges and Water Quality Requirements

Successful glycol programs maintain these parameters:

| Parameter | Recommended Range |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Minimum inhibited glycol concentration** | ≥ 25% by volume |
| **pH** | 8.0–9.5 |
| **Phosphate inhibitor** | 2,250–3,750 ppm as PO₄ |
| **Azole** | > 150 ppm |
| **Reserve alkalinity** | 3.0–6.0 |

Makeup water quality protects the inhibitor package from precipitation and depletion:
- Calcium hardness < 50 ppm
- Magnesium hardness < 50 ppm  
- Sulfate < 25 ppm

## Key Takeaways

Test glycol concentration monthly with a refractometer and maintain minimum 25% levels to ensure adequate inhibitor protection. Monitor reserve alkalinity quarterly as an early warning system for glycol degradation—falling RA below 3.0 signals the need for inhibitor replenishment or glycol replacement. Consider switching to dedicated glycol-specific inhibitor systems rather than conventional closed-loop treatments, since standard nitrite/molybdate programs don't adequately protect glycol solutions.

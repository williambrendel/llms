# How is legionella risk assessed and documented in open cooling tower and closed chilled water systems?

## Executive Summary

Legionella risk assessment follows a structured Water Management Program approach using CDC toolkits or ASHRAE 188 frameworks, requiring system-specific hazard analysis, defined control measures with limits, and comprehensive monitoring and documentation. Both cooling towers and closed chilled water systems need tailored risk documentation, with towers treated as high-priority due to aerosol dispersion potential and closed loops assessed based on temperature profiles and contamination pathways.

## Overview

A single cooling tower can disperse Legionella-containing aerosols across several city blocks, yet many facilities still rely on reactive testing rather than proactive risk management. The difference between a well-documented Water Management Program and ad-hoc maintenance can mean the difference between preventing an outbreak and explaining one to regulators. Most successful programs share the same DNA whether they follow CDC or ASHRAE 188 guidance—they systematically identify where Legionella could grow, define specific control boundaries, and document everything.

## Water Management Program Framework

Most Legionella risk assessment programs follow the same core structure regardless of whether facilities use CDC toolkits or ASHRAE 188 concepts. Programs create a Water Management Program (WMP), perform system-specific hazard analysis, define control measures with control limits, then monitor, correct, verify, validate, and record all activities.

The step-by-step assessment process begins with defining scope and team responsibilities. Facilities identify responsible parties including owners, operators, facility managers, water treatment vendors, and contractors. Each party's role in the risk management process gets documented upfront.

System description comes next. Teams document complete equipment lists, operating modes covering normal operation, low-load conditions, and startup/shutdown procedures. They catalog water sources including makeup water specifications, chemical additives, and any secondary water inputs.

## Process Documentation and Hazard Analysis

Creating a process flow diagram or schematic represents a core WMP element in CDC guidance. These diagrams show complete water pathways, basins and tanks, low-flow dead legs, bypass lines, sampling points, blowdown locations, and drift eliminators for cooling towers.

Hazard analysis identifies where Legionella could grow and where human exposure could occur. Teams look for conditions supporting bacterial growth and spread: temperatures in the growth range, water stagnation, biofilm formation, scale buildup, sediment accumulation, and inadequate biocide or disinfectant levels. CDC guidance specifically emphasizes these growth drivers for cooling tower systems.

Control measures and control limits get identified and documented with specific parameters. Examples include biocide programs with defined residual levels, scale and corrosion control chemistry, filtration specifications, cleaning and disinfection frequencies, drift control measures, operating setpoints, and detailed startup/shutdown procedures.

## Monitoring and Response Systems

Monitoring plans specify what parameters to measure, sampling locations, measurement frequency, acceptable ranges, and personnel responsible for recording data. These plans connect directly to the identified control limits from the hazard analysis.

Corrective action plans provide pre-written responses when control limits are exceeded. Common triggers include low oxidant residual, high turbidity readings, loss of circulation, high differential pressure across filters, or temperature excursions. Each trigger has a specific response protocol documented in advance.

Verification and validation serve different but complementary functions. Verification confirms that tasks are being performed as written through logs, checklists, calibration records, and inspection documentation. Validation confirms that the overall program actually controls the Legionella hazard, often including review of health outcomes and system performance data.

## Open Cooling Tower Risk Assessment

Cooling towers receive high-priority treatment because they can amplify Legionella populations and disperse contaminated aerosols over wide areas. Risk assessment focuses on several tower-specific areas.

Aerosol exposure pathway analysis examines drift potential and tower location relative to air intakes, public areas, and building occupancy. Water quality drivers include scale formation, corrosion products, sediment accumulation, visible slime or biofilm, basin cleanliness, and sidestream filtration effectiveness.

Biocide control systems require automated control verification, chemical feed system checks, oxidant and non-oxidant strategy coordination, and monitoring under varying demand conditions. Operating mode documentation covers seasonal startup and shutdown procedures, idle cell management, low-load operation, and response to loss-of-flow events.

The typical tower documentation package includes system description with schematic or process flow diagram, complete WMP with control points and control limits, and daily or shift logs covering disinfectant/biocide control parameters, pH, conductivity/blowdown rates, makeup water rates, and basin inspection notes.

Maintenance records document cleaning schedules, disinfection procedures, drift eliminator inspections, filter service intervals, and repair activities. Corrective action reports capture what happened, response actions taken, and re-check verification. Verification and validation records include internal review results and optional Legionella test results if performed.

## Closed Chilled Water System Risk Assessment

True closed-loop systems typically present lower risk than cooling towers, but assessment still documents why risk remains low and identifies conditions that could elevate it. Risk factors include warm temperatures, water stagnation, and contamination entry points.

Closed-loop risk assessment examines whether systems are truly closed by evaluating expansion tank types (open/vented versus sealed), makeup water frequency, known leaks, and maintenance practices involving refills or draining. Temperature profiling identifies any system segments operating in the 20-45°C growth range, including heat recovery sections, warm idle periods, or mixed-loop configurations.

Stagnation zone identification covers bypass lines, inactive coils or air handling units, dead legs, rarely used branches, and seasonal equipment. Biofilm and sludge potential assessment examines corrosion products, filtration effectiveness, side-stream treatment, and chemical control quality.

Closed-loop documentation packages include system description with complete loop schematic showing expansion tanks and makeup connections, treatment plans covering corrosion and scale inhibitors plus biocide strategy if needed, and monitoring logs tracking loop chemistry, clarity/turbidity indicators, makeup volume events, and temperature/flow exceptions.

Maintenance and commissioning procedures document fill/flush protocols, cleaning procedures after maintenance work, and return-to-service checklists. Corrective actions address high makeup events, contamination incidents, sludge discovery, and prolonged shutdown responses.

## Legionella Testing in Risk Documentation

Many successful programs rely primarily on routine monitoring of control parameters—biocide residuals, pH, conductivity, and cleanliness indicators—as their primary evidence of effective risk control rather than direct Legionella testing.

When facilities choose to include Legionella testing, ASHRAE 188 does not prescribe specific methods, frequencies, or sampling sites. The facility team defines these parameters and must also establish response actions for different test results. Testing becomes one tool within the broader risk management framework rather than the primary control measure.

## Key Takeaways

Implement a structured Water Management Program with documented control limits and response procedures, conducting hazard analysis every 12 months or after significant system changes. Monitor biocide residuals daily in cooling towers (maintaining 0.5-1.0 ppm free chlorine or equivalent), and consider automated chemical feed systems with remote monitoring for facilities operating multiple towers or complex closed-loop systems.

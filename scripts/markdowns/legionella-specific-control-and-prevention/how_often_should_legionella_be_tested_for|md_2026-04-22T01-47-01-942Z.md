# How Often Should I Test for Legionella in My Building's Water System?

## Executive Summary

There is no single evidence-based "right" frequency for routine Legionella testing across all buildings. ASHRAE 188 does not prescribe whether to test, how often, or where—it leaves those decisions to the water management team.

## Overview

The CDC states that routine testing over time is the best method to validate a water management program, yet major standards explicitly avoid setting universal testing schedules. High-risk facilities like inpatient healthcare should conduct routine testing, while standard commercial buildings often use baseline testing followed by periodic validation based on their control performance and risk conditions.

## Testing Frequency: A Risk-Based Framework

### High-Risk Facilities and Conditions

The CDC identifies facilities that house or treat higher-risk people as those that may benefit from routine testing. These include buildings that can't meet control limits consistently or have a history of Legionella cases.

The CDC does not give a specific number for testing frequency. You set it based on risk and how stable your controls are.

### Standard Commercial Buildings

Many programs with stable control limits use baseline testing to establish performance, then periodic testing mainly as validation and trending. The frequency remains a local team decision based on your water management program and risk assessment.

### Cooling Towers

Jurisdictions may mandate specific schedules for cooling towers. For example, NYC requires Legionella sampling at least every 90 days for cooling towers. Outside regulated areas, frequency still depends on your water management program and risk conditions.

The bottom line: There is no universally "correct" frequency because major standards and guidance explicitly do not set one.

## Sampling Locations

### Environmental Assessment First

CDC's sampling guidance emphasizes conducting an environmental assessment first, then developing a sampling plan that represents the whole system.

### Common Potable Water Sampling Points

The CDC's potential sampling sites list includes:

- **Incoming water main** (where water enters)
- **Wells or water towers** (if present)
- **Holding tanks and cisterns**
- **Centralized water heater**
- **Hot water expansion tank**
- **Hot and cold water returns**
- **Before and after softeners, filters, or disinfection systems**
- **Representative distal outlets**: showers and faucets (choose proximal and distal to risers/heaters; sample across floors/wings)

### Aerosol-Generating Endpoints

For showers and faucets, the CDC notes collecting both a biofilm swab and bulk water from each site during investigation-style sampling.

### Cooling Towers and Other Devices

The CDC provides device-specific site lists, as components vary by system, and advises engaging someone familiar with the tower to pick appropriate sampling points.

## Sampling Methods

### Planning and Assessment

Conduct a system environmental assessment examining sediment/biofilm, temperature, water age, and disinfectant residual before creating your sampling plan.

### Sample Type and Volume

For routine testing, the CDC states that typically 250 mL is sufficient. Larger volumes or swabs may add information for at-risk facilities. For investigation-style protocols, the CDC often uses 1 L bulk water plus swabs at outlets.

### Field Parameters and Neutralization

CDC's sampling protocol uses sodium thiosulfate to neutralize residual disinfectants. Measure temperature, pH, and chlorine at sampling sites during collection.

### Outlet Sampling Procedure

Remove showerheads or aerators, collect biofilm swabs and bulk water, and avoid overheating the sampled water at collection.

### Laboratory Considerations

The CDC highlights that methods differ—culture versus qPCR, antigen testing, and others—and results alone don't directly measure health risk. Use results as performance indicators within your water management program.

## Key Takeaways

Establish your testing frequency based on your facility's risk tier and control performance history rather than searching for a universal standard. High-risk facilities should test routinely, while standard commercial buildings can use baseline testing followed by periodic validation. For cooling towers, check local regulations first—many jurisdictions mandate specific schedules like NYC's 90-day requirement. Focus on representative sampling locations across your system and use results as performance indicators within your broader water management program.

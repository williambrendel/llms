# What are typical disinfection options for Legionella?

## Executive Summary

Nine main disinfection options exist for Legionella control, ranging from shock chlorination and thermal disinfection for immediate response to copper-silver ionization for long-term maintenance. Shock chlorination with 20-50 mg/L free chlorine ranks highest for immediate effectiveness, while several non-oxidizing biocides work only in non-potable systems.

## Overview

A single Legionella outbreak can shut down an entire facility within days, yet the disinfection method that works fastest might not be the one that prevents future problems. The most aggressive approach—shock chlorination at 50 mg/L—can achieve high immediate kill rates, but its effectiveness drops significantly as pH climbs toward 9 due to reduced hypochlorous acid formation.

## Immediate Response Options

**Shock chlorination** leads the pack for emergency response in potable water systems. This approach uses 20-50 mg/L free chlorine maintained for 1-2 hours before flushing the system. The method delivers high immediate effectiveness at pH 7.5, though performance degrades as pH approaches 9 because less hypochlorous acid forms at higher pH levels.

**Thermal disinfection** offers another rapid response option, though the CDC does not recommend it for routine control. The process involves raising system temperatures to achieve 55-60°C at all outlets, followed by 10-30 minute flushing periods. While this method can produce high to moderate immediate reduction when temperatures reach all system areas, Legionella populations typically rebound without sustained controls.

## Industrial and Cooling Tower Solutions

**DBNPA** provides fast-kill capability specifically for cooling towers and industrial water systems, not potable water. Typical applications involve 5-30 ppm doses applied 2-3 times weekly. This non-oxidizing biocide achieves high immediate kill rates when properly matched to system demand, but efficacy drops above pH 8.5.

**Glutaraldehyde** serves as another non-oxidizing option for cooling towers, requiring approximately 100 ppm active ingredient maintained for 6 hours of contact time. This method produces moderate to high effectiveness, though it works slower than oxidizers and depends heavily on contact time and existing biofilm load.

**Isothiazolinone** rounds out the industrial biocide options with typical doses of 60-100 ppm. Effectiveness varies significantly based on specific product chemistry, dose calculation method, and system demand characteristics.

## Supplemental Disinfection Methods

**Chlorine dioxide** functions as a supplemental disinfectant in facilities where byproduct formation can be managed. The EPA maximum residual disinfectant level caps this option at 0.8 mg/L. Chlorine dioxide maintains moderate to high immediate effectiveness across wider pH ranges than free chlorine, but success depends on maintaining adequate residuals while managing chlorite and chlorate formation.

**Lower-dose, longer-hold chlorination** applies CT-based hyperchlorination concepts for controlled remediation scenarios. A typical target involves achieving 3,000 mg-min/L contact time—for example, maintaining 2.2 mg/L residual for 24 hours. This approach produces moderate to high effectiveness without rapid action, relying on achieving the full CT value throughout the entire system.

## Long-Term Control Systems

**Monochloramine** works better for ongoing control than rapid remediation. The EPA allows up to 4.0 mg/L as chlorine in potable systems. This disinfectant provides moderate effectiveness with slow action, excelling at maintaining residuals in distribution systems rather than immediate knockdown. Success requires careful control to prevent nitrification issues.

**Copper-silver ionization** offers gradual, long-term control popular in healthcare facilities. Systems typically maintain copper levels between 0.2-0.8 mg/L and silver concentrations from 0.01-0.08 mg/L. This method produces moderate effectiveness over extended timeframes rather than rapid shock treatment, with performance depending on water chemistry and distribution system characteristics.

## Regulatory and Safety Constraints

Two critical constraints prevent misuse of these disinfection options. EPA maximum residual disinfectant levels limit chlorine and chloramine to 4.0 mg/L each, while chlorine dioxide cannot exceed 0.8 mg/L in potable water systems. Non-oxidizing biocides including glutaraldehyde, isothiazolinone, and DBNPA are restricted to cooling tower and industrial applications—never potable water disinfection.

## Key Takeaways

Choose shock chlorination for immediate Legionella response in potable systems, maintaining 20-50 mg/L free chlorine for 1-2 hours before flushing. Monitor effectiveness monthly through Legionella testing, targeting complete elimination in the immediate term. For long-term prevention, consider copper-silver ionization in healthcare settings or monochloramine residuals in distribution systems, keeping pH below 8.5 to maximize disinfectant effectiveness.

# How Do You Control Legionella Risk When Reopening Buildings After Shutdown or Low Occupancy?

## Executive Summary

Building shutdowns and low occupancy periods create ideal conditions for Legionella growth across all water systems, requiring systematic flushing, cleaning, and verification procedures before occupants return. Each system type—cooling towers, closed loops, and potable water—faces distinct challenges that demand specific control strategies.

## Overview

A six-month building closure can transform a well-managed water system into a Legionella amplification site. Stagnant water loses its disinfectant protection, temperatures drift into the bacterial growth sweet spot, and biofilms accumulate undisturbed. The CDC explicitly warns that these conditions create hazards for returning occupants, but the control strategies vary dramatically depending on whether you're dealing with an open cooling tower that sprays aerosols or a closed chilled water loop.

## Cross-Cutting Challenges During Shutdown and Reopening

Reduced occupancy and shutdown conditions push all building water systems toward the main Legionella drivers: higher water age and stagnation, loss of disinfectant residual, temperature drift into the growth range (77-108°F), and increased biofilm and sediment impacts.

The CDC explicitly warns that prolonged shutdown or reduced operation creates hazards, including Legionella, for returning occupants. These universal challenges affect every water system in the building, though the exposure pathways and control responses differ significantly.

## Open Cooling Towers Present the Highest Risk

Evaporative cooling towers face the most severe challenges during low-load or shutdown periods. The combination of warm, recirculating water and intermittent operation creates dead zones where biocide contact weakens and deposits accumulate. The CDC emphasizes that scale, corrosion, and sediment control combined with system cleaning are critical, and that disinfectant residual should be monitored and adjusted—often with automation.

Idle and seasonal layup periods create particular risks. When towers are idled and then restarted, biofilm, debris, and low biocide coverage can allow amplification and then aerosolization at restart. The CDC's cooling tower toolkit guidance explicitly calls for cleaning and disinfection practices before startup, when idling, and after shutdown.

The aerosol exposure pathway remains active throughout the reopening process. A tower returning to service can expose both occupants and the surrounding community if control is lost during the restart phase.

**Key control actions for cooling tower reopening:**
- Establish written layup and startup procedures that include cleaning as needed, confirming treatment system operation, establishing circulation, and verifying disinfectant control before returning to normal operation
- Re-establish and verify residual biocide control and deposit control with documented checks

## Closed-Loop Chilled Water Systems Require Different Approaches

Closed HVAC loops present lower Legionella exposure risk since they're not designed to aerosolize water. However, extended idle periods still cause microbial growth, biofilm development, and deposit and corrosion issues that can destabilize control when restarted.

Risk increases significantly if the loop isn't truly closed—such as systems with open tanks, frequent drain and refill events, or components that can aerosolize. The CDC's cooling tower materials highlight why open atmospheric contact matters for Legionella growth and spread in tower systems, but do not provide the same prescriptive reopening checklist for closed chilled loops as for potable systems.

**Key control actions for closed loop reopening:**
- Keep systems closed by minimizing open interfaces and managing makeup and fill events with documented procedures
- Maintain treatment and circulation as designed, avoid long-term isolated stagnant branches where possible, and confirm treatment control after downtime

## Potable Hot Water Systems Need Temperature and Flow Restoration

Hot water systems face immediate challenges when flow and recirculation decrease. Hot water cools into the Legionella growth range in low-use branches, creating tepid zones perfect for bacterial amplification. The CDC identifies reduced use as a hazard for reopening buildings.

Disinfectant residual decays with higher water age, especially in distal areas, while biofilm can persist and reseed the system after restart. The EPA explicitly notes that low or no use creates stagnant water conditions and recommends checking disinfectant levels and temperature as part of restoration.

Reopening creates additional complications through mixing and sloughing. Restarting flow can release accumulated sediment and biofilm into the water stream, temporarily worsening water quality at the point of use.

**Key control actions for hot water reopening:**
- Implement structured flushing by starting near the building entry and moving to the farthest outlets, checking temperature and disinfectant parameters to confirm fresh water reaches points of use
- Verify hot water recovery at outlets through temperature stabilization as evidence of restored circulation and reduced stagnation

## Potable Cold Water Systems Face Temperature and Residual Challenges

Cold water systems develop problems when stagnant plumbing allows cold water to warm, especially in heated mechanical spaces, pushing temperatures toward the Legionella growth range. Residual disinfectant decays with water age, so distal cold outlets can lose their protective chlorine or chloramine levels.

First-draw water after reopening often shows degraded quality with higher metals and particulates. The EPA discusses checking water quality parameters after flushing to verify system restoration.

**Key control actions for cold water reopening:**
- Flush cold water first and verify stable temperature and disinfectant levels as indicators of adequate system flushing
- Prioritize low-use zones such as wings or floors that sat idle longest for targeted flushing and verification

## Takeaway

Implement a phased reopening protocol that flushes cold water first, verifies hot water temperature recovery at all outlets, and confirms disinfectant residual levels weekly for the first month. For cooling towers, establish automated biocide monitoring and require documented cleaning before any restart after more than 72 hours of shutdown.

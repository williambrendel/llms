# What Should You Do After Getting a Positive Legionella Test Result?

## Executive Summary

A positive Legionella test confirms the organism's presence in your water system but doesn't automatically mean people will get sick or that you must shut down the building. The appropriate response depends on your risk context, whether control measures were functioning, and local regulations—ranging from targeted outlet restrictions to system-wide corrections.

## Overview

Finding Legionella in a water sample can trigger panic and hasty decisions that may be unnecessary or even counterproductive. The CDC emphasizes that environmental detection alone doesn't predict disease risk, yet many facility managers face pressure to take dramatic action immediately. The key lies in understanding what the result actually means and responding proportionally to the specific risk factors in your situation.

## Understanding What a Positive Result Actually Means

A positive Legionella test provides evidence that the organism exists somewhere in your water system at the time of sampling. However, the CDC clearly states that finding Legionella doesn't necessarily mean people will get sick. Environmental test results alone cannot predict disease risk.

The detection confirms presence but doesn't prove that an exposure occurred, that illness will result, or that the building poses immediate danger to occupants. This distinction becomes crucial when deciding how aggressively to respond.

## Building Shutdown: When It's Actually Necessary

Most positive results don't require building-wide shutdowns. Facilities typically reserve this extreme measure for specific high-risk scenarios:

**Suspected or confirmed disease cases** linked to the building trigger public health investigations that may recommend closure. **High-risk settings** like inpatient healthcare facilities with results suggesting broad system failure often warrant more aggressive action. **High-aerosol devices** such as hot tubs or decorative fountains require immediate attention—the CDC indicates any detection of viable Legionella in these systems should prompt restricting use while implementing corrections.

In routine situations, targeted controls prove more effective than blanket shutdowns. This approach focuses on restricting specific outlets or devices showing contamination, correcting underlying system conditions, and resampling to confirm control.

## Notification Requirements: Know Your Context

Notification obligations depend heavily on why you tested and your local jurisdiction. If testing occurred due to suspected or confirmed Legionnaires' disease cases, public health departments typically lead investigations and notification becomes required under disease reporting rules that vary by state and locality.

For routine validation testing with no known cases, the CDC doesn't mandate notifying authorities for every positive result. Instead, it emphasizes that building managers must act appropriately and inform people at increased risk when situations warrant precautions.

Specific regulations add another layer. Some jurisdictions require defined sampling, actions, and reporting—particularly for cooling towers. New York City, for example, operates a comprehensive cooling tower regulatory program with mandatory sampling intervals and response protocols.

## Systematic Response Framework

**Confirm the result's meaning** by examining the testing method used. PCR can detect DNA from non-viable organisms while culture indicates viable growth but suffers from false negatives. Determine whether results show Legionella pneumophila specifically or Legionella species generally. Review sampling conditions including pre-flush versus post-flush timing and whether samples included biofilm swabs or water only.

**Assess immediate risk context** by evaluating occupant vulnerability. Inpatient healthcare settings and immunocompromised populations increase response urgency. Consider aerosol potential since showers, cooling towers, and certain devices generate inhalable droplets that raise concern. Examine the extent of contamination—single sites versus multiple locations across different zones reveal important patterns.

**Evaluate control measure performance** by checking whether your water management plan's controls were functioning properly. Hot water temperature control at storage, recirculation, and distal points often shows the first signs of system problems. Cold water temperature control prevents warming into Legionella's growth range. Disinfectant residual levels and evidence of decay indicate water age issues. Stagnation indicators reveal problems in low-use areas, dead legs, and low-flow branches.

## Corrective Actions and Communication

Corrective actions should match the situation's severity. Restore control limits for temperature, residual, flow, and biocide programs when monitoring reveals deficiencies. Clean and maintain implicated components to address biofilm, scale, and sediment buildup. Manage use of contaminated outlets or devices by restricting access to certain showers or equipment while implementing corrections. Escalate response when results show widespread contamination, increasing levels, or occur in high-risk settings.

Communication decisions require internal escalation per your water management plan to facilities leadership and infection prevention or environmental health and safety teams. Occupant communications become necessary when situations warrant precautions, especially for high-risk populations. Public health notification applies when cases exist, outbreaks are suspected, or local regulations require reporting.

## Validation and Ongoing Monitoring

Resampling confirms that corrective actions restored system control. Base your validation approach on your water management plan's requirements and any applicable regulations to understand contamination trends and extent.

ASHRAE 188 requires program teams to define their plans but doesn't prescribe specific testing frequencies or action levels. Teams set these parameters based on their facility's characteristics and local regulatory requirements.

## Key Takeaway

Respond to positive Legionella results by first confirming what the test actually detected, then implementing targeted corrections based on your system's control failures rather than defaulting to building shutdown. Monitor hot water temperatures monthly at storage tanks and quarterly at distal outlets, maintaining storage above 140°F and returns above 120°F, while considering chlorine dioxide or copper-silver ionization for systems that struggle with temperature-based control.

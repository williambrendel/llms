# Why Does Water Stagnation Increase Legionella Risk?

## Executive Summary

Water stagnation increases Legionella risk by creating a perfect storm of conditions: disinfectant residuals decay, temperatures drift into the bacterial growth range (77–113°F), and biofilm builds up to provide habitat and protection for the bacteria.

## Overview

A building's water system can transform from protective to dangerous in just days of stagnation. The CDC identifies four critical factors that stagnation directly attacks: sediment and biofilm accumulation, temperature control, water age, and disinfectant residual levels. When water stops moving, these protective barriers collapse in sequence, creating conditions where Legionella thrives.

## How Stagnation Damages Core Growth Controls

Stagnation increases Legionella risk by systematically undermining the conditions that normally suppress bacterial growth:

**Disinfectant decays** when water sits without replenishment. The CDC notes that warm temperatures, water age, and sediment all accelerate the depletion of disinfectant residuals. Stagnant plumbing often shows low or undetectable disinfectant levels.

**Temperatures drift into the growth range** as hot water cools and cold water warms. The CDC specifically identifies how stagnant water allows hot water temperatures to fall into Legionella's preferred growth range of 77–113°F (25–42°C).

**Biofilm and sediment build** in areas where water movement cannot sweep surfaces clean. These deposits provide both habitat and protection for bacteria while consuming available disinfectant.

The CDC identifies these interconnected factors as "Sediment and biofilm, Temperature, water Age, and disinfectant Residual" — the key variables affecting Legionella growth in potable water systems.

## Open Cooling Towers: High-Risk Stagnation Zones

Open cooling towers face the greatest stagnation risks because they combine warm water, nutrients, and aerosolization potential.

**Dead zones create perfect breeding grounds.** Sump corners, offline piping, bypassed heat exchangers, and idle pumps allow warm, nutrient-rich water to sit undisturbed. These areas accelerate biofilm and sediment accumulation while receiving weak biocide exposure.

**Intermittent operation compounds the problem.** Seasonal or standby towers present especially high risk because the system remains warm and wet without consistent treatment or circulation. This common scenario appears repeatedly in cooling tower guidance documents as a critical risk condition.

**Effective stagnation control requires multiple approaches:**

- **Keep water moving** through continuous circulation and elimination of dead legs
- **Treat and monitor continuously** with automated systems that maintain disinfectant residual based on operating conditions  
- **Maintain physical cleanliness** through routine removal of sediment, biofilm, scale, and corrosion deposits
- **Follow written layup procedures** when taking towers offline, including cleaning, disinfection, circulation, and chemistry verification before restart

## Closed-Loop Chilled Water: Lower Risk, Same Principles

Closed chilled water loops typically present low Legionella exposure risk because they avoid aerosolization and atmospheric contact. However, stagnation still drives microbiological growth and operational problems.

**Risk emerges at open interfaces.** Open expansion tanks, cross-connections, maintenance fill points, or drained sections left wet can create exposure pathways where the same stagnation mechanisms apply.

**Control focuses on maintaining system integrity:**

- **Maintain designed circulation** and avoid isolating branches for extended periods
- **Keep the system truly closed** by eliminating open tanks, maintaining backflow protection, and controlling makeup water quality
- **Use documented water treatment programs** that address corrosion and biological control, especially during low-load periods

## Potable Hot Water: Temperature and Residual Control

Hot water systems face dual stagnation risks as temperatures drop and disinfectant disappears.

**Stagnation attacks both primary defenses.** Hot water cools into the growth range while disinfectant residual drops, creating conditions the CDC explicitly links to increased Legionella growth and spread in plumbing systems.

**Control strategies target water age and temperature maintenance:**

- **Control water age** by removing dead legs, managing low-use areas, and implementing systematic flushing programs
- **Maintain temperatures at distal points** to ensure hot water stays hot throughout the distribution system
- **Track disinfectant residual** from point-of-entry to point-of-use to detect water age issues

The EPA specifically notes that low or no use creates stagnant water and optimal growth conditions when hot water temperatures decrease and disinfectant residuals decline.

## Potable Cold Water: Preventing Warming and Decay

Cold water systems must prevent both temperature rise and disinfectant loss during stagnation periods.

**Stagnation warms cold water** toward the growth range while allowing disinfectant residual to decay. The CDC highlights how stagnant conditions lead to low disinfectant levels that support Legionella growth.

**Prevention requires active temperature and chemical control:**

- **Reduce water age** through flushing plans for low-use areas and dead leg removal
- **Keep cold water cold** by controlling heat gain through insulation, routing, and temperature verification at representative outlets
- **Track residual loss** from entry points to distal locations as an operational indicator of high water age

## Key Takeaways

Monitor disinfectant residual weekly at both entry and distal points — residual loss greater than 50% indicates dangerous water age conditions. Maintain hot water above 120°F and cold water below 68°F at the furthest outlets. When systems show stagnation signs, implement automated circulation controls or consider point-of-use treatment technologies like UV disinfection for high-risk areas.

# How Does Construction and Renovation Increase Legionella Risk in Water Systems?

## Executive Summary

Construction and renovation projects increase Legionella risk by creating or worsening the four CDC-identified growth drivers: sediment and biofilm formation, temperature fluctuations, water stagnation, and disinfectant residual loss. City water changes during projects compound these risks through disinfectant instability, nitrification, hydraulic disturbances, and chemistry shifts that disrupt existing control measures.

## Overview

A single construction project can transform a well-controlled water system into a Legionella breeding ground within weeks. New pipe sections sitting unused for months, cooling towers filled with construction debris, and hot water systems losing temperature control create perfect conditions for bacterial amplification. Even city water changes—seemingly beyond your control—can destabilize disinfectant residuals and trigger nitrification events that undermine years of careful water management.

## Project-Driven Risk Factors

Most construction-related Legionella risk stems from disrupting the four CDC growth drivers that normally keep bacterial populations in check. Each driver creates specific challenges that require targeted controls.

### Stagnation and Increased Water Age

New pipe sections create the most predictable risk during construction. Dead legs sit unused during commissioning delays, temporary bypasses isolate entire building sections, and capped branches eliminate flow entirely. These low-flow and no-flow zones allow water age to increase dramatically, giving Legionella time to multiply.

Design teams should eliminate dead legs where possible and verify field conditions match drawings. Planned flushing becomes critical for affected branches, outlets, and pipe runs—include specific frequencies during construction and document a complete "return-to-service" flush protocol. Rebalance recirculation systems after any alterations, particularly domestic hot water return loops, cooling tower cell balancing, and pump operating schedules.

### Disinfectant Residual Loss

Certain equipment accelerates disinfectant decay, creating vulnerable zones downstream. The CDC specifically identifies UV devices, water softeners, carbon filters, and heaters as components that speed residual loss. Construction debris and organic contamination further increase disinfectant demand, overwhelming existing chemical feed systems.

Identify residual-consuming components during design review and add monitoring points upstream and downstream of each device. Increase monitoring frequency during commissioning and after tie-ins to catch residual loss early. Verify that chemical feed and control systems operate correctly—cooling towers should monitor and adjust disinfectant residual automatically rather than relying on manual dosing.

### Temperature Changes and Stratification

Hot water temperature drops in distant areas while cold water warms in unconditioned spaces, creating the 68-122°F range where Legionella thrives. Oversized or poorly mixed storage tanks develop temperature gradients that provide ideal growth conditions.

Maintain proper insulation to preserve hot and cold temperatures throughout the distribution system. Place mixing valves according to CDC design recommendations and right-size storage tanks to prevent stratification. Recommission recirculation controls after system alterations to ensure temperature targets remain achievable.

### Sediment and Biofilm Formation

Construction activities introduce particulates through cutting, drilling, and disturbing existing surfaces. New piping contributes scale and corrosion products, while dirty cooling towers provide abundant nutrients for biofilm development. These deposits protect Legionella from disinfectants and create amplification sites.

Implement strict cleanliness protocols during tie-ins and flush systems thoroughly before placing them into service. Cooling towers require particular attention—follow manufacturer guidance for cleaning and disinfection prior to commissioning, before startup, when idling, and after shutdown. Perform at least annual offline disinfection and cleaning, and consider filtration where suspended solids loads remain high.

## Critical Project Phases

### Commissioning and Startup Events

Cooling towers started after idle periods present elevated risk if treatment and turnover aren't maintained during downtime. Newly renovated building zones often experience low occupancy after handover, creating stagnation conditions just when systems should prove their effectiveness.

Maintain cooling tower treatment during standby periods and prevent stagnation by flushing low-flow areas at least weekly. Manage blowdown appropriately and maintain recirculation during intermittent operation. Building potable systems need structured "return to service" plans that verify temperature and residual targets while addressing low-flow fixtures—the CDC identifies these mechanically complex fixtures as higher risk.

### System Complexity Increases

Sensor faucets and complex fixtures increase stagnation risk through low-flow operation and internal dead spaces. New treatment devices like filters, softeners, and UV systems can reduce residual or create bacterial niches if not properly maintained.

Pre-approve fixtures and devices against Legionella risk criteria and require maintenance plus flushing plans for each added component. Add sampling and monitoring ports to support ongoing verification and validation of control measures.

## City Water Disruptions

### Disinfectant Changes and Instability

Utilities may temporarily switch between chlorine and chloramine disinfection or boost residual levels during emergency responses. These changes alter incoming residual levels, decay rates, and byproduct formation, making existing building control points ineffective.

Treat disinfectant changes as Management of Change events in your Water Management Program. Verify residual at the meter or a standard checkpoint and compare levels to expected values. The CDC specifically recommends coordinating with local water utilities and checking disinfectant residual entering buildings. Increase monitoring frequency for disinfectant, temperature, and cooling tower chemistry parameters until stability returns.

### Nitrification in Chloraminated Systems

Chloramine breakdown releases ammonia and contributes to nitrification, which degrades water quality and destroys disinfectant persistence. This process can accelerate during construction when systems experience stress from debris, temperature fluctuations, and altered flow patterns.

Track early warning indicators including utility notices, declining total chlorine residual, rising nitrite and nitrate levels, and customer complaints. Coordinate with utility flushing and disinfectant adjustments while implementing structured building flushing and verification testing consistent with your Water Management Program.

### Hydraulic Disturbances

Main breaks, pressure events, and large-scale utility flushing programs mobilize sediment and disturb biofilm throughout the distribution system. These particles increase disinfectant demand and foul strainers and aerators, creating conditions that support Legionella growth.

Implement a utility disturbance response standard operating procedure that includes inspecting and cleaning strainers and point-of-use devices where particulates appear. Flush systematically from the service entrance outward and verify disinfectant residual and temperature after flushing activities.

### Source Water Chemistry Shifts

Changes in source blending, treatment processes, or corrosion control alter scaling and corrosion tendencies, increasing deposits that protect biofilm from disinfectants. These shifts often coincide with construction projects when monitoring attention focuses elsewhere.

Add temporary monitoring aligned to your system type. Open cooling systems require watching for rapid changes in makeup conductivity and alkalinity that affect scale and corrosion control programs. Building potable systems need verification of temperature and residual stability at representative points, with updated flushing protocols if decay rates change significantly.

## Implementation Framework

### Pre-Project Planning

Update water system diagrams to identify new dead legs and low-flow risks before construction begins. Define temporary operating conditions including shutdowns and bypasses, and establish monitoring plans for temperature and residual in potable systems plus disinfectant residual and chemistry in cooling towers.

### During Construction

Track isolation periods and execute scheduled flushing and turnover procedures. Control debris introduction and verify chemical feed and automation systems where applicable. Document any deviations from planned operating conditions.

### Post-Project Commissioning

Perform cleaning and disinfection steps appropriate to each system type—cooling towers have explicit pre-commissioning and startup guidance that must be followed. Verify that control limits are met and remain stable, including temperature, residual, and water age indicators. Document corrective actions and update the Water Management Program to reflect new "as-operated" conditions.

## Water Management Program Integration

Include a Water Utility Change and City Water Disturbance trigger in your Legionella minimization program with specific activation criteria: utility notices of disinfectant changes, main breaks, boil water advisories, pressure events, sudden residual shifts at the meter, and significant turbidity or particulate observations.

Required response actions should include coordinating with the utility, implementing increased monitoring frequencies, executing systematic flushing procedures, performing device maintenance, maintaining documentation, and establishing criteria to return to normal monitoring. The CDC's Water Management Program toolkit supports continuous review and prompt intervention when deviations arise, making these protocols auditable and defensible.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor disinfectant residual at building entry points weekly during construction and daily during commissioning phases, maintaining minimum 0.2 mg/L free chlorine or 0.5 mg/L total chlorine. Flush dead legs and low-flow areas at least weekly during construction, with complete system flushing before return to service. Consider automated monitoring and control systems for cooling towers rather than manual chemical dosing, as construction activities can rapidly overwhelm manual adjustment capabilities.

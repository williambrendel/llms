# What is the role of biofilm in legionella control and how do we remove/control it?

## Executive Summary

Biofilm creates a protective habitat where Legionella multiplies while shielding bacteria from disinfectants, making it the primary barrier to effective Legionella control. Successful biofilm management requires layered prevention strategies combining physical removal, temperature control, and disinfectant residuals rather than relying on chemical treatment alone.

## Overview

A single biofilm colony can consume enough disinfectant to eliminate protective residuals throughout an entire building's water system. This slimy matrix of microorganisms doesn't just harbor Legionella—it actively sabotages the chemical and thermal treatments designed to kill it. Even successful disinfection events often fail long-term because remaining biofilm reseeds the system within weeks.

## The Biofilm Protection Mechanism

Biofilm forms as a slimy layer of microorganisms and organic/inorganic material that adheres to pipe and fixture surfaces. This matrix creates multiple barriers to Legionella control that work simultaneously against building water safety programs.

Legionella lives and multiplies within biofilms and inside amoebae that inhabit biofilm communities. The biofilm matrix shields these bacteria from disinfectants and environmental stress that would otherwise kill them in open water. This protection allows Legionella to survive even aggressive chemical treatments.

The biofilm and its associated organic matter consume disinfectant as it flows through the system. This consumption reduces the disinfectant residual that reaches distant points in the building—one of the main factors tied to Legionella growth in premise plumbing systems.

After disinfection events, remaining biofilm reseeds the system with new bacterial growth. This explains why many "one-time" shock treatments fail to provide lasting control without ongoing system management of temperature, disinfectant levels, water age, and maintenance.

Biofilm also traps sediment and scale, creating additional surface area for bacterial colonization. Sediment and biofilm are specifically identified as key factors influencing Legionella growth in potable water systems.

## Chemical Treatment Options for Biofilm Control

Different biocides show varying effectiveness against biofilm, particularly in the pH range of 7.5–9 common in many building water systems.

### Oxidizing Biocides

**Sodium hypochlorite (free chlorine)** provides rapid knockdown of planktonic bacteria in potable water remediation and some industrial systems. However, free chlorine becomes less potent as pH increases due to the shift from hypochlorous acid (HOCl) to hypochlorite ion (OCl⁻), and biofilm can protect organisms even from high chlorine concentrations.

**Chlorine dioxide (ClO₂)** is often selected specifically for biofilm penetration and maintains effectiveness across a broader pH range than free chlorine. It works in potable supplemental systems and industrial applications where biofilm is a primary concern.

**Monochloramine (NH₂Cl)** maintains disinfectant residuals deeper into distribution systems than free chlorine. While not a fast biofilm removal agent, it can reduce regrowth over time when used in municipal residual programs or building supplemental systems.

### Non-Oxidizing Biocides for Industrial Systems

**Tetrakis (hydroxymethyl) phosphonium sulfate (THPS)** targets microbial control in cooling towers and industrial water where biofilm and microbiologically influenced corrosion are concerns. Laboratory studies show it can be part of effective anti-biofilm treatments when properly paired and optimized.

**DBNPA (2,2-dibromo-3-nitrilopropionamide)** acts quickly against biofilm in cooling towers and industrial water systems. Its efficacy decreases as pH rises above 8.5, which is important to consider within the 7.5–9 pH range.

**Isothiazolinones** (commonly CMIT/MIT, also BIT/OIT) provide broad-spectrum biofouling control in cooling towers and industrial water. Research shows that biocide-biofilm interactions can be complex, with immediate effects varying by system and product formulation.

**Glutaraldehyde** works for microbial control in cooling towers and industrial water but acts more slowly than fast-kill oxidizers or DBNPA. Performance depends heavily on contact time and existing deposit load.

**Bronopol (2-bromo-2-nitropropane-1,3-diol)** addresses industrial biofouling and microbiologically influenced corrosion in cooling towers, with peer-reviewed research supporting its use in cooling-water microbial control contexts.

**Copper-silver ionization** provides ongoing antimicrobial action in potable water systems, particularly in healthcare facilities. This method targets long-term suppression rather than rapid biofilm removal, and its effectiveness is influenced by water chemistry, scale formation, and existing biofilm.

### Biofilm Dispersants

These chemicals break up biofilm matrix to help biocides penetrate and work more effectively. They function as adjuncts rather than stand-alone disinfectants and are most commonly used in cooling water programs.

**Surfactant/penetrant blends** use nonionic and anionic surfactants to penetrate the biofilm matrix and detach deposits through sloughing action in cooling tower systems.

**Dispersant polymers** including polyacrylates, polymethacrylates, and sulfonated polymers keep loosened solids and organics dispersed to prevent redeposition and support removal through blowdown and filtration.

**D-amino acids** represent an emerging approach where specific amino acid mixtures disrupt biofilm signaling. Laboratory studies show these compounds can enhance biocide performance when used with THPS, isothiazoline derivatives, or bleach against biofilm consortia.

## Practical Biofilm Control Strategy

No single "biofilm remover" exists. Effective control requires layered prevention strategies that address multiple factors simultaneously.

### Primary Prevention

Reduce stagnation and water age by removing dead legs, managing low-use areas, and implementing regular flushing where needed. Keep temperatures out of the bacterial growth range for controllable parts of the system—hot water stays hot, cold water stays cold.

Maintain disinfectant residual where applicable, since loss of residual is a major driver of Legionella growth. The residual must reach distal points in the system to be effective.

### Physical Removal

Mechanical cleaning provides the most reliable biofilm removal for components that can be accessed and cleaned, including strainers, tanks, cooling tower basins, and accessible fixtures. Physical removal is often necessary because biofilm resists chemical-only approaches.

Descale and remove sediment regularly, since scale, sediment, and biofilm reinforce each other's growth and persistence.

### Chemical Control

Use disinfection chemistry as a control tool rather than a stand-alone cure. Disinfection events can reduce planktonic organisms and weaken biofilm, but long-term control depends on maintaining unfavorable system conditions through temperature management, disinfectant residuals, adequate flow, and regular maintenance.

### Verification and Validation

Verification confirms that program activities occurred as planned, including flushing schedules, maintenance tasks, and temperature/residual checks. Validation uses trend data and Legionella testing where appropriate to confirm the system remains under control over time.

## Key Indicators of Effective Biofilm Control

Successful biofilm management shows up as fewer low-flow zones and fewer chronic temperature drift areas where hot and cold water temperatures converge. Systems maintain stable disinfectant residual that reaches distal points when residual-based disinfection is used.

Regular cleaning of components that accumulate sediment and biofilm becomes routine rather than crisis-driven. The system shows reduced repeat positive Legionella results and requires fewer emergency shock treatments over time.

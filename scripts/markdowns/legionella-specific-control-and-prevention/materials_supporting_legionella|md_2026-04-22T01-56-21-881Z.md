# Which cooling tower materials increase Legionella risk?

## Executive Summary

Carbon steel, galvanized steel, and natural rubber components create the highest Legionella risk in cooling towers through corrosion, scaling, and biofilm formation that shields bacteria from disinfectants.

## Overview

A single cooling tower can harbor over 100 billion Legionella bacteria per liter of water when conditions align. The material choices you make for tower construction and components directly determine whether your system becomes a bacterial amplification site or remains controllable. Most facility managers focus on water treatment chemistry while overlooking how their infrastructure materials actively undermine disinfection efforts.

## High-Risk Materials in Open Cooling Systems

### Carbon Steel and Cast Iron Components

Carbon steel, mild steel, and cast iron create ideal conditions for Legionella proliferation when used as wetted surfaces in cooling towers. These materials corrode continuously in the oxidizing environment of cooling systems, generating iron-rich sediment that accumulates in tower basins and distribution systems.

The corrosion process roughens metal surfaces, creating microscopic anchor points where biofilm attaches and grows. Once established, biofilm matrices protect Legionella from chlorine, bromine, and other biocides by creating diffusion barriers that prevent disinfectants from reaching bacterial cells. The iron particles themselves provide nutrients that support the broader microbial ecosystem, including the amoebae that Legionella colonizes as intracellular parasites.

ASHRAE cooling tower guidance explicitly identifies corrosion and sediment control as critical elements of Legionella prevention programs, recognizing that even aggressive chemical treatment cannot overcome the protective environment created by corroded steel surfaces.

### Galvanized Steel Infrastructure

Galvanized steel appears frequently in cooling tower construction for basins, piping, and internal components due to its lower cost compared to corrosion-resistant alternatives. However, the zinc coating provides only temporary protection before the underlying steel begins corroding in the aggressive cooling tower environment.

Galvanized surfaces become particularly problematic as they age because they promote both corrosion and mineral scaling. The combination creates a complex surface topography with deep crevices where biofilm establishes protected colonies. Water treatment chemicals struggle to penetrate these deposits, allowing Legionella populations to multiply even in systems with apparently adequate biocide residuals.

### Natural Rubber and Problematic Polymers

Natural rubber components used in hoses, gaskets, and seals actively support Legionella amplification according to ASHRAE Guideline 12 material specifications. These organic materials leach nutrients into the cooling water while providing surfaces that biofilm colonizes more readily than inorganic alternatives.

Certain plastic and elastomer formulations used in drift eliminators, flexible connections, and sealing applications create similar problems. These polymers can release assimilable organic carbon that feeds the microbial community, while their surface chemistry promotes biofilm adhesion and growth.

## Lower-Risk Material Alternatives

### Stainless Steel Systems

Stainless steel resists the corrosion and surface roughening that makes other metals problematic for Legionella control. The smooth, stable surface provides fewer attachment sites for biofilm initiation and generates minimal sediment that could accumulate in system dead legs.

While stainless steel construction costs more initially, the material choice reduces long-term maintenance requirements and makes chemical treatment programs more effective by eliminating the constant influx of corrosion products that consume biocides and create protective deposits.

### Copper in Specific Applications

Copper exhibits some inhibitory effects against Legionella growth in certain plumbing contexts, though its effectiveness depends heavily on water chemistry and surface condition. The antimicrobial properties of copper ions can suppress bacterial populations, but this benefit diminishes as surfaces oxidize or when water chemistry prevents adequate copper dissolution.

Copper's performance as a Legionella control material remains context-dependent rather than universally protective, making it a supplementary consideration rather than a primary control strategy.

## Practical Implementation

Facility managers should prioritize replacing carbon steel and galvanized components in wetted areas during planned maintenance cycles, starting with tower basins where sediment accumulation creates the most problematic conditions. Specify stainless steel or other corrosion-resistant materials for new installations, particularly in areas with limited access for cleaning.

Eliminate natural rubber components from cooling water contact and specify synthetic alternatives that meet both mechanical requirements and biofilm resistance criteria. Document material specifications in your Legionella management plan to ensure replacement parts maintain the same risk profile during future maintenance.

Monitor corrosion rates monthly using corrosion coupons or electrochemical methods to quantify how quickly your current materials generate the sediment that supports bacterial growth. Systems with corrosion rates exceeding 2 mils per year typically require more aggressive cleaning frequencies and higher biocide dosages to maintain control.

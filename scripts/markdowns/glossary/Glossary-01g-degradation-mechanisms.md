# Degradation Mechanisms — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers the physical and chemical processes that damage equipment and reduce system performance over time. Defines scaling, fouling, corrosion, erosion, and deposition as distinct but related failure modes, and explains passivation as the protective counterprocess. Understanding how these mechanisms differ is essential for diagnosing system problems and selecting appropriate treatment responses.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between scaling, fouling, and corrosion?

**A:** Scaling is mineral precipitation onto surfaces; fouling is any unwanted accumulation including biological, particulate, or chemical deposits; corrosion is the chemical or electrochemical deterioration of the material itself.

---

**Q:** What is passivation?

**A:** The formation of a thin protective oxide or chemical film on a metal surface that reduces its corrosion rate.

---

**Q:** When is passivation important in a water system?

**A:** When new metal surfaces are first exposed to the system, or after cleaning or repairs have disturbed the protective surface film — passivation treatment ensures a protective layer forms before normal operating chemistry takes over.

---

**Q:** What is erosion in water systems?

**A:** Material loss from a surface caused by the mechanical abrasion of moving fluid or suspended particles — distinct from corrosion, which is chemical. High velocity at elbows, pump impellers, and orifices is a common cause.

---

**Q:** What is deposition in water systems?

**A:** The settling or attachment of suspended or precipitated solids onto a surface — the physical accumulation step that follows from scaling, fouling, or particulate transport in recirculating systems.

# Biocides, Biological Control, and Common Organisms — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers the chemical and biological vocabulary of microbiological control in water systems. Defines oxidizing and non-oxidizing biocide families by active ingredient, explains dosing strategies (continuous, shock, and slug feed), and describes the organisms most relevant to industrial water systems — bacteria, algae, fungi, protozoa, sulfate-reducing bacteria, iron bacteria, and slime formers. Explains how biofilm forms, why it is resistant to treatment, and how microbiologically influenced corrosion connects biological activity to metal deterioration.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is a biocide?

**A:** A chemical used to kill or control microorganisms in water systems.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between an oxidizing and a non-oxidizing biocide?

**A:** An oxidizing biocide kills organisms through bulk oxidation of cell components; a non-oxidizing biocide acts through specific toxic mechanisms such as disrupting cell membranes or metabolism without relying on strong oxidation.

---

**Q:** Why are both oxidizing and non-oxidizing biocides often used together?

**A:** Because organisms can develop resistance or tolerance to either type alone — alternating or combining them provides broader and more reliable biological control.

---

**Q:** What is a biodispersant?

**A:** A chemical that loosens and removes slime, biofilm, or debris from surfaces so it can be flushed away — often used alongside biocides to improve their penetration and effectiveness.

---

**Q:** What is a biofilm penetrant?

**A:** A treatment that helps active biocide chemicals reach organisms protected deep inside biofilm — biofilm can shield bacteria from concentrations of biocide that would kill them in open water.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between shock feeding and continuous feeding of a biocide?

**A:** Shock (or slug) feeding applies a short, high-dose treatment to quickly reduce biological activity; continuous feeding maintains a steady lower-level residual. Many programs use both — routine continuous dosing plus periodic shock doses.

---

**Q:** What is free chlorine?

**A:** The active chlorine residual present mainly as hypochlorous acid and hypochlorite ion — the primary disinfectant form of chlorine in treated water.

---

**Q:** What is hypochlorous acid, and why does it matter?

**A:** The most germicidally effective free-chlorine species — it penetrates bacterial cell walls much better than hypochlorite. Its proportion relative to hypochlorite increases at lower pH, which is why chlorine disinfection is more effective in slightly acidic water.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between sodium hypochlorite and calcium hypochlorite?

**A:** Sodium hypochlorite is a liquid (bleach) commonly used for water treatment dosing; calcium hypochlorite is a solid that releases chlorine when dissolved — both are oxidizing biocides.

---

**Q:** What is bromine, and when is it preferred over chlorine?

**A:** Bromine is an oxidizing biocide that remains effective at higher pH levels where chlorine loses much of its germicidal activity — commonly used in cooling towers, spas, and systems where pH tends to run high.

---

**Q:** What is chlorine dioxide?

**A:** A selective oxidizing biocide and disinfectant generated on site — effective against biofilm, Legionella, and other organisms at lower concentrations than chlorine, and remains active across a wide pH range.

---

**Q:** What is ozone as a biocide?

**A:** A very strong oxidant generated on site from oxygen or air — highly effective at disinfection and oxidation but decomposes quickly and leaves no residual.

---

**Q:** What is peracetic acid?

**A:** A strong oxidizing biocide formed from acetic acid and hydrogen peroxide — used for disinfection in food processing, cooling, and some water systems; leaves no persistent residues.

---

**Q:** What is monochloramine?

**A:** A combined chlorine disinfectant formed from chlorine and ammonia — weaker than free chlorine but more stable in distribution, penetrates biofilm better, and is less affected by pH changes.

---

**Q:** What is glutaraldehyde?

**A:** A non-oxidizing biocide commonly used in industrial cooling, oilfield, and paper-making water systems — effective against a wide range of bacteria including sulfate-reducing bacteria.

---

**Q:** What is an isothiazolin?

**A:** A family of non-oxidizing biocides widely used in water treatment and preservation — includes methylisothiazolinone (MIT) and methylchloroisothiazolinone (CMIT) as common active ingredients.

---

**Q:** What is DBNPA?

**A:** Dibromonitrilopropionamide — a fast-acting non-oxidizing biocide used in cooling water, paper, and oilfield applications; it is also known as 2,2-dibromo-3-nitrilopropionamide.

---

**Q:** What are quaternary ammonium compounds (quats)?

**A:** A family of cationic biocides and surfactants that disrupt microbial cell membranes — used in cooling water, HVAC systems, and surface disinfection; benzalkonium chloride is a common example.

---

**Q:** What is copper sulfate used for in water treatment?

**A:** Primarily as an algaecide in ponds, reservoirs, and some open water systems — copper ions are toxic to algae at relatively low concentrations.

---

**Q:** What is biofilm, and why is it a concern?

**A:** A structured community of microorganisms encased in a protective slime matrix attached to surfaces — it protects organisms from biocides, reduces heat transfer, promotes corrosion, and can harbor Legionella.

---

**Q:** What are sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB)?

**A:** Bacteria that consume sulfate under anaerobic conditions and produce hydrogen sulfide — responsible for severe localized corrosion, often under deposits or in dead legs.

---

**Q:** Why are sulfate-reducing bacteria particularly damaging?

**A:** Because they create a locally aggressive, acidic, high-sulfide environment under deposits that can rapidly pit and perforate metal, often with no visible external warning.

---

**Q:** What are iron bacteria?

**A:** Bacteria that oxidize dissolved iron, creating slime and ochre-colored deposits — they can clog distribution systems, foul filters, and promote under-deposit corrosion.

---

**Q:** What are slime-forming bacteria?

**A:** Bacteria that produce sticky extracellular material contributing to biofilm formation — they create the matrix that protects other organisms and fouls heat-transfer surfaces.

---

**Q:** What is an algaecide?

**A:** A chemical specifically formulated to control or kill algae — used in open cooling towers, ponds, and other lighted water systems where photosynthetic growth can occur.

---

**Q:** What are protozoa, and why are they relevant to water treatment?

**A:** Single-celled organisms that feed on bacteria — some protozoa such as amoebae can engulf and shelter Legionella bacteria inside themselves, protecting the pathogen from disinfectants.

---

**Q:** What is microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC)?

**A:** Corrosion caused or accelerated by microbial activity — SRB, iron bacteria, and acid-producing bacteria can all create local conditions that dramatically accelerate metal deterioration beneath biofilm.

---

**Q:** How is free chlorine residual maintained in a recirculating cooling system?

**A:** By continuous or intermittent dosing of a chlorine source such as sodium hypochlorite, with automated control based on ORP or direct chlorine measurement — pH must also be managed to keep the active hypochlorous acid fraction high.

---

**Q:** When is monochloramine preferred over free chlorine as a disinfectant?

**A:** When the system has long distribution runs, high organic loading, or pH conditions that reduce free chlorine effectiveness — monochloramine is more stable and penetrates biofilm more effectively, though it is slower-acting than free chlorine.

---

**Q:** What are the limitations of quaternary ammonium compounds in cooling systems?

**A:** Quats can adsorb onto surfaces and lose effectiveness in water with high suspended solids or organic loading — they can also cause foaming in open systems and may be less effective against established biofilm than other non-oxidizing biocides.

---

**Q:** When is algaecide treatment needed in a cooling tower?

**A:** When visible algae growth appears in lighted areas of the basin, fill, or distribution deck — or proactively in systems with significant light exposure. Algae growth supports biofilm and biological fouling that can harbor Legionella.

---

**Q:** How are iron bacteria controlled in water systems?

**A:** By maintaining oxidizing biocide residual, reducing iron concentration in the makeup water through filtration or softening, and periodic shock dosing with non-oxidizing biocides effective against iron bacteria — physical cleaning of deposits is also usually required.

---

**Q:** How is biofilm detected in a water system?

**A:** Through dip slides or surface swabs for microbial counts, ATP (adenosine triphosphate) testing for rapid biological activity measurement, visual inspection for slime or discoloration, and in some cases pipe section coupons or bore-scope inspection of system internals.

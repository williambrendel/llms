# Corrosion, Metallurgy, and Corrosion Inhibition — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers the mechanisms, forms, and chemical control of corrosion in water systems and associated metals. Defines the major corrosion types — general, pitting, crevice, galvanic, stress corrosion cracking, MIC, and under-deposit — and explains the electrochemical basis that distinguishes anodic from cathodic attack. Defines the inhibitor families used to protect ferrous metals (passivating inhibitors, oxygen scavengers, molybdate, nitrite) and copper alloys (azoles), and explains how cathodic protection and corrosion monitoring fit into a complete treatment program.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is corrosion rate?

**A:** The speed at which metal is being lost due to chemical or electrochemical attack — typically expressed in mils per year (mpy) or millimeters per year.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between general corrosion and pitting corrosion?

**A:** General corrosion produces relatively uniform metal loss across a surface and is more predictable; pitting corrosion is localized, forming small but deep holes that can perforate thin materials long before significant total metal loss occurs.

---

**Q:** What is crevice corrosion?

**A:** Localized corrosion occurring in shielded gaps or tight spaces — the crevice traps stagnant liquid where oxygen depletes and aggressive chemistry develops, accelerating local attack.

---

**Q:** What is galvanic corrosion?

**A:** Corrosion accelerated when two dissimilar metals are electrically connected in a conductive liquid — the less noble metal corrodes preferentially.

---

**Q:** When does galvanic corrosion become a concern?

**A:** Whenever dissimilar metals are in contact or connected through a conductive water path — especially when the area ratio is unfavorable, such as a large copper pipe connected to small steel fittings.

---

**Q:** What is microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC)?

**A:** Corrosion caused or accelerated by microbial activity — sulfate-reducing bacteria, iron bacteria, and other organisms create local conditions far more aggressive than the bulk water chemistry.

---

**Q:** What is under-deposit corrosion?

**A:** Corrosion that occurs beneath deposits on a metal surface — inside the deposit, oxygen is depleted, pH can drop, and chlorides can concentrate, creating a very aggressive local environment.

---

**Q:** What is stress corrosion cracking?

**A:** Cracking caused by the combined effect of tensile stress and a corrosive environment — can cause sudden failure at stress levels far below those that would cause mechanical failure alone.

---

**Q:** What is dezincification?

**A:** Selective loss of zinc from certain copper-zinc alloys (brass), leaving behind a weak, porous copper structure — common in hot or aggressive water and in certain brass alloys.

---

**Q:** What is graphitic corrosion?

**A:** Selective leaching of iron from gray cast iron in corrosive water, leaving behind a weak graphite skeleton that retains the original shape but has little structural strength.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between an anodic inhibitor and a cathodic inhibitor?

**A:** An anodic inhibitor mainly reduces metal dissolution at anodic sites where oxidation occurs; a cathodic inhibitor mainly slows the cathodic reaction where reduction occurs. Most practical programs use mixed inhibitors that address both.

---

**Q:** What is a passivating inhibitor?

**A:** A corrosion inhibitor that helps form a protective metal-oxide film on the surface, reducing corrosion rate — examples include chromate (now restricted), molybdate, and nitrite.

---

**Q:** What is a filming inhibitor?

**A:** An inhibitor that adsorbs onto the metal surface and forms a thin protective organic film that physically separates the metal from the corrosive water.

---

**Q:** What is an oxygen scavenger?

**A:** A chemical that reacts with and removes dissolved oxygen from water — important in boiler systems and closed loops because oxygen drives pitting corrosion.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between sodium sulfite and hydrazine as oxygen scavengers?

**A:** Sodium sulfite is a common, safe oxygen scavenger used in many boiler systems; hydrazine is a more powerful and volatile scavenger that is now restricted or avoided in most applications due to its carcinogenicity.

---

**Q:** What is molybdate as a corrosion inhibitor?

**A:** A corrosion inhibitor often used in closed cooling and heating loops — it forms a protective film on metal surfaces and is relatively non-toxic compared to chromate.

---

**Q:** What is a nitrite inhibitor?

**A:** A corrosion inhibitor commonly used in closed cooling and heating systems — forms a protective oxide layer on ferrous metals. Should be maintained within a concentration range; too low leaves the metal unprotected, too high can cause problems.

---

**Q:** What is an azole inhibitor?

**A:** A class of corrosion inhibitors specifically used to protect copper and copper alloys — tolyltriazole (TT) and benzotriazole (BZT) are the most common examples.

---

**Q:** Why are azoles specifically needed for copper protection?

**A:** Copper corrodes by different mechanisms than ferrous metals — azoles form a stable complex with copper ions at the surface, creating a film that dramatically reduces copper dissolution and release.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between tolyltriazole and benzotriazole?

**A:** Both are azole inhibitors that protect copper alloys — tolyltriazole is slightly more soluble and often preferred in cooling water programs; benzotriazole was historically more common. They are used in similar applications.

---

**Q:** What is an orthophosphate inhibitor?

**A:** A corrosion-control approach using phosphate ions to form a protective calcium phosphate or iron phosphate film on pipe surfaces — used in potable water distribution and some process systems.

---

**Q:** What is cathodic protection?

**A:** A method that reduces corrosion by making the protected metal surface act as a cathode in the electrochemical cell — achieved either by connecting a sacrificial anode or by applying a protective electric current.

---

**Q:** What is erosion corrosion?

**A:** Combined material loss caused by both corrosion and mechanical wear from high-velocity fluid or particles — common at bends, orifices, and pump impellers.

---

**Q:** What is zinc salt used for in cooling water treatment?

**A:** As a cathodic inhibitor that helps form a protective film on metal surfaces — typically used in combination with phosphonate or other anodic inhibitors for balanced corrosion protection.

---

**Q:** Why is corrosion monitoring important in recirculating water systems?

**A:** Because corrosion damage accumulates silently — monitoring with corrosion coupons, electrochemical probes, or metal ion analysis catches program failures before they cause leaks or equipment failures.

---

**Q:** How is crevice corrosion prevented?

**A:** By eliminating or sealing crevices through design — using welded rather than bolted connections where possible, ensuring proper drainage so crevices do not trap stagnant liquid, and maintaining treatment chemistry that minimizes aggressive ion concentration.

---

**Q:** When is stress corrosion cracking a concern?

**A:** When a susceptible metal such as austenitic stainless steel is under tensile stress and exposed to specific ions — particularly chlorides — even at moderate concentrations. It is a particular risk in heat exchanger components, welds, and cold-worked surfaces.

---

**Q:** How is dezincification prevented?

**A:** By selecting dezincification-resistant brass alloys (typically containing arsenic, antimony, or phosphorus as inhibitors), using inhibited brass fittings in hot or corrosive water applications, and maintaining treatment chemistry that does not promote selective leaching.

---

**Q:** When is an oxygen scavenger needed in a water system?

**A:** In boiler feedwater, closed heating and cooling loops, and any system where dissolved oxygen cannot be tolerated — oxygen scavengers are particularly important when mechanical deaeration alone cannot reduce oxygen to safe levels.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between sacrificial anode and impressed current cathodic protection?

**A:** A sacrificial anode is a more active metal — typically zinc, aluminum, or magnesium — attached to the protected structure and allowed to corrode preferentially; impressed current cathodic protection uses an external power supply to drive the same electrochemical effect without consuming a metal anode as quickly.

---

**Q:** Where are filming inhibitors typically used?

**A:** In oilfield water systems, produced water handling, and some cooling water programs — particularly where film-forming amines or other organics can coat metal surfaces effectively. They are less common in potable or high-purity water systems.

# Heat Exchangers and Heat-Transfer Equipment — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers the design, configuration, and maintenance vocabulary for the full range of heat exchanger types used in water, process, and HVAC systems. Defines exchanger geometries (shell-and-tube, plate-and-frame, brazed plate, air-cooled), flow arrangements (countercurrent, co-current, crossflow), and internal components (tube sheets, baffles, gaskets, plate packs). Also addresses the performance and failure concepts most relevant to water treatment — fouling factors, thermal resistance, biofouling, under-deposit corrosion, pressure drop — and the cleaning methods used to restore performance.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is a heat exchanger?

**A:** A device that transfers heat from one fluid to another without the two fluids directly mixing.

---

**Q:** What is a shell-and-tube heat exchanger?

**A:** A heat exchanger with one fluid flowing inside a bundle of tubes and the other fluid flowing around the outside of those tubes within a surrounding shell.

---

**Q:** What is a plate-and-frame heat exchanger?

**A:** A heat exchanger made of stacked corrugated metal plates with gaskets or welds between alternating channels — compact, easily cleaned, and thermally efficient.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between a brazed plate and a gasketed plate heat exchanger?

**A:** A brazed plate exchanger is permanently bonded with brazing material and cannot be disassembled for mechanical cleaning; a gasketed plate exchanger uses removable gaskets, allowing the plates to be separated and cleaned.

---

**Q:** What is an air-cooled heat exchanger?

**A:** A heat exchanger that rejects heat to ambient air rather than cooling water — eliminates the need for a water loop but requires more surface area and is limited by air temperature.

---

**Q:** What is a finned-tube heat exchanger?

**A:** A tube bundle with extended fins on the outside to increase air-side heat-transfer area — commonly used in air-cooled condensers and HVAC coils.

---

**Q:** What is a condenser?

**A:** A heat exchanger that removes heat from a vapor so it condenses to liquid — used in refrigeration, steam, and process systems.

---

**Q:** What is an evaporator?

**A:** A heat exchanger that supplies heat to a liquid refrigerant or process fluid, causing it to boil — the cold side of a refrigeration system where useful cooling occurs.

---

**Q:** What is an economizer in heat exchanger context?

**A:** A heat exchanger that recovers waste heat from one stream to pre-heat another, improving overall system efficiency.

---

**Q:** What is countercurrent flow?

**A:** A flow arrangement where the two fluids move in opposite directions through the exchanger — maximizes temperature difference along the entire length, providing the best heat transfer efficiency.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between countercurrent and co-current flow?

**A:** In countercurrent flow the fluids move in opposite directions, allowing the hot outlet to approach the cold inlet temperature closely; in co-current flow both fluids move in the same direction, limiting how closely outlet temperatures can approach each other.

---

**Q:** What is a tube sheet?

**A:** A plate that supports and seals the ends of the tubes in a shell-and-tube heat exchanger — it separates the tube-side fluid from the shell-side fluid.

---

**Q:** What is a baffle in a shell-and-tube exchanger?

**A:** An internal plate that directs shell-side flow across the tube bundle in a path that improves heat transfer and supports the tubes against vibration.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between the tube side and shell side of an exchanger?

**A:** The tube side is where fluid flows inside the tubes; the shell side is where fluid flows around the outside of the tubes within the shell. The more corrosive, fouling-prone, or high-pressure fluid is typically placed on the tube side.

---

**Q:** What is a fouling factor?

**A:** A design allowance added to account for the expected reduction in heat transfer as deposits accumulate on surfaces over time — a higher fouling factor means the exchanger is designed with extra capacity.

---

**Q:** What is the overall heat-transfer coefficient?

**A:** A combined measure of all resistances to heat transfer across the exchanger wall, including the fluid films on both sides and the wall material itself — lower means poorer heat transfer.

---

**Q:** What is approach temperature?

**A:** The temperature difference between the two fluid streams at a specified end of the exchanger — a small approach means the exchanger is working efficiently.

---

**Q:** What is a temperature cross?

**A:** A condition where the leaving temperature of the cold stream exceeds the leaving temperature of the hot stream — only achievable with countercurrent or multi-pass arrangements.

---

**Q:** What is a pinch point?

**A:** The location in the exchanger where the temperature difference between the hot and cold streams is smallest — it limits the maximum heat recovery possible.

---

**Q:** What is tube fouling?

**A:** Deposit buildup on the inside or outside of exchanger tubes — includes scale, biological slime, corrosion products, and particulate, all of which increase thermal resistance.

---

**Q:** What is biofouling in a heat exchanger?

**A:** Biological deposit buildup such as slime, algae, or biofilm on heat-transfer surfaces — even thin biofilm layers significantly reduce heat transfer efficiency.

---

**Q:** What is under-deposit corrosion?

**A:** Corrosion that occurs beneath deposits on a metal surface, where locally aggressive chemistry — high chloride, low pH, oxygen depletion — develops hidden from the bulk water.

---

**Q:** What is thermal shock in a heat exchanger?

**A:** A rapid temperature change that creates differential thermal stress in the materials — can crack welds, gaskets, or brittle components if temperature changes are too abrupt.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between a fixed tubesheet and a floating-head exchanger?

**A:** A fixed tubesheet exchanger has tube sheets rigidly connected to the shell, limiting its ability to handle differential thermal expansion; a floating-head exchanger allows one tube sheet to move, accommodating expansion and also enabling easier mechanical cleaning.

---

**Q:** What is a U-tube exchanger?

**A:** A shell-and-tube exchanger where each tube is bent into a U shape — both ends connect to the same tube sheet, allowing thermal expansion but making tube cleaning more difficult.

---

**Q:** What is a once-through heat exchanger?

**A:** An exchanger using cooling water that passes through once and is then discharged or sent elsewhere — simpler but uses more water than a recirculating system.

---

**Q:** What is pressure drop in a heat exchanger?

**A:** The loss of fluid pressure as it moves through the exchanger — excessive pressure drop indicates blockage or fouling and increases pumping energy cost.

---

**Q:** What is chemical cleaning of a heat exchanger?

**A:** Removal of deposits by circulating cleaning chemicals — solvents, acids, or alkaline solutions depending on the deposit type — through the exchanger without disassembly.

---

**Q:** What is mechanical cleaning of a heat exchanger?

**A:** Physical removal of deposits using brushes, rods, high-pressure water jetting, or pigging — typically requires at least partial disassembly.

---

**Q:** What is pigging in the context of heat exchangers?

**A:** Cleaning a tube or pipe by pushing or pulling a close-fitting cleaning body (a pig) through it, scraping deposits from the inner surface.

---

**Q:** What is a hydrotest?

**A:** A pressure test performed with liquid, usually water, to confirm that the exchanger can hold its design pressure without leaking.

---

**Q:** What is an expansion joint on a heat exchanger?

**A:** A flexible component in the shell or connected piping that accommodates thermal movement, preventing excessive stress on the exchanger body or connections.

---

**Q:** What is a kettle reboiler?

**A:** A reboiler with an oversized shell that acts as a boiling vessel — the tube bundle is submerged in the boiling liquid, and vapor disengages above.

---

**Q:** What is a thermosiphon reboiler?

**A:** A reboiler that circulates process fluid by natural density difference between the denser unboiled liquid and lighter boiling fluid — no pump required.

---

**Q:** What is a plate pack?

**A:** The stacked set of corrugated plates in a plate heat exchanger that forms the alternating hot and cold flow channels.

---

**Q:** What is a gasket in a plate heat exchanger?

**A:** A sealing material placed between adjacent plates to prevent leakage and direct fluids into their correct alternating channels.

---

**Q:** How is biofouling controlled in heat exchangers?

**A:** Through biocide treatment in the recirculating water, maintaining adequate disinfectant residual, side-stream filtration, and periodic mechanical or chemical cleaning — biofouling must be addressed in the water chemistry program, not just by cleaning after the fact.

---

**Q:** When is chemical cleaning preferred over mechanical cleaning for a heat exchanger?

**A:** When the deposits are scale or corrosion products that respond to acid or alkaline solvents, when the exchanger cannot be easily disassembled, or when tube geometry prevents physical access — mechanical cleaning is preferred when deposits are soft or biological and the exchanger can be opened.

---

**Q:** When is a hydrotest performed on a heat exchanger?

**A:** After manufacture, after repair or re-tubing, or when a leak is suspected — the test confirms the exchanger can safely hold its design pressure before returning to service.

---

**Q:** Why might a facility choose a once-through cooling system over a recirculating one?

**A:** When abundant low-cost water is available and discharge is permitted, or when the process requires very precise inlet temperature control — but once-through systems consume far more water and are increasingly restricted by discharge regulations.

---

**Q:** How is under-deposit corrosion detected?

**A:** Often only by opening and inspecting the exchanger — externally it may be invisible. Elevated metal ion levels in water samples, unexplained pressure drop increases, or routine inspection during cleaning can reveal it. Corrosion coupons placed in representative locations also help.

# Physical & Sensory Properties — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers the observable and measurable physical characteristics of water that are detectable by instrument or human senses. Defines turbidity, color, odor, and taste as water quality indicators, and conductivity and resistivity as practical proxies for dissolved ion concentration. These properties are often the first signals of a water quality problem and are used in both field monitoring and automated control.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is turbidity?

**A:** A measure of water cloudiness caused by suspended particles scattering light — a high turbidity indicates more particulate matter.

---

**Q:** What is specific conductivity?

**A:** A measure of water's ability to conduct electricity, used as a practical indicator of dissolved ion concentration.

---

**Q:** Why is conductivity commonly used to monitor recirculating systems?

**A:** Because it rises predictably as dissolved solids concentrate, making it a fast and inexpensive proxy for overall water quality and bleed control.

---

**Q:** What is resistivity, and when is it used?

**A:** Resistivity is the inverse of conductivity and is used to describe very high-purity water where dissolved ions are extremely low.

---

**Q:** What causes color in water?

**A:** Dissolved organics such as humic and fulvic acids from decaying vegetation, dissolved metals like iron and manganese, or very fine particles — color can signal organic loading, corrosion, or contamination.

---

**Q:** What causes odor in water?

**A:** Dissolved gases such as hydrogen sulfide, volatile organics, or metabolic byproducts of microbial activity — odor is a common indicator of biological activity or contamination even when other parameters appear normal.

---

**Q:** What causes taste problems in water?

**A:** Dissolved minerals, chlorine residuals, dissolved gases, or microbial metabolites — taste complaints are often the first signal users notice when water chemistry is off.

# Disinfection Methods — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers chemical and physical approaches to pathogen control and residual management. Defines ultraviolet disinfection, ozonation, chlorination, chloramination, breakpoint chlorination, and dechlorination — explaining not just what each method does but when it is preferred and why. Residual management is a recurring theme: some methods leave no residual (UV, ozone), while others maintain long-lasting protection through the distribution system.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is ultraviolet disinfection?

**A:** Disinfection using ultraviolet light at specific wavelengths to damage the DNA of microorganisms and prevent reproduction — effective without adding chemicals.

---

**Q:** What is ozonation?

**A:** Treatment using ozone as a very strong oxidant and disinfectant — ozone is generated on site and decomposes quickly, leaving no chemical residue.

---

**Q:** What is chlorination?

**A:** The addition of chlorine-based chemicals to water for oxidation or disinfection — one of the most widely used disinfection methods.

---

**Q:** What is breakpoint chlorination?

**A:** The addition of enough chlorine to fully satisfy demand and destroy ammonia before a free chlorine residual begins to appear in the water.

---

**Q:** When is breakpoint chlorination needed?

**A:** When ammonia or combined chlorine compounds are present and interfering with effective disinfection — adding chlorine past the breakpoint establishes a free residual with full germicidal activity.

---

**Q:** What is dechlorination?

**A:** The removal or neutralization of chlorine residual from water, typically before discharge or use in sensitive applications.

---

**Q:** What is chloramination, and when is it used?

**A:** Chloramination is the deliberate formation of chloramines by combining chlorine with ammonia — used in potable water distribution where a more stable, longer-lasting disinfectant residual is needed than free chlorine can maintain over long pipe runs.

---

**Q:** When is ultraviolet disinfection preferred over chemical disinfection?

**A:** When adding chemical residuals is undesirable — such as in high-purity water systems, aquaculture, or food production — or as a final barrier before consumption. UV inactivates organisms without altering water chemistry.

---

**Q:** When is ozonation used and why?

**A:** When powerful oxidation is needed to destroy organics, taste and odor compounds, or resistant organisms — ozone is used in drinking water treatment, wastewater reuse, and high-purity systems where its rapid decomposition is an advantage rather than a limitation.

# Hardness & Mineral Content — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers hardness classifications and the key measurements used to characterize dissolved and suspended mineral content in water. Defines hardness types (total, calcium, magnesium, temporary, permanent), softness, and the two primary bulk measurements — total dissolved solids and total suspended solids — that underpin water quality assessment and bleed control decisions.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is hardness in water?

**A:** A measure of dissolved multivalent minerals, mainly calcium and magnesium, that can form scale on surfaces and equipment.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between temporary and permanent hardness?

**A:** Temporary hardness is mainly associated with bicarbonate alkalinity and can be reduced by heating or lime softening; permanent hardness is associated with sulfate or chloride salts and is not removed by simple boiling.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between calcium hardness and magnesium hardness?

**A:** Calcium hardness is the hardness contribution from dissolved calcium salts; magnesium hardness is from dissolved magnesium salts. Both contribute to scale formation, but calcium is typically the larger fraction.

---

**Q:** What are total dissolved solids (TDS)?

**A:** The total amount of dissolved minerals and salts in water, typically measured in mg/L or ppm.

---

**Q:** What are total suspended solids (TSS)?

**A:** Particles in water large enough to be retained by a standard filter, measured as the mass of solids captured per volume of water.

---

**Q:** Why does TDS matter in recirculating water systems?

**A:** As TDS rises, water becomes more aggressive toward metals and more likely to form scale — high TDS also indicates dissolved solids are concentrating, which drives the decision to bleed.

---

**Q:** What is soft water?

**A:** Water with low concentrations of calcium and magnesium — either naturally occurring or produced by softening treatment. Soft water is less likely to form scale but may be more corrosive to some metals.

---

**Q:** What is hard water?

**A:** Water containing significant concentrations of dissolved calcium and magnesium that can precipitate and form scale on surfaces, pipes, and equipment.

---

**Q:** What is very hard water?

**A:** Water with high hardness levels and a strong tendency to form scale — typically above 180 mg/L as calcium carbonate. Requires aggressive scale inhibition or softening to protect equipment.

# Ammonia Condensers and Refrigeration Terms — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers the terminology of industrial ammonia refrigeration systems, with emphasis on the condenser side where water treatment intersects with refrigeration performance. Defines condenser types, refrigerant states and flow paths, key vessels (receivers, accumulators, oil separators), compressor types, and safety equipment. Explains how water-side fouling, oil carryover, and non-condensable gas accumulation affect condensing pressure and system efficiency — the primary connection points between refrigeration operation and water treatment programs.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is ammonia refrigeration?

**A:** A refrigeration system that uses ammonia as the refrigerant — widely used in industrial food processing, cold storage, and large HVAC applications due to its high efficiency.

---

**Q:** What is an ammonia condenser?

**A:** A heat exchanger that removes heat from hot ammonia vapor so it condenses back into a liquid, completing the refrigeration cycle.

---

**Q:** What is an evaporative ammonia condenser?

**A:** A condenser in which ammonia condenses inside a coil while water evaporates on the coil exterior and air flows past — combining evaporative cooling and refrigerant condensing in one unit.

---

**Q:** Why are evaporative ammonia condensers common in industrial refrigeration?

**A:** Because evaporative cooling allows condensing at much lower head pressures than air-cooled condensers, improving system efficiency significantly in warm climates.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between an evaporative condenser and a cooling tower used with a water-cooled condenser?

**A:** An evaporative condenser has refrigerant flowing directly inside the coils with water sprayed on the outside; a water-cooled condenser uses a separate water loop that is then cooled in a standalone cooling tower.

---

**Q:** What is condensing pressure?

**A:** The refrigerant pressure at which vapor condenses to liquid in the condenser — lower condensing pressure generally means better system efficiency.

---

**Q:** What is head pressure?

**A:** A common operating term for the condenser-side refrigerant pressure — high head pressure typically indicates a problem such as fouled condenser surfaces or non-condensable gas buildup.

---

**Q:** What are non-condensable gases, and why are they a problem?

**A:** Gases such as air that do not condense in the condenser — they accumulate and raise head pressure, reducing system efficiency and capacity.

---

**Q:** What is a purge unit?

**A:** Equipment that automatically removes non-condensable gases from the high side of a refrigeration system to restore proper condensing performance.

---

**Q:** What is subcooling?

**A:** Cooling of liquid refrigerant below its saturation temperature — it increases the refrigeration effect and reduces flash gas formation at the expansion valve.

---

**Q:** What is superheat?

**A:** Heating of refrigerant vapor above its saturation temperature — typically controlled at the evaporator outlet to ensure no liquid refrigerant enters the compressor.

---

**Q:** What is flash gas?

**A:** Vapor formed when liquid refrigerant pressure drops suddenly — it reduces the useful cooling capacity delivered by the liquid.

---

**Q:** What is an oil separator?

**A:** A device that removes lubricating oil from the compressed refrigerant stream before it enters the condenser, preventing oil fouling of heat-transfer surfaces.

---

**Q:** Why does oil fouling matter in an ammonia condenser?

**A:** Even thin oil films on condenser coil surfaces significantly reduce heat-transfer efficiency, raising condensing pressure and operating cost.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between a high-pressure receiver and a low-pressure receiver?

**A:** A high-pressure receiver stores condensed liquid refrigerant on the high-pressure liquid side; a low-pressure receiver collects liquid and vapor on the suction side in recirculated ammonia systems.

---

**Q:** What is an accumulator in a refrigeration system?

**A:** A vessel on the suction side that separates liquid refrigerant from vapor, preventing liquid slugs from reaching and damaging the compressor.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between a screw compressor and a reciprocating compressor?

**A:** A screw compressor uses intermeshing rotating screws to continuously compress refrigerant vapor and is common in large industrial systems; a reciprocating compressor uses pistons in cylinders and is more common in smaller or older systems.

---

**Q:** What is desuperheating?

**A:** The removal of heat from superheated refrigerant vapor until it reaches saturation temperature — the first stage of heat rejection in the condenser.

---

**Q:** What is a relief valve in refrigeration?

**A:** A safety valve that automatically opens if refrigerant pressure exceeds a safe limit, protecting equipment from overpressure failure.

---

**Q:** What is a king valve?

**A:** A service valve used to isolate equipment or a section of the refrigeration system — typically used to isolate the receiver from the liquid line.

---

**Q:** What is an ammonia leak detector?

**A:** An instrument that continuously senses ammonia gas concentration in the air and triggers alarms or emergency shutdowns if unsafe levels are detected.

---

**Q:** What is defrost in a refrigeration context?

**A:** A process used to melt accumulated ice from evaporator coils or surfaces — necessary because ice acts as insulation and reduces heat transfer efficiency.

---

**Q:** What is hot gas in a refrigeration system?

**A:** High-temperature, high-pressure refrigerant vapor discharged from the compressor on its way to the condenser — hot gas is also used in some defrost cycles to melt ice from evaporator coils from the inside.

---

**Q:** Why is flash gas a problem in refrigeration systems?

**A:** Because flash gas in the liquid line displaces liquid refrigerant, reducing the mass of refrigerant reaching the evaporator and lowering system cooling capacity — subcooling and proper piping design are used to minimize it.

---

**Q:** Where are ammonia leak detectors typically installed?

**A:** At low points in machinery rooms where heavier-than-air gas pockets could form, near compressors and vessels, and at room exits — codes typically require detectors at concentrations well below the immediately dangerous to life and health threshold.

---

**Q:** How is defrost performed in industrial ammonia refrigeration systems?

**A:** Most commonly using hot gas defrost, where high-pressure refrigerant vapor from the compressor discharge is diverted through the frost-covered coil to melt ice from the inside — water and electric defrost methods are also used in some configurations.

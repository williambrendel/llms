# Chemical Properties & pH — Q&A

**Summary**
Covers the acid-base chemistry parameters that govern treatment program performance. Defines pH, acidity, alkalinity and its three contributing forms (bicarbonate, carbonate, hydroxide), buffering capacity, oxidation-reduction potential, dissolved oxygen, and the two oxygen demand measures (BOD and COD). These parameters collectively determine how water behaves chemically and how treatment chemicals perform.

**Scope:** This glossary is organized for app seeding and user education. Definitions are intentionally short and practical. Legionella-prevention entries align with widely used building-water-management principles, and chemical entries use common active-ingredient names rather than brand names.

---

**Q:** What is pH?

**A:** A measure of how acidic or basic water is, on a scale where 7 is neutral, below 7 is acidic, and above 7 is basic.

---

**Q:** What is alkalinity in water treatment?

**A:** The capacity of water to neutralize acid, largely due to bicarbonate, carbonate, and hydroxide ions.

---

**Q:** What is buffering capacity?

**A:** The ability of water to resist a change in pH when acid or base is added — water with high alkalinity typically has more buffering capacity.

---

**Q:** Why does buffering capacity matter in treatment programs?

**A:** Because it determines how stable pH remains when acids, bases, or chemicals are added, which affects corrosion, scale, and disinfection performance.

---

**Q:** What is oxidation-reduction potential (ORP)?

**A:** A measure of the tendency of water to oxidize or reduce substances — commonly used to monitor disinfectant activity in treated water.

---

**Q:** What is dissolved oxygen?

**A:** The concentration of oxygen gas dissolved in water — important because oxygen drives many corrosion reactions and supports biological life.

---

**Q:** What is the difference between biological oxygen demand (BOD) and chemical oxygen demand (COD)?

**A:** BOD estimates oxygen consumed by microbes while degrading biodegradable matter; COD measures the oxygen equivalent needed to chemically oxidize all organic matter. COD is always equal to or greater than BOD.

---

**Q:** Why is pH one of the most important parameters to control in a water system?

**A:** Because pH affects nearly every other treatment outcome — corrosion rates, scale formation, disinfection effectiveness, and chemical stability all shift significantly with pH. Most programs target a specific pH window where all these are balanced.

---

**Q:** How is ORP used to monitor disinfection in water systems?

**A:** ORP rises as oxidizing disinfectants such as chlorine or bromine are added — a sustained ORP above a target threshold indicates that an effective disinfectant residual is present, without requiring direct chemical measurement.

---

**Q:** What is acidity in water?

**A:** The capacity of water to neutralize a base — measured by titration and reflecting dissolved carbon dioxide, mineral acids, or hydrolyzable metal ions. Distinct from pH, which measures the current hydrogen ion activity.

---

**Q:** What is bicarbonate alkalinity?

**A:** The most common form of alkalinity in natural waters — contributed by bicarbonate ions formed when carbon dioxide dissolves in water and reacts with carbonate minerals. It is the dominant buffering component in most source waters.

---

**Q:** What is carbonate alkalinity?

**A:** Alkalinity contributed by carbonate ions — present at higher pH levels (typically above 8.3) where bicarbonate converts to carbonate. It represents a smaller fraction of total alkalinity in most systems.

---

**Q:** What is hydroxide alkalinity?

**A:** Alkalinity contributed by hydroxide ions — present in strongly basic water above pH 10. Rarely significant in natural waters but can appear in boiler systems or caustic-treated streams.

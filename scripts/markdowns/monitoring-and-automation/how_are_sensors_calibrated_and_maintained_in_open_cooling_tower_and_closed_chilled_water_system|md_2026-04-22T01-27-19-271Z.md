# How often should cooling tower sensors be calibrated and maintained?

## Executive Summary

All critical probes in cooling towers and closed chilled-water systems must be cleaned and calibrated by the water treatment company on every service visit, with pH probes replaced every 6 months and conductivity probes replaced annually on a preventive maintenance schedule.

## Overview

A single drifting pH probe can cause thousands of dollars in chemical waste or corrosion damage before anyone notices the problem. Most facilities run their cooling tower controls to failure, but the hidden cost shows up as chronic overfeed, underfeed, and system upsets. The most reliable operations track deviation between handheld and controller readings within tight bands—typically ±1% for conductivity and ±0.1 pH units.

## Sensor Calibration and Maintenance Practices

### Core Principle

All critical probes used for control or compliance must meet three requirements. At minimum, this includes pH and conductivity sensors, plus any ORP/oxidant residual, temperature, flow, makeup meters, DP/pressure, and dissolved oxygen probes where used.

First, the water treatment company must clean and calibrate these probes on every service visit. Second, operators must verify each probe against an independent reference method, with results documented and trended. Third, this practice applies more critically to open cooling towers due to high fouling potential, but closed chilled-water loops require the same attention despite typically cleaner conditions.

### Standard Maintenance Procedures

**pH Probes (Online and Handheld)**

The water treatment company performs maintenance on every visit by inspecting sensor condition, including the glass bulb, reference junction, cable, connectors, and flow cell. Technicians clean the probe using scale and biofilm removal methods appropriate to the probe type and fouling conditions.

A two-point calibration using fresh, in-date buffers follows—typically pH 7 and pH 10 for tower ranges, though sites may specify different ranges. Post-calibration verification involves checking against a known buffer or grab test.

Rather than run-to-fail replacement, facilities should use a preventive maintenance schedule replacing online pH probes every 6 months. pH probes commonly drift or fail from reference junction fouling, dehydration, coating, and aging, especially in towers. Proactive replacement reduces control errors and nuisance upsets.

**Conductivity Probes (Online and Handheld)**

Maintenance on every water treatment visit includes inspecting and cleaning electrode surfaces and flow cells, since scale represents the most common issue in towers. Technicians calibrate and verify using a traceable conductivity standard near the operating range, then confirm temperature compensation functions properly and remains stable.

Preventive replacement works better than run-to-fail for conductivity probes, with annual replacement recommended. Conductivity probes prove more robust than pH sensors but still drift due to coating, erosion, or electronics issues. Annual replacement reduces hidden drift risk in blowdown and cycles control.

**Other Critical Probes and Meters**

ORP probes in towers serve as oxidant-control surrogates. Technicians clean and inspect for coating or plating, which represents a common failure mode. They verify response using a known ORP check solution or by comparison to a validated handheld meter, while confirming stable sample flow through the flow cell. Since ORP functions as a control surrogate, operators must verify oxidant control periodically with a direct residual test like DPD, even when ORP drives automation.

Online chlorine and bromine residual analyzers require cleaning of sensors and sample lines, plus verification of sample conditioning including flow and filtration. Technicians check reagents for colorimetric systems, along with tubing and pumps, then calibrate against grab-sample residual methods and document any bias.

Temperature, flow, pressure/DP, and makeup meters need signal plausibility verification through trend checks, plus inspection for fouling or plugging where applicable. Technicians confirm meter totalizers and alarm thresholds work correctly, performing periodic field verification per site standard operating procedures.

Dissolved oxygen sensors in closed loops require cleaning and calibration using air-saturation and zero references as applicable to the sensor type, with stability verification. Operators use DO trending to detect oxygen ingress events from leaks or makeup water.

### Handheld Probe Management

**Daily Testing Requirements**

Site teams must calibrate handheld probes prior to every use. pH meters require two-point calibration, or at minimum one-point calibration with verification, using fresh buffers. Conductivity meters need verification and calibration with a known standard.

Operators maintain a simple calibration log recording date and time, buffers or standards used, results, pass/fail status, and operator initials.

### Controlling-Probe Alignment and Deviation Tracking

**Two Measurement Chains**

Facilities typically operate two measurement systems: site team handheld measurements for daily operational checks, and water company or controller probes that actually control feed and blowdown systems.

**Required Tracking Practice**

Operations must track deviation between handheld readings and controller probe readings. Sites set acceptable deviation limits, with typical approaches using tight bands for conductivity (±1%) and reasonable bands for pH (±0.1 units, depending on stability).

When deviation exceeds limits, technicians clean both sensors, re-check sample points and sampling technique, then re-calibrate both devices. If drift persists, they replace the probe using the preventive maintenance schedule rather than waiting for failure. All corrective actions and outcomes require documentation.

The goal involves minimizing and stabilizing deviation, since the controller probe drives chemical feed and blowdown decisions. Untracked bias causes chronic overfeed or underfeed conditions and loss of control.

## System-Type Differences

**Open Cooling Towers**

These systems experience higher fouling frequency from scale, biofilm, and solids. More frequent cleaning and tighter verification becomes necessary. ORP and halogen analyzers show greater sensitivity to poor sample conditioning.

**Closed Chilled-Water Loops**

These systems typically show lower fouling rates, but probes still drift over time. Operations should focus on stable sample points, verification after makeup or refill events, and maintaining inhibitor and chemistry control.

## Key Takeaways

Implement preventive maintenance schedules replacing pH probes every 6 months and conductivity probes annually, rather than waiting for failure. Track deviation between handheld and controller readings within ±1% for conductivity and ±0.1 pH units. Calibrate handheld probes before every use and maintain detailed calibration logs to ensure reliable chemical feed and blowdown control.

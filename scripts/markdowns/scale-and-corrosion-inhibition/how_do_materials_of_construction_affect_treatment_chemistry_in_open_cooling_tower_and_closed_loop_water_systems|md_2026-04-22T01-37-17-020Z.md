# How do materials of construction affect treatment chemistry in cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Materials of construction fundamentally determine your treatment chemistry options because different metals and plastics create unique electrochemical environments, protective film requirements, and chemical compatibility constraints that force specific pH ranges, inhibitor choices, and oxidant strategies.

## Overview

A single galvanized fitting can eliminate your ability to run high-pH chemistry that works perfectly for carbon steel systems. Meanwhile, copper alloys demand azole inhibitors that stainless steel systems never need, and aluminum components can corrode rapidly under alkaline conditions that actually protect other metals. The most expensive cooling system failures often trace back to treatment programs that ignored one "minor" material in the metallurgy mix.

## Core Mechanisms Behind Material-Chemistry Interactions

### Galvanic Coupling

Dissimilar metals electrically connected in tower or loop water drive the less noble metal to corrode faster as an anode. This galvanic acceleration often forces stronger corrosion inhibition or completely different chemistry setpoints than single-metal systems would require.

### Protective Film Formation and Stability

Most successful treatment programs rely on stable surface films: carbonate/phosphate films on steel, zinc carbonate on galvanized surfaces, oxide films on stainless steel, or azole films on copper alloys. Film stability depends critically on pH, alkalinity, oxidant levels, chlorides, temperature, and flow velocity. When materials change, film chemistry requirements change with them.

### Metal Ion Release and Side Reactions

Corrosion releases iron, copper, zinc, and aluminum ions that consume inhibitors, catalyze oxidant decay, increase deposition and fouling, and create under-deposit corrosion cells with microbiological sheltering. Each corroding material adds its own interference pattern to your water chemistry.

### Chemical Compatibility Beyond Metals

Oxidizing biocides, extreme pH conditions, solvents, and some amines attack plastics, coatings, and elastomers including pump seals, gaskets, and membrane components. Non-metallic failures can shut down systems just as effectively as metal corrosion.

## Open Tower Systems

Oxygen continuously replenishes in open towers, making corrosion drivers stronger. Concentration through cycles of concentration raises chloride, sulfate, and total dissolved solids levels, increasing pitting risk especially for stainless steel and driving conductivity-based galvanic corrosion.

Biocide use becomes essential, with both oxidizing and non-oxidizing options creating oxidant compatibility constraints. The need for scaling and fouling control often competes directly with corrosion control requirements, particularly around pH and alkalinity management.

## Closed Loop Systems

Once properly de-aerated and chemically stabilized, closed loops maintain low oxygen levels that dramatically reduce corrosion potential if chemistry stays controlled. Treatment focuses primarily on corrosion inhibition and pH control, with biocide addition only when microbiological risk exists in glycol loops, warm loops, or contaminated systems.

Compatibility with glycols and established nitrite/azole inhibitor programs becomes the dominant chemistry consideration rather than oxidant management.

## Material-Specific Chemistry Requirements

### Carbon Steel

**Main risks:** General corrosion and under-deposit corrosion.

**Open tower implications:** Requires inhibitors such as blended phosphates, polyphosphates, and polymers, sometimes with zinc or molybdate additions. pH and alkalinity control must balance scaling prevention against corrosion protection.

**Closed loop implications:** Common programs include nitrite-based inhibitors combined with azoles for copper alloy protection, molybdate systems, or filming amines depending on system design.

**Key sensitivity:** Oxygen combined with low alkalinity or low pH dramatically accelerates steel corrosion rates.

### Copper and Copper Alloys

**Main risks:** Pitting, erosion corrosion, ammonia attack when present, and oxidant-related metal loss.

**Closed loop implications:** Systems typically require azole additions (tolyltriazole or benzotriazole) specifically for copper alloy protection.

**Open tower implications:** Oxidizing biocides like chlorine or bromine at high residuals or low pH accelerate copper corrosion. Azoles help but get consumed or oxidized and need constant replenishment.

**Common constraints:** Avoid sustained aggressive oxidant residuals in copper-heavy systems. Maintain pH levels that support copper stability while considering scale formation and other metallurgy requirements.

### Galvanized Steel

**Main risks:** Rapid zinc loss through "white rust" formation, underfilm corrosion, and accelerated attack under high alkalinity or strong oxidant conditions.

**Chemistry implications:** High pH or high alkalinity increases zinc dissolution rates. Aggressive oxidizers directly attack zinc coatings. Open-tower programs require careful tuning to protect zinc through controlled pH and specialized inhibitor selection.

Startup procedures become critical since zinc benefits from stable carbonate/hydroxide film formation. Overly aggressive initial chemistry can strip protective films permanently.

**Practical impact:** Galvanized components typically narrow allowable pH and oxidant operating windows significantly.

### Aluminum and Aluminum Alloys

**Main risks:** Pitting and rapid general corrosion outside relatively narrow pH ranges, with particular sensitivity to high pH conditions.

**Chemistry implications:** Aluminum generally demands tighter pH control than other common metals. High pH programs that work well for steel systems, especially in open cooling applications, can severely damage aluminum components.

Certain inhibitor chemistries and high chloride levels worsen pitting susceptibility.

**Practical impact:** Significant aluminum in wetted paths eliminates "run high pH for scale control" strategies entirely.

### Stainless Steel

**Main risks:** Pitting and crevice corrosion, particularly with chlorides, oxidizers, and deposit accumulation.

**Open tower implications:** High concentration cycles push chloride levels up and create pitting conditions, especially under deposits or in crevice areas. Strong oxidants worsen pitting risk in chloride-rich water. Material grade matters significantly - 316 stainless resists pitting better than 304 but remains vulnerable under severe conditions.

**Critical requirement:** When stainless steel protects critical equipment, chloride limits and deposit control through filtration, dispersants, and cleanliness become as important as inhibitor selection.

### Plastics, Elastomers, and Coatings

**Main risks:** Oxidant degradation, swelling, shrinkage, softening, and stress cracking depending on specific polymer chemistry.

**Treatment implications:** Oxidizing biocides and high-temperature oxidant exposure shorten seal and gasket service life. Some amines and solvents in specialty inhibitors affect certain elastomers. Coating and lining failures under aggressive oxidants or improper pH create underfilm corrosion and system debris.

**Compatibility requirement:** Treatment programs must verify compatibility with seals and linings, not just metallic components.

## Mixed-Metal System Challenges

When systems combine carbon steel, copper alloys, stainless steel, and galvanized components - common in data centers and complex facilities - chemistry gets constrained by the most sensitive material and galvanic coupling effects.

**Typical outcomes:**
- Copper or brass presence almost always requires azole copper inhibitors
- Aluminum or galvanized components may demand tighter pH control  
- Stainless steel pitting concerns may require chloride caps and lower concentration cycles
- Deposit control through filtration and dispersants becomes more valuable because deposits create crevices and differential aeration cells

## Material-Chemistry Constraint Reference

| **Material** | **Open Tower Constraints** | **Closed Loop Constraints** |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon steel | Needs corrosion inhibitor plus scale balance; oxygen drives corrosion | Maintain inhibitor residual; keep oxygen low; control pH |
| Copper alloys | Avoid aggressive oxidant residuals; use azole protection | Use azole; avoid chemistry that strips azole films |
| Galvanized | Avoid high pH/alkalinity and harsh oxidant exposure; careful startup | Less common; still pH sensitive when present |
| Aluminum | Avoid high pH strategies; monitor chlorides and inhibitors | Tight pH control; avoid pitting conditions |
| Stainless | Control chlorides/cycles; minimize deposits; oxidants plus chlorides increase pitting | Lower risk if clean and chloride controlled; deposits still critical |
| Elastomers/plastics | Oxidizer and temperature compatibility; lining integrity | Glycol/inhibitor compatibility; seal/gasket compatibility |

## Implementation Strategy

Create an accurate materials inventory by system including piping, heat exchangers, valves, pumps, tanks, and all wetted components. Identify the "limiting material" - often galvanized steel, aluminum, or critical stainless steel components in high-chloride makeup water areas.

Set operating windows around that limiting material for pH, maximum chloride levels, oxidant strategy, and inhibitor family selection. Address deposit formation through filtration, side-stream treatment, and dispersants because deposits eliminate the benefits of proper bulk water chemistry.

**Verification approach:** Monitor with corrosion measurement methods appropriate to each system such as corrosion coupons, electrical resistance probes, or linear polarization resistance where applicable, and document consistent adherence to established setpoints.

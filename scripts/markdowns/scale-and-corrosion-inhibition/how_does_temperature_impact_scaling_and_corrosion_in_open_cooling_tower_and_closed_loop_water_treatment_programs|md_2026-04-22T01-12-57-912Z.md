# How does temperature affect scaling and corrosion of open cooling tower and closed loop water systems?

## Executive Summary

Temperature increases scaling risk at heat-transfer surfaces and accelerates corrosion kinetics, but the net effects differ dramatically between open cooling towers (which concentrate minerals and continuously ingest oxygen) and closed loops (where corrosion by-products dominate over mineral scale).

## Overview

A chilled water loop at 45°F rarely forms mineral scale, while the same building's cooling tower at 95°F battles calcium carbonate deposits daily. Temperature drives this difference through two mechanisms: it pushes scale-forming minerals toward saturation while simultaneously accelerating the electrochemical reactions that cause corrosion. The dissolved oxygen content of water drops from 12.77 mg/L at 5°C to just 8.26 mg/L at 25°C, yet warmer systems typically corrode faster because temperature effects on reaction kinetics outweigh the reduced oxygen availability.

## Scaling Effects by System Type

### Open Cooling Towers

Temperature generally increases scaling risk at heat-transfer surfaces. As temperature rises, especially at the tube wall which runs hotter than bulk water, many scale-forming systems move closer to or beyond saturation. Precipitation becomes more likely in the heat transfer zone.

Calcium carbonate scaling responds strongly to temperature and carbonate equilibria. In practice, scale commonly forms on the hottest surfaces first — condenser tubes and plate exchangers. These deposits reduce heat transfer efficiency and create under-deposit conditions that accelerate corrosion.

Higher heat loads and hotter condenser approaches create more scale propensity at exchangers, even when bulk chemistry appears acceptable. Scaling indices and control limits must be evaluated at actual operating temperature, including estimated skin temperature, rather than relying on room-temperature lab numbers.

### Closed Loop Water Systems

Closed loops face lower scaling risk than towers because they do not concentrate minerals through evaporation. Mineral scale usually takes a back seat to corrosion and deposit by-products. However, high-temperature zones can still develop scale on heat-transfer surfaces.

Cold chilled water loops rarely form mineral scale unless the fill water is hard and significant makeup occurs or initial cleaning was inadequate. Hot water and heat recovery loops can scale where surface temperatures are elevated, so testing and control should focus on those zones specifically.

## Corrosion Mechanisms and Temperature

### Open Cooling Towers

Temperature can raise corrosion rates, but the net effect depends on oxygen levels, deposits, and microbiology. Two competing effects occur simultaneously: reaction kinetics accelerate with temperature (speeding up corrosion), while dissolved oxygen solubility drops with temperature (reducing available oxygen to drive oxygen-reduction corrosion).

In open towers, the practical outcome commonly involves more corrosion risk at higher temperature. Warmer water promotes faster biofilm growth, and biofilm deposits create differential aeration cells and under-deposit corrosion. Higher temperatures and loads often coincide with higher cycles of concentration and solids content, leading to more deposit formation and under-deposit corrosion.

### Closed Loop Systems

Temperature interacts with oxygen ingress and passivation in closed loops. Dissolved oxygen drives corrosion by entering through aerated makeup, pressurization imbalance, or permeation through non-barrier materials. Higher temperature generally increases corrosion kinetics, but when oxygen is controlled and the system is properly passivated, stable protective films become more achievable.

Cold chilled water loops operate with lower kinetics, but corrosion can still be significant if oxygen or makeup water is present. Deposits like magnetite and iron sludge become the dominant scaling-like problem. Warmer closed loops combine higher kinetics with oxygen ingress, resulting in faster corrosion and faster deposit formation.

## Temperature-Specific Control Strategies

### Open Cooling Tower Controls

Base scaling control on operating temperature and exchanger skin-temperature risk. Manage cycles of concentration, pH/alkalinity, and dispersant/polymer programs accordingly. The hottest surfaces require the most attention for scale prevention.

Manage deposits aggressively through filtration and cleanliness programs because deposits both insulate heat transfer surfaces and accelerate corrosion underneath. Higher biofouling pressure accompanies rising bulk temperature, so biocide demand and residual control must remain stable as conditions warm.

### Closed Loop Controls

Make oxygen control the top priority by minimizing makeup, maintaining proper pressurization, and eliminating air ingress points. Treat "scale" primarily as corrosion by-product deposition (iron and magnetite) rather than mineral scale, except in higher-temperature loops where mineral scale becomes plausible.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor scaling indices monthly at actual operating temperatures, not lab temperatures, and maintain calcium carbonate saturation index below +0.5 in cooling towers above 85°F. Control dissolved oxygen below 0.1 mg/L in closed loops, and consider upgrading to barrier-type expansion tanks in systems operating above 140°F to minimize oxygen ingress and temperature-accelerated corrosion.

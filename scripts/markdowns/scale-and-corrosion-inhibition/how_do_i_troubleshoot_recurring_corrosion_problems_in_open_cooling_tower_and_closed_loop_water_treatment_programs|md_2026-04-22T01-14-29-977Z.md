# How do I troubleshoot recurring corrosion problems in open cooling tower and closed loop water systems?

## Executive Summary

Recurring corrosion stems from five root causes: wrong environment conditions, oxygen control failure, deposit/biofilm formation, galvanic effects, or treatment control failure. Open towers concentrate salts while staying oxygenated, while closed loops should remain oxygen-limited but often don't.

## Overview

A single corrosion failure can cost tens of thousands in equipment replacement, but recurring corrosion problems signal deeper system issues that multiply those costs. The feedback loop works like this: deposits create under-deposit corrosion sites, which generate more iron, which forms more deposits. Meanwhile, what looks like a "closed" loop system often consumes hundreds of gallons of makeup water monthly, introducing fresh oxygen that drives rust formation throughout the piping network.

## Diagnosing Corrosion Mode

Accurate diagnosis requires multiple indicators working together. Coupons provide uniform loss rates in mils per year plus pitting depth measurements, but only when handled according to ASTM G1 standards for cleaning and evaluation procedures. Corrosion byproduct trends track total iron and soluble iron concentrations over time. Visual inspection reveals tubercles, under-deposit attack patterns, and pitting at crevices where stagnant conditions accelerate localized corrosion.

Coupon handling must follow standardized procedures because cleaning and evaluation methods directly affect the reliability of corrosion rate measurements. ASTM G1 provides the commonly referenced practice for preparing, cleaning, and evaluating corrosion test specimens for both mass loss and pitting analysis.

## Open Cooling Tower Troubleshooting

Treatment and control failures offer the highest diagnostic yield because they're common and fixable. Chemical pump problems, empty drums, wrong dilution ratios, and stuck check valves can eliminate corrosion protection overnight. Poor injection points mean chemicals never reach low-flow or dead zones where corrosion accelerates. Instrumentation drift occurs when pH, conductivity, or ORP probes accumulate scale, or sample lines become fouled, sending false signals to automated control systems.

Blowdown control failure allows salts to concentrate beyond design limits, accelerating corrosion and undermining protective films. The key corrosivity drivers that commonly drift in towers include chloride, sulfate, and conductivity levels that increase over time. High salinity accelerates general corrosion and increases pitting risk in stainless steel components. pH control affects both ends of the spectrum - low pH accelerates general corrosion while high pH increases deposition that leads to under-deposit corrosion.

Cycles of concentration should be verified using chloride ratios rather than conductivity alone, which can mislead operators about actual concentration levels.

## Deposit-Driven Corrosion

When iron levels rise alongside fouled heat exchangers and dirty basin conditions, treat the problem as deposit-driven corrosion first. The solution requires mechanical cleaning, filtration upgrades, and dispersant programs rather than simply adding more corrosion inhibitor to the system.

CDC tower guidance connects proper control to managing scale, corrosion, sediment, and cleaning as core preventive measures. The feedback loop accelerates problems: thicker deposits create more under-deposit corrosion sites, generating higher iron concentrations that form additional deposits. This cycle increases downtime and potentially creates higher health-risk management burdens under water management programs, since deposits and corrosion byproducts contribute to the risk environment that CDC regulations address.

## Material Compatibility Issues

Galvanic corrosion occurs when dissimilar metals like carbon steel, copper alloys, stainless steel, and galvanized components connect in the same system. Localized attack concentrates at joints, flanges, crevices, and areas under gaskets where oxygen concentration cells develop.

Stainless steel pitting becomes a significant risk when chloride levels rise and crevices or stagnant zones exist, conditions commonly found in tower basins and plate heat exchangers.

## Closed Loop Systems

NIH research shows closed-loop corrosion commonly occurs due to dissolved oxygen and low pH, with oxygen infiltrating through makeup water or system leaks and reacting with iron to form rust throughout the piping network.

The first diagnostic question asks whether the system operates as truly "closed." Measure makeup rates in gallons per day or as a percentage of total system volume per month. If makeup isn't near zero, recurring corrosion becomes inevitable. Common reasons include leaks in coils, valves, and pumps; open or atmospheric expansion tanks with continuous air exchange; auto-fill or pressure relief valve problems; and frequent venting or air removal without addressing root causes.

## Oxygen Control and Monitoring

Dissolved oxygen measurements should be taken at multiple points: near the expansion tank, at the far return, and at the lowest-pressure point in the system. Total iron trends and visual appearance help identify problems - black magnetite sludge indicates active corrosion processes. Strainer solids characterization determines whether magnetic particles indicate corrosion byproducts accumulating in the system.

Inhibitor control must be based on actual chemical concentrations rather than surrogate measurements. Conductivity readings cannot reliably control nitrite-based programs, which depend on maintaining proper residual concentrations to support and repair protective oxide films. When microbiological indicators like iron-reducing bacteria or sulfate-reducing bacteria appear, deposits and low-oxygen niches can shift corrosion toward microbiologically influenced corrosion even when bulk water chemistry appears acceptable.

NIH notes that nitrite can serve as a nutrient for denitrifying bacteria, creating potential biological complications in systems with existing biofilm problems.

## System Cleaning and Passivation

Poor startup cleaning and passivation procedures commonly leave mill scale, oils, and construction debris that promote rapid deposit formation. These conditions encourage early biofilm development and under-deposit attack that persists throughout system operation.

The corrective approach typically involves mechanical flushing and low-point blowdown combined with strainer cleaning. Side-stream filtration with magnetic separation helps when magnetite accumulation is significant. Chemical cleaning must match the specific deposit types present in the system. Re-passivation and inhibitor residual stabilization follow cleaning procedures, with verification through coupon monitoring, iron trend analysis, and deposit reduction measurements.

## Cost of Delayed Action

Closed loop systems that "slow roll" corrosion problems accumulate iron and magnetite deposits that create additional under-deposit corrosion sites, making future cleanup more difficult and expensive. The probability shifts from uniform corrosion loss toward localized pitting failures that cause leaks rather than gradual thinning. NIH research on oxygen-driven rust formation supports taking urgent action when oxygen ingress continues unchecked.

## Diagnostic Best Practices

Open cooling towers require weekly to monthly monitoring of pH, conductivity, alkalinity, calcium hardness, chloride for cycles validation, and total iron. Coupon racks should include carbon steel, copper, and stainless steel specimens as needed, with pitting checks following ASTM G1-consistent handling procedures. Basin cleanliness monitoring through turbidity or total suspended solids measurements tracks filtration performance. Operational parameters include approach temperature and heat exchanger pressure drop measurements.

Closed loops need monthly monitoring that increases to weekly during upset conditions. Key measurements include makeup volume, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, and direct measurement of inhibitor residuals like nitrite or molybdate. Total iron and strainer solids analysis track corrosion byproduct formation. Carbon steel coupons provide minimum corrosion rate monitoring for these systems.

## Key Takeaways

Address treatment control failures first since they offer the highest probability of quick resolution. For open towers, monitor chloride levels monthly and maintain cycles of concentration below 4:1 to prevent accelerated corrosion. In closed loops, measure makeup water consumption weekly - anything above 1% of system volume monthly indicates oxygen ingress that requires immediate attention. Install magnetic separators when iron levels exceed 5 ppm to prevent deposit accumulation that drives under-deposit corrosion throughout the system.

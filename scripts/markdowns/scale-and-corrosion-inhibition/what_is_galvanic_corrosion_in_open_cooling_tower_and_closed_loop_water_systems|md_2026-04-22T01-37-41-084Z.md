# How do materials of construction affect treatment chemistry in Open Tower and Closed Loop cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Dissimilar metals in cooling systems create galvanic corrosion that accelerates metal loss when electrically connected through conductive water. Open towers experience more severe galvanic effects due to higher conductivity and continuous oxygen replenishment, while closed loops face slower but persistent corrosion at mixed-metal interfaces unless chemistry and oxygen control are maintained.

## Overview

A small steel component connected to a large copper network can corrode ten times faster than it would alone. This happens because cooling water acts as a battery electrolyte, turning metal connections into miniature power cells that literally consume your equipment from the inside out.

## Understanding Galvanic Corrosion in Cooling Systems

Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals are electrically connected and contact an electrolyte like circulating water. The less noble metal becomes the anode and corrodes faster, while the more noble metal becomes the cathode and gains protection.

In cooling systems, the circulating water serves as the electrolyte. Open cooling towers present higher risk because oxygen continuously replenishes and contaminants concentrate, creating higher conductivity conditions. Closed chilled-water loops typically have lower oxygen after stabilization and lower conductivity when treated properly, but galvanic effects still occur at mixed-metal interfaces.

## System-Specific Manifestations

### Open Cooling Tower Systems

Higher conductivity and constant oxygen exposure make galvanic effects more likely and severe. Symptoms appear as localized attack near joints, unions, and fittings where metals meet. Pitting or rapid thinning develops on less noble components, leading to leaks at transitions, threaded connections, brazed joints, and around inserts.

### Closed Loop Systems

Galvanic effects proceed more slowly unless chemistry or oxygen control fails. Accelerated corrosion often follows water chemistry changes like makeup water switches, inhibitor loss, biocide upsets, or cleaning events.

## Common Problematic Metal Combinations

The most troublesome pairs in HVAC and cooling applications include:

- **Carbon steel or cast iron with copper/brass**
- **Aluminum (including microchannel coils) with copper/brass** 
- **Carbon steel with stainless steel**
- **Zinc (galvanized) with copper**

The larger the cathode area relative to the anode area, the more severe the corrosion becomes. A small steel component connected to a large copper/brass network faces particularly aggressive attack.

## Primary Contributing Factors

### High Conductivity Water

Higher conductivity creates stronger galvanic current. This occurs most commonly in open towers due to concentration effects and high-chloride makeup water.

### Oxygen Availability

Open towers maintain constant oxygen presence, with oxidizing biocides and aeration increasing corrosion driving forces. Closed loops experience problems from frequent makeup, air ingress, non-functioning air separators, or open expansion tanks that maintain oxygen levels.

### Inhibitor Problems

Underfeeding, poor distribution, insufficient film formation time, or incompatibility with system metallurgy all contribute to galvanic corrosion. In closed loops, low inhibitor residual after flushing or new fill commonly triggers problems.

### Stray Current Issues

DC stray currents from improper grounding, bonding, or nearby electrical sources can mimic or amplify galvanic attack, causing severe localized damage.

## Elimination Strategies

### Breaking the Electrical Path

The most effective elimination strategy involves breaking electrical continuity between dissimilar metals. Dielectric unions, flanges, isolation spool pieces, and insulating gaskets or sleeves work when installed correctly. Avoid creating small bypass paths, as improperly installed bonding jumpers can defeat isolation.

Non-conductive sections using properly rated polymer piping or hose at metal transitions provide another solution where design, pressure, temperature, and fire codes permit.

### Reducing Electrolyte Driving Force

Control conductivity and cycles through blowdown control and, when needed, partial softening, reverse osmosis, or side-stream desalting. Pay special attention to chlorides and sulfates, which increase both conductivity and pitting risk on some alloys.

Maintain stable pH and alkalinity within ranges compatible with metallurgy and inhibitor programs, with exact targets depending on specific programs and materials.

## Treatment Chemistry Solutions

### Open Tower Approach

Use anodic and cathodic film-formers, commonly phosphates/phosphonates with polymers. Sometimes zinc is used where regulations allow, plus azoles for copper alloys. Ensure the program addresses all metals present: carbon steel, copper alloys, stainless steel, and aluminum/microchannel components.

### Closed Loop Approach

Deploy nitrite/borate/azole, molybdate-based, or other closed-loop inhibitors appropriate for regional regulations, discharge constraints, and metallurgy. Maintain proper residuals and ensure initial passivation on new steel.

Inhibitors reduce galvanic current by forming protective films and shifting polarization, but they don't fully eliminate galvanic coupling the way electrical isolation can.

### Oxygen Control for Closed Loops

Control oxygen and air ingress by fixing chronic makeup issues from leaks, relief valves, and poor separator performance. Use effective air separation and maintain appropriate expansion tank strategy with bladder tanks versus open designs. For severe problems, consider deaeration or oxygen scavengers after addressing ingress sources.

## Material Strategy and Geometry

Avoid unfavorable area ratios where small anodes connect to large cathodes. Standardize metallurgy in new construction, avoiding aluminum mixed with copper without isolation. For coils, ensure compatibility with expected tower chemistry, as microchannel aluminum shows particular sensitivity.

Verify stray current and grounding issues if corrosion appears severe and localized in unusual patterns through electrical surveys, bonding/grounding checks, and DC source identification.

## Key Takeaways

For open towers, identify mixed-metal interfaces monthly, control conductivity through proper blowdown plus makeup treatment, run tower corrosion inhibitor programs compatible with all system metals while maintaining residuals, and add dielectric isolation at critical transition points. Monitor with corrosion coupons or linear polarization resistance plus regular water chemistry analysis.

For closed chilled loops, eliminate frequent makeup and air ingress immediately, restore closed-loop inhibitor to target levels with confirmed passivation, correct mixed-metal transitions with dielectric isolation where appropriate, and maintain side-stream filtration with low solids levels.

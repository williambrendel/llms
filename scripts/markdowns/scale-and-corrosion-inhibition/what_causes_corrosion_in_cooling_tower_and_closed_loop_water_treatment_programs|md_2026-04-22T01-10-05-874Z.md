# What causes corrosion in cooling tower and closed loop cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Cooling towers corrode primarily due to constant oxygen exposure and concentrated salts from evaporation, while closed loop systems corrode mainly from oxygen ingress through leaks and poor sealing. Both systems suffer when protective inhibitor chemistry fails or deposits create localized attack zones.

## Overview

A single pinhole leak in a closed loop system can dump thousands of gallons and cost tens of thousands in downtime — yet that same system could run decades without corrosion if properly sealed and inhibited. The difference between these outcomes often comes down to understanding that cooling towers and closed loops fail for fundamentally different reasons, despite using similar metals and water.

## Cooling Tower Corrosion Drivers

Cooling towers operate in an inherently aggressive environment where water concentrates and air contact never stops. The constant evaporation-concentration cycle creates multiple corrosion pathways.

**Oxygen from continuous air contact** drives the primary electrochemical reactions. Open towers stay saturated with dissolved oxygen, providing the cathodic reactant that sustains corrosion on carbon steel and most other metals. Unlike closed systems, you cannot eliminate this oxygen source.

**Concentrated dissolved salts** accumulate as water evaporates, leaving chlorides, sulfates, and total dissolved solids behind. This rising conductivity accelerates general corrosion rates across all metallurgy. Chlorides pose particular risks for localized attack — pitting and crevice corrosion on stainless steels, especially under deposits where conditions become more aggressive.

**pH instability** compounds these effects. Low pH increases acid-driven general corrosion, while rapid pH swings destabilize the protective films that inhibitors work to maintain. Tower chemistry shifts constantly as makeup water varies and blowdown removes treatment chemicals along with contaminants.

**Microbiologically influenced corrosion** develops when biofilms create differential aeration cells and concentrate corrosive species locally. Sulfate-reducing bacteria thrive in anaerobic zones under deposits, driving severe localized attack that can penetrate metal rapidly.

**Under-deposit corrosion** occurs when scale, silt, or iron oxides create oxygen concentration differences. The metal surface under deposits becomes anodic relative to clean surfaces and begins pitting. Poor filtration or makeup water with high solids content accelerates this mechanism.

**Inadequate corrosion inhibitors** leave systems vulnerable through underfeed, overfeed that destabilizes some treatment programs, poor control, or wrong inhibitor selection for the metallurgy present. Copper alloys require azole addition to most programs for adequate protection.

**Galvanic corrosion** accelerates when dissimilar metals couple electrically in the conductive tower water. The less noble metal — typically carbon steel when coupled with copper alloys — experiences accelerated attack.

**Velocity and erosion-corrosion** remove protective films through high flow rates, impingement, or entrained solids. Elbows, inlets, and high-turbulence zones show characteristic localized metal loss patterns.

**Chemical oxidants** from biocides can increase corrosion risk if not properly balanced. Chlorine and bromine treatments raise system oxidation-reduction potential, potentially overwhelming inhibitor protection for copper alloys and carbon steel under certain conditions.

## Closed Loop System Corrosion

Closed loops should resist corrosion because they avoid evaporation, concentration, and continuous air contact. When corrosion occurs, the loop has usually become "open" chemically through oxygen ingress or chemistry drift.

**Oxygen ingress** represents the most common root cause of closed loop corrosion. Leaks, frequent makeup addition, open expansion tanks, faulty air vents, vacuum conditions, maintenance activities, or low system pressure introduce dissolved oxygen. Once present, oxygen plus iron drives ongoing corrosion and iron oxide sludge formation.

**Improper pH control** allows general corrosion when pH drops below the inhibitor's effective range. Poor buffering leads to pH instability that disrupts protective film formation and maintenance.

**Inadequate inhibitor residuals** leave metal surfaces unprotected. Low nitrite, molybdate, or azole concentrations — depending on the program — increase corrosion risk. Mixed metallurgy systems require targeted protection, with azole addition essential for copper and brass components.

**High chlorides or sulfates** in fill water or makeup create long-term problems because closed loops cannot use blowdown to remove these species. Elevated chloride levels increase pitting risk and reduce the safety margin when oxygen enters the system.

**Microbiologically influenced corrosion** develops in stagnant sections, dead legs, low-flow areas, or where glycol degradation provides nutrients. Warm temperatures and persistent makeup water introduction compound microbial risks.

**Deposit-related corrosion** occurs when iron oxide sludge, scale, or construction debris create differential aeration cells and under-deposit attack sites. These deposits often result from previous corrosion episodes or inadequate system cleaning.

**Galvanic effects** between dissimilar metals become significant when conductive water and oxygen create the conditions for accelerated localized attack on less noble metals.

**Stray electrical current** from poor bonding, grounding issues, or DC current sources can cause rapid, severe localized corrosion that appears suddenly and progresses quickly.

**Temperature effects** increase reaction rates generally, accelerating both corrosion and inhibitor consumption while influencing oxygen solubility and microbial activity.

## System Differences

The fundamental difference between these systems drives different failure modes. Cooling towers must manage an inherently aggressive environment where constant oxygenation, salt concentration, and biological activity create multiple simultaneous threats. Treatment programs work to control rather than eliminate these drivers.

Closed loops can achieve very stable, low-corrosion conditions when properly sealed and inhibited. Failures typically trace to oxygen ingress and chemistry drift — problems that proper design and maintenance can prevent.

## Diagnostic Approach

Fast diagnosis requires measuring the parameters that separate these failure modes. For both system types, check pH, conductivity, chloride, and sulfate levels to establish baseline water chemistry. Measure dissolved iron and copper concentrations along with turbidity to quantify active corrosion and deposit formation.

Verify inhibitor residuals — nitrite, molybdate, and azole for closed loops, or tower inhibitor residuals for open systems. Test for microbial activity using ATP or culture methods when deposits or slime are visible.

Review corrosion coupon data and failure locations to identify patterns. Under-deposit attack, galvanic effects, and velocity-related damage show characteristic signatures that point toward specific root causes and solutions.

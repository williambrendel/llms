# How does oxygen affect corrosion rates in cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Oxygen drives corrosion by serving as the primary cathodic reactant that enables iron dissolution. In open towers, oxygen stays near saturation (8-9 mg/L) and creates continuous corrosion pressure, while closed loops can control oxygen below 0.5 mg/L to dramatically reduce corrosion rates.

## Overview

A single milligram of oxygen can dissolve roughly 7 milligrams of iron through electrochemical reactions. Open cooling towers fight this reality every day, while closed loops can eliminate the problem entirely—if they stay sealed. The difference between oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor systems often separates corrosion rates of 1 mpy from rates exceeding 10 mpy.

## The Basic Corrosion Mechanism

Oxygen controls corrosion through a two-part electrochemical reaction. At the anode, iron dissolves into solution: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻. At the cathode, oxygen consumes those electrons: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. The overall reaction produces iron oxides: 2Fe + O₂ + 2H₂O → 2Fe(OH)₂ → Fe₃O₄ / Fe₂O₃.

When oxygen supply gets limited, the cathodic reaction slows and corrosion drops. When oxygen gets continuously replenished, corrosion accelerates. This makes oxygen availability the master switch for most cooling system corrosion.

## Open Cooling Tower Systems

Open towers maintain dissolved oxygen near saturation through continuous aeration. Water gets sprayed, air gets forced through the tower, and surfaces get wetted and rewetted constantly. At 25°C, this keeps dissolved oxygen around 8-9 mg/L at atmospheric pressure.

**Sustained General Corrosion**

Continuous oxygen supply prevents corrosion products from forming stable protective films unless inhibitors are present. Untreated systems typically see 5-15 mpy carbon steel corrosion rates, sometimes higher. Proper treatment brings this down to 1-3 mpy target ranges.

**Differential Aeration and Pitting**

Oxygen concentration differences create corrosion cells where high oxygen areas become cathodes and low oxygen areas become anodes. Under scale or sludge, oxygen diffusion gets limited, creating anodic sites that drive pit formation. The covered area corrodes while the exposed area stays protected.

**Air-Water Interface Acceleration**

Splash zones and intermittently wetted steel corrode faster due to alternating wet-dry exposure, high oxygen availability, and salt concentration during drying. These areas show severe rusting and coating failure.

**Chloride Amplification**

Oxygen plus chlorides dramatically increases pitting probability in both stainless and carbon steel. Open towers concentrate chlorides through evaporation, amplifying oxygen-driven pitting risk.

Since oxygen stays near saturation in open towers, corrosion rate gets controlled by inhibitor film strength, pH, temperature, chloride concentration, and deposit formation rather than oxygen concentration itself. Open towers are oxygen-rich by design.

## Closed Loop Cooling Systems

Closed loops can control oxygen levels below 0.1-0.5 mg/L after initial startup purging. Low oxygen means low corrosion, but this requires proper maintenance.

**Startup Oxygen Spike**

During filling, water contains 6-10 mg/L dissolved oxygen. Initial corrosion spikes often occur in the first 24-72 hours if systems aren't passivated. Iron levels may rise temporarily, making passivation critical.

**Oxygen Ingress During Operation**

Closed loops shouldn't have continuous oxygen entry. When oxygen enters through leaks, makeup water, air venting, or expansion tank failure, corrosion accelerates sharply. If dissolved oxygen rises from 0.2 mg/L to 6 mg/L and stays replenished, corrosion rates may jump from under 1 mpy to 3-6+ mpy depending on pH and inhibitors.

Common oxygen entry mechanisms include frequent makeup water, leaking auto-fill valves, low system pressure, failed bladder expansion tanks, air separator malfunctions, and partial draining. Even small continuous oxygen entry produces significant magnetite sludge over months.

**Magnetite Formation and Stability**

In low-oxygen closed loops, protective magnetite (Fe₃O₄) forms and stabilizes on metal surfaces. If oxygen remains present, magnetite converts to less protective ferric oxides, creating sludge and ongoing corrosion.

## System Comparison

| Parameter | Open Tower | Closed Loop |
|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Dissolved Oxygen | 6-9 mg/L (continuous) | <0.5 mg/L after stabilization |
| Oxygen Control Possible? | No (inherent) | Yes (critical) |
| Corrosion Driver | Oxygen + deposits + chlorides | Oxygen ingress |
| Corrosion Sensitivity to DO Changes | Moderate (always high) | Very High |

## Temperature Effects

Higher temperatures increase corrosion kinetics while decreasing oxygen solubility. The reaction rate increase outweighs the solubility decrease, so hot condenser water can corrode aggressively despite lower oxygen solubility.

## Practical Diagnostic Indicators

In open towers, oxygen-driven corrosion shows up as increased corrosion in splash zones, pitting under deposits, and high iron levels despite otherwise good chemistry. In closed loops, watch for trending iron increases, black magnetite sludge, frequent air binding, and repeated needs for inhibitor additions.

## Quantitative Relationships

Corrosion rate roughly increases proportionally with oxygen availability when oxygen diffusion limits the cathodic reaction. In closed loops, doubling continuous oxygen supply may approximately double corrosion rates, all else being equal. In open towers, oxygen rarely limits corrosion since inhibitor film strength becomes the limiting factor instead.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor dissolved oxygen weekly in closed loops and maintain levels below 0.5 mg/L through proper system sealing and air elimination. For open towers, focus on maintaining inhibitor film integrity since oxygen control isn't possible. Target corrosion rates below 2 mpy through robust chemical treatment programs rather than oxygen management.

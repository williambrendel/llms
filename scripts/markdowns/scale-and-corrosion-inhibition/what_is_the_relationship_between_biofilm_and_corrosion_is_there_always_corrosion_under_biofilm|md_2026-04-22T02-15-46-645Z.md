# Does biofilm always cause corrosion in water systems?

## Executive Summary

Biofilm does not always cause corrosion. The relationship depends on the specific metal, water chemistry, flow conditions, and microbial community involved—biofilms can accelerate, reduce, or have minimal effect on corrosion rates.

## Overview

A single biofilm can simultaneously protect one section of pipe while accelerating corrosion just inches away. This happens because biofilms create distinct micro-environments at metal surfaces where oxygen levels, pH, and chemistry differ dramatically from the bulk water flowing through your system. Under some conditions, microorganisms actually form protective barriers that slow oxidation rates.

## How Biofilms Interact with Corrosion

Biofilms fundamentally alter the metal-water interface where corrosion reactions occur. They create localized conditions at the surface that can differ significantly from bulk water chemistry. These micro-environments drive differential aeration cells and concentration cells—electrochemical conditions that either accelerate or inhibit metal deterioration.

When biofilms accelerate corrosion, they typically do so through localized attack mechanisms. The biofilm acts like a deposit that hinders normal transport processes, creating chemically distinct zones at the deposit-metal interface. Microbial activity within these zones can alter electrochemical reactions, either inhibiting or accelerating the corrosion process depending on the specific organisms and conditions present.

However, biofilms can also protect metal surfaces. Under certain conditions, microorganisms form biofilms or biominerals that act as protective barriers, actually reducing oxidation rates. This protective effect explains why biofilm presence alone cannot predict corrosion activity.

## When Biofilm-Related Corrosion Occurs

Corrosion under biofilm becomes most likely when the biofilm combines with deposits or exists in crevice-like geometry. These under-deposit zones develop chemistry that promotes localized corrosion through strong concentration gradients. Large oxygen gradients, stagnant zones, or intermittent flow patterns increase the risk of localized attack.

The key distinction lies in recognizing biofilm as a risk indicator rather than proof of active corrosion. Confirmation requires actual corrosion evidence such as pitting, tubercles, thickness loss, or coupon data.

## Identifying Active Biofilm-Driven Corrosion

Localized corrosion patterns provide the clearest evidence of biofilm-driven corrosion. Pitting and crevice-like attack patterns beneath deposits frequently associate with microbiologically influenced corrosion in field systems.

Surface sampling proves more valuable than bulk water testing because attached organisms exert the greatest influence on localized corrosion. Liquid samples often fail to represent the actual surface community driving the corrosion process.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor biofilm as a corrosion risk factor, but verify with direct corrosion measurements before implementing treatment. Sample biofilms directly from metal surfaces monthly rather than relying on bulk water microbial counts, and look for localized corrosion patterns with pit depths exceeding 0.1 mm as evidence of active microbiologically influenced corrosion.

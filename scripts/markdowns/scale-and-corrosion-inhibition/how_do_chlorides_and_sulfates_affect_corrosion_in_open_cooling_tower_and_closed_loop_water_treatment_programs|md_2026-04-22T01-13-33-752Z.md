# How do chlorides and sulfates affect corrosion in open cooling tower and closed loop water systems?

## Executive Summary

Chlorides and sulfates accelerate corrosion by breaking down protective films on metals and increasing electrical conductivity, with cooling towers concentrating these salts through evaporation and closed loops showing elevated levels when leaks allow frequent makeup water entry.

## Overview

A cooling tower operating at 4 cycles of concentration can turn 50 mg/L chloride makeup water into 200 mg/L circulating water—enough to trigger pitting in stainless steel components during summer peak loads. Meanwhile, rising chloride levels in a supposedly "closed" loop system often signal the expensive reality that the system isn't actually closed at all, burning through inhibitor chemistry while deposits accumulate in low-flow zones.

## Corrosion Mechanisms

### Chloride Attack Patterns

Chlorides promote localized corrosion by breaking down passive films, particularly devastating to stainless steels. The ions concentrate in crevices and under deposits, creating the perfect conditions for pitting corrosion. On carbon steel, chlorides contribute to pitting under deposits while raising conductivity throughout the system.

Stainless steel components face the highest risk, with guidance documents establishing "safe" chloride levels around 200 mg/L in warm water—and lower thresholds at higher temperatures. The combination of temperature, chloride concentration, and stagnant conditions in crevices creates the triple threat that leads to pitting failures.

### Sulfate Corrosion Pathways

Sulfates raise system conductivity just like chlorides, increasing both general corrosion rates and galvanic corrosion in mixed-metal systems. They contribute to deposit formation, particularly calcium sulfate under certain conditions, which then sustains under-deposit corrosion by creating differential aeration cells.

Sulfates also provide the electron acceptor for sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) when anaerobic conditions exist within biofilms or deposits. This creates a microbial corrosion pathway that operates independently of the direct chemical effects. Water corrosivity indices commonly group chloride and sulfate together as key drivers, with metrics like the Larson Ratio indicating corrosivity potential for iron and steel components.

## Open Cooling Tower Systems

### Concentration Effects

Cooling towers concentrate dissolved salts through evaporation, turning modest makeup water chloride and sulfate levels into aggressive concentrations in the circulating water. Higher salt concentrations combined with elevated temperatures at heat exchanger surfaces and the presence of deposits or biofilm accelerate both pitting and under-deposit corrosion.

The visual evidence appears as pitting in stainless steel crevices—around gaskets, under deposits, and in weld heat-affected zones, especially during warm seasons or high-load periods. Carbon steel shows higher general corrosion rates and increased under-deposit attack where sludge and scale accumulate. Galvanized components face higher risk of zinc attack and white rust during startup or chemistry upsets, with salts increasing the aggressiveness of these conditions.

### Control Strategies

Chloride and sulfate control in towers relies primarily on blowdown management and makeup water source modifications. When these ions climb above acceptable levels, operators must increase blowdown rates, implement softening or reverse osmosis treatment, or blend with lower-salt water sources.

Deposit and biofilm control becomes non-negotiable in high-salt environments. Scale, corrosion, and sediment controls must work together with regular system cleaning, since deposits convert salt stress into localized under-deposit corrosion and create microbiological risk zones.

## Closed Loop Water Systems

### Elevated Risk Per Unit Concentration

Closed loops should maintain chemically stable, low-salt conditions after initial conditioning. High or rising chloride and sulfate levels typically indicate frequent makeup due to leaks, poor initial fill water quality, or contamination ingress.

This matters critically because closed loops depend on passivating inhibitor films for corrosion protection. Chlorides destabilize these passive films and increase pitting propensity in susceptible alloys, particularly stainless steel and carbon steel under deposits.

### Failure Patterns

Systems show increasing iron levels, magnetite formation, black deposits, and plugged strainers—typically from the combination of oxygen ingress, deposits, and elevated salts. Pitting failures concentrate in low-flow areas and dead legs where deposits accumulate.

Operators often encounter the frustrating scenario where nitrite inhibitor levels look acceptable, but corrosion persists. Salts combined with oxygen ingress can undermine inhibitor film performance even with adequate chemical residuals present.

## Testing and Monitoring

### Essential Parameters

Both tower and closed loop systems require direct chloride testing using ion chromatography or field titration methods, with sulfate testing via laboratory ion chromatography or reliable photometric methods. Conductivity provides useful trending data but cannot substitute for specific ion measurements.

Metal trend monitoring should track total iron in both system types, plus copper when present in wetted components. Corrosion coupons must include carbon steel at minimum, with stainless steel coupons when these alloys exist in wetted components. Regular deposit inspection of strainers, low points, and heat exchanger approach conditions provides early warning of salt-deposit interactions.

**What it measures:** The relationship between aggressive ions and corrosion potential in water systems.

Formula:
```
Larson Ratio = (Cl⁻ + SO₄²⁻) / HCO₃⁻ (all in mg/L as equivalent CaCO₃)
```

**Example:** Water with 200 mg/L chloride, 150 mg/L sulfate, and 100 mg/L bicarbonate alkalinity yields a Larson Ratio of 2.4, indicating high corrosivity potential requiring aggressive inhibitor programs or blowdown control.

### Tower-Specific Monitoring

Cooling towers require additional routine checks for residual biocide or ORP levels, suspended solids, and visual inspection protocols consistent with CDC emphasis on sediment and scale control through regular cleaning.

## Treatment and Control Actions

### Tower Management

Establish chloride and sulfate operating ceilings based on the most sensitive metallurgy in the system—often stainless steel or galvanized components. Use blowdown control or lower-salt makeup sources to maintain levels below these ceilings.

Prevent the synergistic effects of salts and deposits through side-stream filtration where possible, strict basin cleanliness protocols, and deposit control chemistry appropriate to the specific scaling regime.

### Closed Loop Recovery

Confirm the loop operates as truly closed by tracking makeup volume and investigating any sustained makeup requirements. Control inhibitor chemistry by actual concentration rather than conductivity readings, which can mislead in high-salt conditions.

When salts are already elevated and deposits exist throughout the system, plan a comprehensive cleaning and filtration program followed by re-passivation, then maintain with minimal-makeup operation.

## Key Takeaways

Test chloride and sulfate levels monthly in cooling towers and quarterly in closed loops, maintaining chloride below 200 mg/L in systems with stainless steel components. When levels exceed these thresholds, increase blowdown in towers or investigate leak sources in closed loops, and implement side-stream filtration to break the salt-deposit corrosion cycle before pitting failures require expensive component replacement.

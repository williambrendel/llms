# What is Polyacrylate and How Does It Work in Cooling Tower Water Treatment?

## Executive Summary

Polyacrylate is a synthetic polymer that prevents scale formation and keeps contaminants suspended in cooling tower water, making it essential for high-cycle operations and phosphate-free treatment programs.

## Overview

A single polymer can handle what once required multiple chemicals. Polyacrylate delivers both scale inhibition and dispersant action in cooling towers, often allowing systems to run at higher cycles of concentration than traditional treatments. This dual functionality becomes critical when towers operate on reclaimed water or under strict phosphorus discharge limits.

## Understanding Polyacrylate Chemistry

Polyacrylate starts as acrylic acid monomers that link together into long chains. Manufacturers control the molecular weight during polymerization, creating polymers ranging from a few thousand to over a million Daltons. Most cooling tower applications use sodium polyacrylate in liquid form for easy handling and dosing.

The polymer's carboxyl groups give it a negative charge in water, which drives its effectiveness as both a scale inhibitor and dispersant. Higher molecular weight versions excel at dispersing particles, while lower molecular weight polymers work better for threshold scale inhibition.

## Scale Inhibition Mechanisms

Polyacrylate attacks scale formation through two distinct pathways. Threshold inhibition keeps scale-forming ions like calcium and carbonate dissolved at concentrations that would normally cause precipitation. The polymer molecules interfere with the initial nucleation process, preventing crystals from forming in the first place.

When crystals do form, polyacrylate changes their structure through crystal distortion. The polymer incorporates into growing calcium carbonate, calcium sulfate, and barium sulfate crystals, creating irregular shapes that don't adhere well to heat transfer surfaces. These distorted crystals stay suspended and get removed through blowdown.

**What it measures:** The minimum polymer concentration needed to prevent scale formation at a given water chemistry

Formula:
```
Threshold Dosage (ppm) = K × [Ca²⁺] × [Alkalinity] / MW
```

**Example:** A tower with 400 ppm calcium hardness and 300 ppm alkalinity might need 8-12 ppm polyacrylate to prevent calcium carbonate scale, compared to 25-50 ppm for older phosphonate inhibitors.

## Dispersant Action

Polyacrylate excels at keeping unwanted particles suspended in cooling water. Silt, iron oxides, biofilm fragments, and corrosion products all carry surface charges that cause them to aggregate and settle. The polymer's charged groups surround these particles, creating electrostatic repulsion that keeps them dispersed.

This dispersant action proves especially valuable in preventing under-deposit corrosion. When particles settle on metal surfaces, they create oxygen concentration cells that accelerate localized corrosion. Polyacrylate keeps these particles moving through the system where they can be removed through filtration or blowdown.

## Chemical Compatibility and Stability

Polyacrylate works well with other cooling water treatment chemicals. It stabilizes zinc, phosphate, and phosphonate programs by preventing precipitation in hard water. The polymer remains stable across the pH range of 6-9 that most cooling towers operate within, and it resists thermal degradation at typical cooling water temperatures.

Unlike some organic inhibitors, polyacrylate doesn't interfere with oxidizing biocides like chlorine or bromine. It also shows good compatibility with non-oxidizing biocides, making it suitable for comprehensive treatment programs.

## Application Guidelines and Dosing

| Water Condition | Typical Dosage (ppm) | Primary Function |
|---|---|---|
| Low hardness, clean makeup | 5-8 | Scale inhibition |
| High hardness or alkalinity | 10-15 | Scale inhibition + dispersant |
| Reclaimed water | 15-20 | Heavy dispersant duty |
| High cycles (>6) | 12-18 | Scale inhibition at concentration |

Dosage depends on cycles of concentration, calcium and alkalinity levels, and contaminant load. Systems running on reclaimed water or with high particulate loading need higher doses for effective dispersion. High-cycle operations require more polymer to handle concentrated scale-forming ions.

## Monitoring and Control

Polyacrylate programs require different monitoring approaches than traditional inhibitors. Fluorescent tracers often accompany the polymer for easy measurement, since direct polymer analysis is complex. Scale coupon testing provides the best long-term verification of program effectiveness.

Particle counting and turbidity measurements help track dispersant performance. Increasing particle counts or turbidity may indicate insufficient polymer dosing or the need for improved filtration.

## Key Takeaways

Test polyacrylate dosing monthly using fluorescent tracer analysis, maintain 8-15 ppm for most applications, and consider upgrading from phosphonate programs when discharge phosphorus limits drop below 1 ppm or when scaling persists at cycles above 5.

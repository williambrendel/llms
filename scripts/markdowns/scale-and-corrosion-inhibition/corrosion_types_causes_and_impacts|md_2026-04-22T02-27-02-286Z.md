# What Types of Corrosion Affect Cooling Tower Systems and How Do They Impact Equipment?

## Executive Summary

Five primary corrosion mechanisms attack cooling tower systems: uniform corrosion causes predictable wall thinning, while pitting, crevice, galvanic, and microbiologically influenced corrosion create localized failures that can perforate equipment unexpectedly. These electrochemical processes, driven by dissolved oxygen, chlorides, pH fluctuations, and deposits, degrade pipes, heat exchangers, and basins through leaks, reduced heat transfer, and costly unplanned outages.

## Overview

A single pinhole leak can shut down an entire cooling system, even when the average corrosion rate appears manageable. Cooling towers create nearly perfect conditions for multiple corrosion mechanisms to operate simultaneously — constant aeration supplies oxygen, evaporation concentrates salts, and deposits create aggressive micro-environments where localized attack thrives.

## Core Mechanism: Electrochemical Corrosion

Corrosion in cooling tower systems operates as an electrochemical process where metal atoms dissolve at anodic sites while cathodic reactions complete the circuit. In aerated cooling water, dissolved oxygen typically drives the cathodic reaction, making oxygen concentration a primary factor in corrosion rates.

The process accelerates when water chemistry and operating conditions create favorable environments. Dissolved oxygen maintains the electrochemical driving force, while chlorides and other dissolved salts increase conductivity and promote localized attack. pH shifts affect surface film stability, and deposits create concentration cells that can drive aggressive under-deposit corrosion.

Flow conditions add another layer of complexity. High velocity and turbulence can cause erosion-corrosion, while stagnant zones allow deposits to accumulate and create crevice-like conditions.

## Five Critical Corrosion Types

### Uniform Corrosion

Uniform corrosion produces relatively even metal loss across surfaces, creating predictable wall thinning. While this mechanism allows for scheduled maintenance planning, it still leads to leaks and failures when thickness margins are consumed. The predictability makes it manageable but not harmless.

### Pitting Corrosion

Pitting creates highly localized penetration that can perforate metal even when overall corrosion rates appear low. Chlorides, oxidizing conditions, deposits, and stagnant zones typically drive pitting, with susceptibility varying by metallurgy. A cooling system can show acceptable general corrosion rates while developing through-wall pits that cause sudden failures.

### Crevice Corrosion

Crevice corrosion attacks shielded, stagnant micro-environments under gaskets, fasteners, and surface deposits. Cooling towers prove especially vulnerable because deposits naturally accumulate and create oxygen differential concentration cells. Under-deposit corrosion represents the most common field manifestation of crevice corrosion in these systems.

### Galvanic Corrosion

Galvanic corrosion occurs when dissimilar metals connect electrically in the same electrolyte, causing accelerated attack of the less noble metal. Mixed-metal piping, fittings, heat exchanger tube-to-tubesheet joints, and repairs using different alloys create common galvanic couples in cooling systems.

### Microbiologically Influenced Corrosion

Microbiologically influenced corrosion involves microorganisms and their biofilms in corrosion initiation or acceleration. Biofilms create localized chemistry changes and oxygen gradients that often contribute to aggressive under-deposit attack. The combination of biological activity and electrochemical corrosion can produce rapid localized failures.

## Equipment Impact Patterns

### Piping Systems

Carbon steel, copper alloys, and stainless steel piping experience different failure modes depending on the corrosion mechanism. Uniform thinning creates gradual wall loss leading to eventual leaks, while pitting and crevice corrosion cause sudden pinhole failures. Under-deposit attack concentrates at low points, dead legs, and beneath tubercles where deposits accumulate.

### Basin Structures

Metal basins suffer crevice and under-deposit corrosion in corners, seams, and areas where sludge or scale accumulates. Concrete basins face corrosion issues with embedded metals and metallic attachments that contact tower water. The electrochemical nature of corrosion affects any metallic components regardless of the surrounding structure material.

### Heat Exchangers

Heat exchanger performance degrades as deposits and corrosion products increase thermal resistance and reduce heat transfer capacity. Tube and plate perforations from pitting and crevice corrosion cause cross-contamination between process streams and force unplanned shutdowns. Erosion-corrosion attacks high-velocity regions, impingement points, and areas where surface roughening increases turbulence.

## Cost and Operational Consequences

Equipment degradation follows two primary pathways. Uniform corrosion creates predictable wall loss that reduces remaining equipment life on a known schedule. Localized corrosion mechanisms cause rapid failures even when average corrosion rates seem acceptable, making them particularly problematic for maintenance planning.

Direct costs include leak repair labor and materials, heat exchanger retubing or replacement, and basin repairs including coating and lining work. Indirect costs often exceed direct repair expenses through downtime, lost cooling capacity, and higher energy consumption when fouling and corrosion products reduce heat transfer efficiency.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor cooling water chemistry weekly for dissolved oxygen, chloride levels, pH stability, and deposit formation patterns. Maintain chloride concentrations below 500 ppm for carbon steel systems and below 1000 ppm for stainless steel to minimize pitting risk. Consider ultrasonic thickness monitoring every six months for uniform corrosion tracking, combined with visual inspection for localized attack signs like tubercles, staining, and deposit accumulation that indicate crevice corrosion conditions.

# What are azoles and how do they work in open tower and closed loop cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Azoles are organic compounds that prevent corrosion in cooling systems by forming protective films on copper and brass components, with benzotriazole and tolyltriazole being the most widely used at concentrations of 3-10 ppm.

## Overview

A single cooling system can contain thousands of dollars worth of copper heat exchangers that corrode rapidly without proper protection. Azoles solve this problem by targeting the specific vulnerability of yellow metals like copper and brass, which standard steel corrosion inhibitors cannot adequately protect. Mixed-metal systems create galvanic corrosion risks that azoles uniquely address.

## Types of Azoles in Cooling Systems

Three azole compounds dominate cooling water treatment, each with distinct characteristics and applications.

Benzotriazole (BZT) serves as the workhorse azole for most cooling applications. Water treatment professionals rely on BZT for its proven track record and broad compatibility with other treatment chemicals.

Tolyltriazole (TTA) offers enhanced performance in high-temperature applications. TTA forms more stable protective films than BZT at elevated temperatures, making it the preferred choice for hot water loops and steam condensate systems.

Mercaptobenzothiazole (MBT) appeared in older formulations but has fallen out of favor. Environmental regulations and toxicity concerns have pushed most facilities away from MBT-based programs.

## Why Cooling Systems Need Azoles

Closed-loop systems present unique challenges that azoles specifically address. These systems typically contain carbon steel piping, copper heat exchangers, brass fittings, and sometimes aluminum components all in the same water circuit.

Traditional corrosion inhibitors like nitrite, molybdate, or phosphate excel at protecting ferrous metals but leave copper alloys vulnerable. Azoles fill this protection gap by forming chelate complexes specifically with copper ions.

The galvanic corrosion risk intensifies the problem. When dissimilar metals contact the same water, electrical currents flow between them, accelerating corrosion of the less noble metal. Azoles interrupt this electrochemical process by creating uniform protective barriers.

## How Azoles Protect Metal Surfaces

Azoles work through a sophisticated chemical mechanism that creates lasting protection. The azole molecules bond directly with metal atoms at the surface, forming a thin but durable protective film.

This film acts as a barrier between the metal and corrosive elements in the water. Oxygen, chlorides, and other aggressive ions cannot reach the metal surface through the azole layer.

The protective mechanism extends beyond simple barrier formation. Azoles also chelate dissolved copper ions in the water, preventing them from participating in further corrosion reactions or depositing elsewhere in the system.

## Dosage Requirements and Application

Proper azole dosing requires precision to achieve protection without waste or interference with other chemicals.

| Azole Type | Target Concentration (mg/L or ppm) |
| --- | --- |
| Benzotriazole (BZT) | 5–10 ppm (as active ingredient) |
| Tolyltriazole (TTA) | 3–10 ppm (as active) |

Initial system startup demands higher concentrations to establish the protective film. New systems or recently cleaned systems require 25-50 ppm as active ingredient for the first few weeks of operation.

Once the protective film forms, maintenance dosing drops to the standard range. Regular testing confirms azole levels remain within the target band, as depletion occurs through oxidation, biodegradation, and system losses.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor azole levels weekly using colorimetric test kits or send samples for laboratory analysis. Maintain benzotriazole at 5-10 ppm or tolyltriazole at 3-10 ppm for ongoing protection. Consider switching from benzotriazole to tolyltriazole in systems operating above 140°F for improved thermal stability and longer-lasting protection.

# What are the main types of scale in open and closed cooling systems?

## Executive Summary

Seven major categories of inorganic scale form in water-based cooling systems: carbonate scales (primarily calcium carbonate), sulfate scales, phosphate scales, silica-based scales, metal hydroxide deposits, iron-based scales, and inhibitor-related precipitates.

## Overview

Calcium carbonate dominates cooling water scaling problems, but six other scale families create equally stubborn fouling issues that most operators never see coming. Iron-based deposits alone include at least seven distinct mineral forms, from black magnetite sludge in closed loops to rust-colored ferric hydroxide in open towers. The chemistry varies so widely across facilities that no single treatment approach works everywhere.

## Common Scale Categories

### Carbonate Scale

**Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃)** represents the most frequent scaling problem in cooling water systems. This scale forms when dissolved calcium and carbonate ions combine as water temperature rises and carbon dioxide escapes from solution.

### Sulfate Scales

**Calcium sulfate** appears in two main crystalline forms: gypsum and anhydrite. The gypsum form (CaSO₄·2H₂O) predominates in most cooling applications due to typical operating temperatures.

**Barium sulfate (BaSO₄)** occurs less frequently but creates extremely hard, difficult-to-remove deposits. This scale type typically originates from process water contamination rather than makeup water chemistry.

### Phosphate Scales

**Calcium phosphate** encompasses various Ca–PO₄ solid compounds that precipitate when phosphate-based inhibitors interact with high calcium concentrations.

**Zinc phosphate (Zn₃(PO₄)₂)** forms specifically in cooling systems using zinc and phosphate inhibitor programs. These treatment chemicals can create their own scaling problems under certain water chemistry conditions.

**Calcium polyphosphate and calcium phosphonate precipitates** result directly from inhibitor chemistry interactions, creating deposits that defeat the original treatment purpose.

### Silica and Silicate Scales

**Amorphous silica (SiO₂·nH₂O)** creates glassy, extremely adherent deposits that resist most cleaning methods. High silica concentrations in makeup water drive this scaling mechanism.

**Magnesium silicate** and **calcium silicate** scales form when dissolved silica reacts with hardness minerals. These mixed metal silicates often appear as hard, ceramic-like deposits.

### Metal Hydroxide and Oxide Deposits

**Iron oxides and hydroxides** create rust-type mineral deposits that function like traditional scale. These deposits reduce heat transfer and restrict water flow through the same mechanisms as carbonate scale.

**Zinc hydroxide (Zn(OH)₂)** precipitates in some inhibitor chemistries when pH rises above optimal ranges.

**Manganese oxides** appear less commonly but form recognized deposit types documented in cooling water literature.

### Iron-Based Scales

Iron chemistry produces the most diverse family of cooling system deposits. **Iron carbonates** include siderite (FeCO₃), which forms when ferrous iron combines with carbonate under reducing conditions.

**Iron oxides and oxyhydroxides** create multiple deposit types:
- **Magnetite (Fe₃O₄)** appears as black sludge in hydronic and closed-loop systems
- **Hematite (Fe₂O₃)** and related iron oxides form rust-type deposits
- **Iron oxyhydroxides (FeOOH family)** create ochre and brown deposits in aerated conditions
- **Ferric hydroxide (Fe(OH)₃)** precipitates when iron oxidizes to insoluble forms

**Iron phosphates**, including ferric phosphate compounds, develop in industrial cooling water systems where iron contamination meets phosphate treatment chemistry.

## Key Takeaway

Monitor water chemistry monthly for the three most problematic scale precursors: calcium levels above 200 ppm, silica above 150 ppm, and iron above 0.3 ppm. When any threshold is exceeded, switch from generic phosphate inhibitors to polymer-based scale control programs that handle multiple scale types simultaneously.

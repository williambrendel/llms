# What causes fouling in heat exchangers in cooling tower and closed loop systems?

## Executive Summary

Heat exchanger fouling stems from five primary mechanisms in cooling towers (mineral scale, biofilm, suspended solids, corrosion products, and process contamination) and five in closed loops (corrosion products, oxygen ingress, construction debris, biofilm, and scaling). Corrosion-product fouling dominates closed systems while mineral scale and biofilm lead in cooling towers.

## Overview

A single cooling tower heat exchanger can accumulate deposits at rates exceeding 0.1 inches per month when fouling mechanisms go unchecked. The root causes differ dramatically between open recirculating systems exposed to air and sealed closed loops, yet both systems share one critical vulnerability: once fouling begins, it accelerates itself by creating conditions that promote even more deposit formation.

## Cooling Tower Heat Exchanger Fouling

### Mineral Scale Precipitation

Evaporation concentrates calcium, alkalinity, silica, and sulfate in cooling tower water. When control limits get exceeded, these minerals precipitate directly onto tubes and plates as hard, crystalline deposits. The concentrated conditions near heat transfer surfaces make scaling particularly aggressive at these critical locations.

### Biofouling and Biofilm Formation

Warm, oxygenated water combined with nutrients and often sunlight creates ideal conditions for biofilm growth. This biological slime doesn't just coat surfaces—it traps silt and corrosion products, making deposits thicker and significantly harder to remove. The biofilm matrix acts like a net that captures other fouling materials.

### Suspended Solids and Sediment Deposition

Airborne dirt, makeup water solids, process contaminants, and drift-borne debris settle and deposit throughout the system. Low-velocity areas become particular collection points where these materials accumulate faster than water flow can sweep them away.

### Corrosion Products and Under-Deposit Acceleration

Rust and metal oxides form and deposit within the system, but they create a more serious problem: under-deposit conditions that accelerate localized corrosion. This generates even more solids in a self-reinforcing cycle that compounds fouling over time.

### Process Contamination

Oil, organics, leaks, or cross-contamination from sources like glycol or process chemicals create sticky films. These films bind solids together and amplify all other fouling mechanisms by providing adhesive surfaces that capture and hold deposits.

## Closed Loop Heat Exchanger Fouling

### Corrosion Product Fouling

Magnetite (black iron oxides), hematite (red-brown deposits), and copper oxides represent the most common fouling mechanism in closed systems. These corrosion products circulate throughout the loop and deposit heavily in coils, plate-and-frame exchangers, and low-flow areas where velocities drop.

### Oxygen Ingress and Ongoing Corrosion

Makeup water events, system leaks, negative pressure points, poor expansion tank function, and frequent venting or maintenance introduce oxygen into supposedly sealed systems. This oxygen drives continuous corrosion that generates the solids responsible for most closed-loop fouling.

### Construction and Maintenance Debris

New installations and retrofits introduce mill scale, solder, flux, pipe dope, gasket debris, and metal fines into the system. Without proper flushing and filtration, these materials rapidly foul heat exchangers during initial operation.

### Biofilm and Microbiologically Influenced Corrosion

Warm loops, glycol systems, stagnant legs, and contamination events can support microbial growth even in closed systems. The resulting biofilm traps solids and drives microbiologically influenced corrosion that creates additional fouling materials.

### Scaling in Closed Systems

Though less common than in cooling towers, scaling occurs from repeated makeup additions, improper chemical dosing, high-temperature operation in hot water loops, or poor initial fill water quality. The sealed environment typically prevents the evaporation that drives scaling in open systems.

## Visual Identification Guide

Hard, crystalline deposits indicate mineral scale formation. Slimy deposits point to biofilm and biofouling. Black sludge with magnetic particles reveals magnetite from closed-loop corrosion. Brown or red particulate suggests general iron oxide corrosion products. Sticky or oily films indicate hydrocarbon or process contamination.

## Key Takeaways

Monitor cooling tower cycles of concentration weekly and closed-loop corrosion rates monthly. Maintain cooling tower conductivity below 3,000 µS/cm and closed-loop iron levels under 5 ppm. Install proper filtration, maintain chemical treatment programs, and eliminate oxygen ingress points to prevent the fouling mechanisms that compound once established.
